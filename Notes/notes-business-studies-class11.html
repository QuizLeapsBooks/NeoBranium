Chapter 1: Business, Trade and Commerce

Business

Definition
Business refers to all human activities undertaken to earn profit through the production, purchase, and sale of goods or services to satisfy human wants.

Characteristics of Business

  • Economic activity (done for profit).
  • Production or procurement and sale/exchange of goods/services.
  • Regularity — occasional transactions are not business.
  • Element of risk and uncertainty.
  • Profit motive — though social objective is also important.

Trade

Trade is the buying and selling of goods. It is a branch of commerce. Types: Internal Trade (within a country) and External Trade / International Trade (across countries).

Commerce

Commerce includes all activities that help in buying and selling — trade + aids to trade (transport, banking, insurance, warehousing, advertising).

BusinessCommerceTrade
Broadest term — all economic activitiesExchange + aids to exchangeOnly buying and selling

Objectives of Business

  • Economic Objectives: Profit, survival, growth, efficiency.
  • Social Objectives: Providing quality goods, fair employment, community welfare.
  • Human Objectives: Employee welfare, job satisfaction.
  • National Objectives: National development, employment generation, self-sufficiency.
Key Points
  • Profession (doctors, lawyers) differs from business — no profit motive; governed by a code of conduct.
  • Employment — regular salary; no ownership; no risk-bearing.
  • Risk is an essential element of business — without risk, there is no chance of profit.

Chapter 2: Forms of Business Organisation

Sole Proprietorship

Definition
A business owned, managed, and controlled by a single individual who bears all risks and enjoys all profits.

Merits: Easy to start, quick decision-making, owner gets all profits, secrecy.
Demerits: Unlimited liability, limited capital, limited managerial ability, lack of continuity.

Partnership

Definition (Indian Partnership Act, 1932)
Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all.

Minimum 2, maximum 50 partners (after Companies Act 2013). Partnership Deed is the written agreement.

Types of Partners

  • Active Partner: Takes active part in management.
  • Sleeping/Dormant Partner: Contributes capital; does not participate in management.
  • Nominal Partner: Lends name and reputation; no capital, no profit share.
  • Minor Partner: Can be admitted only to benefits; not personally liable.
  • Limited Partner: Liability limited to capital contribution (under LLP).

Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)

A hybrid form combining advantages of partnership and limited company. Partners have limited liability. Governed by LLP Act, 2008.

Hindu Undivided Family (HUF)

A unique Indian form of business — all male members of a Hindu family born into it are co-owners (coparceners). Managed by the Karta (senior most male member). Karta has unlimited liability; others have limited liability.

Cooperative Society

A voluntary association of persons with a common economic interest — operates on the principle of service not profit. Democratic management (one member, one vote). Examples: Consumer cooperatives, Producer cooperatives, Credit cooperatives.

Chapter 3: Private, Public and Global Enterprises

Private Sector Enterprises

Owned and managed by private individuals/companies with profit motive. Examples: Tata, Reliance, Infosys.

Public Sector Enterprises

Owned and managed by the government (Central or State). Purpose: Public welfare, not just profit. Examples: ONGC, BHEL, Indian Railways.

Forms of Public Sector Enterprises

FormFeaturesExample
Departmental UndertakingPart of govt. ministry; no separate legal entity; funded from budgetPost & Telegraph, Railways
Statutory CorporationCreated by an Act of Parliament; separate legal entity; govt.-ownedLIC, FCI, Air India
Government Company51%+ shares owned by govt.; registered under Companies ActSAIL, BHEL, ONGC

Disinvestment

The government sells a part of its stake in public sector companies to private investors to raise funds and improve efficiency. Examples: BPCL, Air India disinvestment.

Global Enterprises (Multinational Corporations — MNCs)

Definition
Companies that operate in multiple countries with a central headquarters in one country. They have enormous resources, advanced technology, and worldwide presence.

Examples: Apple, Google, Samsung, Unilever (HUL in India), Amazon.

Joint Ventures

A business arrangement where two or more parties agree to collaborate on a specific project, sharing resources, profits, and risks.

Key Points
  • PSEs (Public Sector Enterprises) are created to prevent monopoly, provide essential services, and promote balanced regional development.
  • MNCs bring capital, technology, and global markets — but also raise concerns about cultural influence and profit repatriation.
  • Public-Private Partnership (PPP): Model where private firms join govt. for public infrastructure projects.

Chapter 4: Business Services

Services vs. Goods

FeatureGoodsServices
TangibilityTangibleIntangible
StorageCan be storedCannot be stored (perishable)
Ownership transferTransferredNot transferred
SeparabilitySeparable from producerInseparable from provider

Banking Services

  • Accepting Deposits: Savings A/c, Current A/c, Fixed Deposit, Recurring Deposit.
  • Granting Loans: Overdraft, Cash Credit, Term Loans, Discounting Bills.
  • Agency Services: Collecting cheques, paying insurance premiums, acting as executor.
  • E-Banking: Internet banking, ATM, Mobile banking, NEFT, RTGS, IMPS.

Insurance Services

Principle of Insurance
Insurance is a contract where the insurer undertakes to compensate the insured for a specified loss in exchange for a premium.

Principles: Utmost Good Faith, Insurable Interest, Indemnity, Subrogation, Contribution, Proximate Cause.

Types of Insurance

  • Life Insurance: Covers human life risk. LIC is the major public insurer.
  • Fire Insurance: Covers loss due to fire (indemnity — not more than actual loss).
  • Marine Insurance: Covers sea cargo and ships.

Postal and Telecom Services

India Post provides postal services, savings bank, money orders, postal life insurance. Telecom services include BSNL, private operators for voice/data communication.

Warehousing Services

Warehouses store goods and create time utility. Types: Private, Public, Bonded (for imported goods), Cold Storage.

Key Points
  • NEFT: Batch processing; RTGS: Real-time, minimum ₹2 lakh; IMPS: 24×7 instant.
  • Principle of Indemnity: Insured gets only actual loss — cannot profit from insurance.
  • Principle of Subrogation: After paying claim, insurer steps into shoes of insured to recover from third party.

Chapter 5: Emerging Modes of Business

E-Business

Definition
E-business (Electronic Business) refers to conducting business activities — production, buying, selling, services — using the internet and digital technology.

E-Commerce vs. E-Business

E-CommerceE-Business
Buying and selling online onlyAll business activities online (broader)
Subset of E-businessIncludes E-commerce + supply chain + customer service

Types of E-Commerce

  • B2B (Business to Business): Transactions between businesses. E.g., Amazon Business, Alibaba.
  • B2C (Business to Consumer): Business sells directly to consumers. E.g., Amazon, Flipkart.
  • C2C (Consumer to Consumer): Consumer sells to consumer. E.g., OLX, eBay.
  • B2G (Business to Government): Businesses supply to government through e-procurement.

Benefits of E-Business

  • 24×7 availability — no time constraint.
  • Global reach — access to worldwide markets.
  • Lower costs — no physical store needed.
  • Faster and convenient for customers.
  • Easy comparison of products and prices.

Limitations of E-Business

  • Lack of personal touch — cannot feel/try the product.
  • Security concerns — cyber fraud, data theft.
  • Technological barriers — internet access, digital literacy.
  • Delivery delays; goods may differ from displayed images.

Outsourcing

Outsourcing means delegating non-core business activities to an external specialist firm. Examples: BPO (Business Process Outsourcing), KPO (Knowledge Process Outsourcing), LPO (Legal Process Outsourcing).

Key Points
  • India is a global hub for BPO and IT outsourcing (Infosys, TCS, Wipro).
  • Payment Gateway is software that authorises online payments (e.g., Razorpay, PayU).
  • Digital Wallet: Paytm, Google Pay — store money digitally for quick transactions.

Chapter 6: Social Responsibilities of Business and Business Ethics

Social Responsibility of Business

Definition
Social responsibility refers to the obligation of business to take decisions and actions that contribute to the welfare of society, beyond just making profit.

Arguments FOR Social Responsibility

  • Business uses society's resources → owes something back.
  • Long-run business interest is served by a healthy society.
  • Avoids government regulation if businesses self-regulate.
  • Improves public image and goodwill.

Arguments AGAINST Social Responsibility

  • Primary objective is profit — deviation reduces efficiency.
  • Adds to cost → higher prices for consumers.
  • Businesses lack the expertise for social work.

Responsibilities Towards Different Groups

StakeholderResponsibility
Shareholders / OwnersFair return on investment; transparency
EmployeesFair wages, safe conditions, training, job security
ConsumersQuality goods, fair prices, after-sales service
GovernmentPay taxes, obey laws, no corruption
Society / CommunityProtect environment, support local development

Business Ethics

Business ethics are the moral principles and standards that guide behaviour in business. They include honesty, fairness, transparency, and avoiding harm to any stakeholder.

Elements of Business Ethics

  • Top management commitment (tone at the top).
  • Code of ethics published and followed.
  • Compliance with law.
  • Whistleblower protection.
Key Points
  • CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility): Companies spending part of profit on social causes. Under Companies Act 2013 — companies with net profit ≥ ₹5 crore must spend 2% of average net profit on CSR.
  • Environment protection is a key responsibility — reducing pollution, sustainable resource use.

Chapter 7: Formation of a Company

Company — Meaning

Definition
A company is an artificial person created by law, with a separate legal entity, limited liability, and perpetual succession, formed to carry on business activities.

Stages in Formation of a Company

  • 1. Promotion Stage: Identifying business opportunity, conducting feasibility study, assembling resources. Person who does this = Promoter.
  • 2. Incorporation Stage: Filing application with Registrar of Companies (ROC) with Memorandum of Association (MOA), Articles of Association (AOA), and consent of directors. ROC issues Certificate of Incorporation.
  • 3. Capital Subscription Stage (for Public Co.): Filing Prospectus, collecting applications from public, allotment of shares.
  • 4. Commencement of Business (for Public Co.): ROC issues Certificate of Commencement of Business.

Important Documents

DocumentContents
Memorandum of Association (MOA)Name clause, Registered office clause, Objects clause, Liability clause, Capital clause — the constitution of the company
Articles of Association (AOA)Internal rules governing day-to-day management
ProspectusPublic advertisement inviting applications for shares — contains financial info

Key Points
  • A Private Company can start business immediately after getting Certificate of Incorporation.
  • A Public Company needs both Certificate of Incorporation AND Certificate of Commencement of Business.
  • Promoters are not employees — they work to bring the company into existence.

Chapter 8: Sources of Business Finance

Need for Business Finance

Every business needs funds for: fixed assets (land, machinery), working capital (day-to-day operations), and expansion/growth. Sources depend on the purpose and duration of the need.

Classification of Sources

BasisTypes
PeriodShort-term (<1 yr), Medium-term (1–5 yr), Long-term (>5 yr)
OwnershipOwned capital (shares, retained earnings), Borrowed capital (debentures, loans)
SourceInternal (retained profits, depreciation), External (banks, public)

Key Sources

  • Shares: Equity shares (voting rights; variable dividend) and Preference shares (fixed dividend; priority on winding up).
  • Debentures: Long-term debt; fixed interest; debenture holders are creditors not owners.
  • Retained Earnings (Ploughing Back of Profits): Profits kept back for reinvestment — cheapest source, no dilution of control.
  • Commercial Banks: Short to medium loans, cash credit, overdraft.
  • Trade Credit: Credit given by suppliers — cost-free short-term source.
  • Public Deposits: Fixed deposits from public; medium-term (1–3 years).
  • Loans from Financial Institutions: SIDBI, IDBI — for industrial projects.

New Sources

  • Venture Capital: High-risk funding for new/startup businesses with high growth potential.
  • Angel Investors: High net worth individuals who invest in early-stage startups.
  • Crowdfunding: Raising small amounts from a large number of people via internet platforms.
  • IPO (Initial Public Offer): A private company offers shares to the public for the first time.
Key Points
  • Equity shares: no guaranteed return, but owners have voting rights and residual claim.
  • Debentures: guaranteed interest; no voting rights.
  • Short-term needs: trade credit, bank overdraft, commercial paper.
  • Long-term needs: shares, debentures, long-term institutional loans.

Chapter 9: Small Business

Small Business in India

Small businesses (also called MSME — Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises) play a vital role in the Indian economy, contributing significantly to GDP, employment, and exports.

MSME Classification (Revised 2020)

CategoryInvestment in Plant & MachineryAnnual Turnover
Micro Enterprise≤ ₹1 crore≤ ₹5 crore
Small Enterprise≤ ₹10 crore≤ ₹50 crore
Medium Enterprise≤ ₹50 crore≤ ₹250 crore

Importance of Small Business

  • Large employment generation (especially in rural areas).
  • Equitable distribution of wealth across regions.
  • Promotes entrepreneurship.
  • Produces specialised goods for niche markets.
  • Contributes significantly to exports (e.g., handicrafts, garments, leather).
  • Acts as ancillary units for large industries.

Problems of Small Business

  • Finance: Difficulty in getting credit; limited capital.
  • Marketing: Lack of brand recognition; cannot compete with big companies.
  • Technology: Cannot afford modern technology.
  • Management: Owner-managed; lack of professional management.
  • Competition: From large organised sector and cheap imports.

Government Support

Government provides support through: MSME Development Act, SIDBI (loans), Mudra Scheme (loans up to ₹10 lakh), cluster development, technology upgradation, marketing support, and reservation of items for SSIs.

Cottage and Rural Industries

Cottage industries (handloom, pottery, handicrafts) use traditional skills, family labour, and local raw materials. They preserve cultural heritage and provide rural employment.

Key Points
  • MSMEs contribute about 30% of India's GDP and over 40% of exports.
  • MUDRA (Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency): Provides loans to micro businesses under Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana.

Chapter 10: Internal Trade

Internal Trade

Definition
Trade within the geographical boundaries of a country is called Internal Trade or Domestic Trade. No customs duty is levied.

Types of Internal Trade

  • Wholesale Trade: Buying in large quantities from manufacturers and selling in smaller quantities to retailers.
  • Retail Trade: Buying from wholesalers and selling in small quantities directly to consumers.

Services of a Wholesaler

To Manufacturers: Bulk buying, storage, risk-bearing, financing, market information.
To Retailers: Availability of goods in small lots, credit, market information, technical advice.

Types of Retailers

Fixed Shop RetailersItinerant Retailers
Department Stores, Chain Stores, Supermarkets, Consumer Cooperatives, Mail-Order HousesHawkers, Pedlars, Market Traders, Street Stalls

Large-Scale Retail Organisations

  • Departmental Store: Multiple departments under one roof; one-stop shopping (Big Bazaar).
  • Chain Stores (Multiple Shops): Same retail outlets across many locations (Reliance Fresh).
  • Consumer Cooperative Store: Owned and managed by consumers; eliminates middlemen.
  • Mail Order Business: Goods sold through catalogues by post; no personal contact.
  • Supermarket / Hypermarket: Self-service; wide range of products; low prices.

GST (Goods and Services Tax)

GST is a unified indirect tax levied on goods and services across India (introduced July 1, 2017). It replaced multiple taxes (VAT, Service Tax, Excise). It follows a dual structure: CGST (Central) + SGST (State) or IGST (for interstate transactions).

Key Points
  • Wholesalers link manufacturers and retailers — they are the backbone of internal trade.
  • GST eliminated the cascading effect of taxes (tax on tax).
  • E-commerce platforms like Amazon, Flipkart are a modern form of retail trade.

Chapter 11: International Business

International Business

Definition
International Business refers to all commercial transactions — private and governmental — between two or more countries. It includes trade in goods, services, capital, and technology across national boundaries.

Why International Business?

  • No country is self-sufficient — different countries have different resources.
  • Principle of Comparative Advantage: Each country should produce what it produces best and trade for the rest.
  • Access to larger markets, spread risk, technology transfer.

Forms of International Business

  • Export-Import Trade: Simplest form — selling goods abroad (export) or buying from abroad (import).
  • Licensing and Franchising: Allowing foreign firms to use brand/patent/technology in exchange for fee/royalty.
  • Joint Ventures: Collaborating with a foreign partner to operate in their country.
  • Wholly Owned Subsidiaries: Setting up own company in a foreign country.
  • MNC: Operating in multiple countries with global integration.

Documents in International Trade

DocumentPurpose
Bill of LadingContract of carriage; title to goods at sea
Airway BillTitle document for air cargo
Bill of EntryFiled by importer at customs
Shipping BillFiled by exporter at customs
Letter of Credit (L/C)Bank guarantee of payment to exporter
Certificate of OriginCertifies the country of production

Trade Barriers and WTO

Trade barriers include tariffs (customs duties) and non-tariff barriers (quotas, licences). The WTO (World Trade Organisation) promotes free trade by reducing barriers between member countries.

Key Points
  • Balance of Trade: Exports − Imports. Favourable if exports > imports.
  • Balance of Payments: Broader than trade balance; includes services, investments, transfers.
  • Exim Bank of India: Provides finance and services for exporters and importers.
