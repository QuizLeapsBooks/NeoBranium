Water Resources High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains the importance of water, the causes of water scarcity, the role of multi-purpose river projects, and the need for rainwater harvesting. Board exams frequently test water scarcity causes, advantages and disadvantages of dams, and traditional rainwater harvesting methods.

1.1 Water: A Renewable Resource

Water covers about three-fourths (75%) of the Earth's surface, but only a small fraction is usable freshwater. Water is a renewable resource because it circulates through the hydrological cycle:

  • Evaporation: Water evaporates from oceans, lakes, and rivers
  • Condensation: Water vapor condenses to form clouds
  • Precipitation: Rain or snow falls back to Earth
  • Runoff and Recharge: Water returns to rivers and groundwater
🎯 Key Point: Water is a renewable resource because of the hydrological cycle. However, freshwater is limited — only 2.5% of Earth's water is freshwater, and less than 1% is available for human use.

2. Water Scarcity and the Need for Conservation

2.1 What is Water Scarcity?

Water scarcity means the shortage of water for a sustained period. It occurs when the demand for water exceeds the available supply, or when water is available but not usable due to poor quality.

2.2 Causes of Water Scarcity

CauseExplanation
Growing PopulationMore people means more demand for water for drinking, agriculture, and industry
Over-exploitationExcessive use of groundwater for irrigation and urban needs
Unequal AccessWater is unequally distributed among different social groups and regions
PollutionIndustrial waste, untreated sewage, and agricultural runoff contaminate water sources
Inefficient ManagementPoor infrastructure, leakage, and wastage in supply systems
Uneven RainfallSome regions receive abundant rainfall while others face droughts

2.3 Why Water Quality Matters

In some places, water is available in sufficient quantity but its quality is poor due to pollution. Such water is not fit for human consumption, leading to water scarcity even when water is physically present.

💡 Example: Many cities have enough water sources but the water is highly polluted with industrial effluents and sewage, making it unusable without treatment.

3. Multi-Purpose River Projects

3.1 What are Multi-Purpose River Projects?

A multi-purpose river project is a project that serves several purposes simultaneously — irrigation, electricity generation, flood control, water supply, and more.

3.2 Uses of Multi-Purpose River Projects

4. Dams: Uses and Criticism

4.1 What is a Dam?

A dam is a barrier built across a river to obstruct, direct, or slow the flow of water, often creating a reservoir, lake, or impoundment.

4.2 Advantages of Dams

  • Provide irrigation water for agriculture
  • Generate hydroelectricity
  • Help control floods
  • Supply water for domestic and industrial use
  • Support fish breeding and inland navigation
  • Create recreational spaces

4.3 Disadvantages of Dams

ProblemExplanation
DisplacementPeople lose homes, farmland, and livelihoods
SubmergenceForests and farmland are submerged
Disturbs River FlowNatural flow and sediment flow are affected
Affects Aquatic LifeMigration and spawning of aquatic animals are disrupted
SedimentationReservoirs fill with sediment, reducing capacity
Flood RiskDams built for flood control may themselves cause floods
Water DisputesInter-state conflicts over water sharing
⚠️ Case Study — Narmada Bachao Andolan: The Sardar Sarovar Dam on the Narmada River faced massive opposition from local communities due to displacement, environmental damage, and inadequate rehabilitation. This became a major environmental movement.

5. Water Disputes

Multi-purpose river projects can lead to inter-state water disputes because river water crosses state boundaries.

5.1 Example: Krishna-Godavari Dispute

Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh objected to the diversion of more water at Koyna by Maharashtra for a multi-purpose project. Their concern was that downstream flow would reduce and affect agriculture and industry.

5.2 Why Do Water Disputes Occur?

  • River water crosses state boundaries
  • When one state stores or diverts more water, another state faces shortage
  • Competing demands for irrigation, industry, and domestic use

6. Rainwater Harvesting

6.1 What is Rainwater Harvesting?

Rainwater harvesting means collecting and storing rainwater for future use. It can also recharge groundwater.

6.2 Why is Rainwater Harvesting Important?

  • It is a local, low-cost, and environmentally friendly alternative
  • Reduces dependence on groundwater and public water systems
  • Prevents urban flooding by reducing surface runoff
  • Improves groundwater levels through percolation
  • Suitable for different geographical conditions

6.3 Modern Methods

MethodDescription
Rooftop Rainwater HarvestingRainwater collected from roofs via pipes and stored in tanks or used for recharge
Percolation PitsPits dug to allow rainwater to seep into the ground and recharge groundwater
💡 Tamil Nadu's Initiative: Tamil Nadu was the first Indian state to make rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory for every building.

7. Traditional Rainwater Harvesting Methods in India

People in different parts of India developed different systems according to rainfall, soil, slope, and water needs.

RegionMethodDescription
Western HimalayasGuls and KulsDiversion channels for agriculture
RajasthanRooftop rainwater harvesting (Tankas)Underground tanks for drinking water
Bengal Flood PlainsInundation ChannelsChannels to irrigate fields
Rajasthan (Arid)Khadins (Jaisalmer) and JohadsRainfed storage structures to moisten soil
MeghalayaBamboo Drip IrrigationBamboo pipes to tap streams and springs

7.1 Tankas in Rajasthan

In semi-arid and desert regions of Rajasthan (Bikaner, Phalodi, Barmer), underground tanks called Tankas were built inside houses or courtyards. They were connected to sloping roofs. The first rain was discarded to clean the roof, and subsequent rainwater was collected. This "palar pani" (rainwater) was considered the purest form of natural water.

7.2 Bamboo Drip Irrigation

In Meghalaya, a traditional system uses bamboo pipes to tap streams and springs and convey water over long distances. About 18-20 litres enter the bamboo system and delivery at the plant site is reduced to 20-80 drops per minute.

🎯 Key Lesson: Local communities everywhere must be involved in natural resource management. Traditional methods are sustainable, low-cost, and adapted to local conditions.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing "water scarcity" with "drought" — scarcity can occur even with adequate rainfall if water is polluted
  • Forgetting that dams have both advantages and disadvantages
  • Not giving region-specific examples for traditional rainwater harvesting methods
  • Mixing up Tankas (Rajasthan) with Guls/Kuls (Western Himalayas)
