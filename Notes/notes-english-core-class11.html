HORNBILL — Prose Chapters

Chapter 1: The Portrait of a Lady

Author: Khushwant Singh

Summary

The narrator recalls his grandmother — a short, fat, slightly bent old woman with a wrinkled face, white hair, always dressed in white. He traces their relationship through three stages: childhood in the village, school years in the city, and after his studies abroad.

In the village, the grandmother was central to his life — she woke him up, prepared him for school, and accompanied him to the village school attached to the temple. When they moved to the city, a wall of silence grew between them as she could not follow his modern education. After his return from abroad, she was excited initially but slowly withdrew into her prayers. She died peacefully one evening, lips still moving in silent prayer.

Key Points
  • Autobiographical — Khushwant Singh writes about his own grandmother.
  • Three phases: village (close bond) → city (growing distance) → abroad return (final parting).
  • Symbol: Sparrows represent the grandmother's love and warmth; they mourn her death.
  • The grandmother's death is peaceful — she dies praying, not lamenting.
  • The wall of silence = generation gap created by modern education.
Themes
  • Generation gap: Modern education creates an unspoken distance.
  • Devotion and faith: Grandmother's lifelong devotion to God.
  • Love and loss: Quiet, unspoken love and grief of parting.
  • Change vs. tradition: Old world values vs. modern urban life.

Character Sketch

The GrandmotherReligious, loving, traditional. Found joy in praying and feeding sparrows. Never complained.
The NarratorRepresents modern educated youth. Feels the growing distance yet retains deep love and respect.
SimileImagerySymbolism (sparrows)FlashbackReminiscence

Chapter 2: We're Not Afraid to Die… if We Can All Be Together

Author: Gordon Cook and Alan East

Summary

The narrator, his wife Mary, son Jonathan (6), daughter Suzanne (7), and two crew members (Larry and Herb) attempt to replicate Captain James Cook's voyage around the world. They sail from Plymouth, England, in their boat Wavewalker.

A massive storm hits near the Cape of Good Hope with waves 15 metres high. The boat is severely damaged; the narrator is badly injured. Despite flooding and broken instruments, they navigate to find Amsterdam Island — a tiny dot in the Indian Ocean. The children's courage is the emotional heart: Jonathan's note "Not to worry Dad" and Suzanne's secret card wishing to be with her father prove that courage has no age limit.

Key Points
  • Voyage began July 2, 1976, from Plymouth, England.
  • Storm struck January 2; waves 15 metres high.
  • The narrator was washed overboard and pulled back to safety.
  • Sue's secret birthday card is the most touching moment in the story.
  • Amsterdam Island found on Jan 6 — a remarkable feat of damaged-instrument navigation.
Themes
  • Courage and determination: Fighting nature's fury without surrender.
  • Family bond: Children's emotional support sustains the adults.
  • Survival instinct: Human will to live against impossible odds.
  • Leadership: Calm decision-making in crisis.
First-person narrativeVivid imagerySuspenseChronological order

Chapter 3: Discovering Tut: the Saga Continues

Author: A.R. Williams

Summary

On January 5, 2005, archaeologist Zahi Hawass led a CT scan of Tutankhamun's mummy at the Valley of the Kings to unravel the mystery of his death. Tut became pharaoh at about 9 years of age and died around age 19. Howard Carter had discovered his tomb in 1922. The CT scan produced over 1,700 digital cross-section images but found no conclusive evidence of murder — the cause of death remains uncertain.

Key Points
  • Tutankhamun ruled c. 1332–1323 BC; died at about 19 years.
  • Tomb discovered by Howard Carter in 1922.
  • CT scan (2005) produced 1,700+ digital images — no murder evidence found.
  • Carter's team had damaged the mummy while removing it from the coffin.
  • Tut's burial included enormous treasures and golden coffins.
Themes
  • History and mystery: The unresolved secrets of ancient civilisations.
  • Science meets history: Using modern technology to explore the ancient world.
  • Mortality: Even the most powerful rulers are not immune to death.
Descriptive writingAllusion (Egyptian history)Scientific languageJournalistic style

Chapter 4: Landscape of the Soul

Author: Nathalie Trouveroy

Summary

The chapter explores the philosophy behind Chinese and European art. The author begins with the story of Chinese master Wu Daozi who painted a landscape for the Emperor and then mysteriously walked into his own painting and vanished — illustrating that Chinese art aims to capture inner spirit, not outer appearance.

This is contrasted with the European Renaissance technique of trompe l'oeil (fooling the eye) which creates the illusion of 3D on a flat canvas. The chapter also explains shanshui painting (mountain-water) representing cosmic duality: Yang (mountain, stable) and Yin (water, fluid). Chinese painter Gu Kaizhi's idea: "outward likeness" is less important than "inward truth."

Key Points
  • Wu Daozi — master painter who disappeared into his own painting.
  • Chinese art captures "spirit" not physical appearance (Daoist philosophy).
  • Shanshui = mountain (Yang) + water (Yin) — cosmic duality.
  • Trompe l'oeil — European technique to create optical illusion of depth.
  • Gu Kaizhi: inner truth more important than outward likeness.
Themes
  • Inner vs. outer reality: Art should capture the soul, not just the surface.
  • East vs. West: Different artistic philosophies and traditions.
  • Nature and cosmos: Art as expression of universal harmony.

Chapter 5: The Ailing Planet: the Green Movement's Role

Author: Nani Palkhivala

Summary

Jurist Nani Palkhivala writes about the deteriorating state of Earth and the rise of the Green Movement. The first truly global environmental movement began in 1972. The concept of sustainable development — meeting present needs without compromising future generations — is central.

He discusses the four principal biological systems: fisheries, forests, grasslands, and croplands — all in alarming decline. He stresses the Earth's resources are finite, that unsustainable population growth accelerates the crisis, and that all nations share responsibility. He ends with Lester Brown's quote urging governments to act as stewards of the planet.

Key Points
  • The Green Movement started in 1972 — first global environmental movement.
  • Sustainable development: Meet present needs without compromising the future.
  • Four principal biological systems: fisheries, forests, grasslands, croplands.
  • World population was growing at ~92 million per year (at time of writing).
  • The World Charter for Nature (1982) is referenced.
  • The Earth is compared to "a patient in declining health" — ailing planet metaphor.
Themes
  • Environmental conservation: Urgent need to protect Earth's limited resources.
  • Sustainable development: Balance between growth and nature's limits.
  • Human responsibility: Every individual and government as Earth's steward.
Metaphor (ailing planet)Statistics and dataRhetorical questionsQuotationsPersuasive tone

Chapter 6: The Browning Version

Author: Terence Rattigan

Summary

Andrew Crocker-Harris is a strict, unpopular Greek and Latin teacher known as "The Crock" or "The Himmler of the Lower Fifth." He is retiring due to illness and has a cold, failing marriage with Millie. A student, Taplow, describes him to Frank Hunter (science teacher) — yet Taplow genuinely respects him. At the end, Taplow presents Crocker-Harris with a copy of Robert Browning's translation of Agamemnon as a farewell gift. The teacher is deeply moved and weeps — his suppressed humanity emerging. Millie cruelly dismisses it as "sucking up."

Key Points
  • Andrew Crocker-Harris: strict, unpopular, called "Himmler of the Lower Fifth."
  • Taplow: a student who genuinely respects the teacher despite his strictness.
  • Frank Hunter: popular science teacher — a foil to Crocker-Harris.
  • The Browning Version = Browning's translation of Agamemnon given as a farewell gift.
  • Crocker-Harris weeps — the cold exterior hides a vulnerable human being.
  • Millie's cruelty vs. Taplow's sincerity — contrasting human responses.
Themes
  • Appearance vs. reality: Cold exterior hides a vulnerable human.
  • Genuine respect: A single sincere act can move the hardest heart.
  • Teacher-student relationship: Real education leaves lasting impressions.
  • Cruelty of indifference: Millie's callousness vs. Taplow's warmth.
Dramatic ironyDialogueContrastFoil characterAllusion (Agamemnon)

Chapter 7: The Adventure

Author: Jayant Narlikar

Summary

History professor Gaitonde from Pune steps into an alternate reality due to a catastrophic accident. In this parallel world, the Marathas won the Battle of Panipat (1761) against Ahmad Shah Abdali — so India was never colonised. The British East India Company remained only a trading company; Bombay is a thriving independent Indian city.

Rajendra Deshpande later explains the science: the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics and the Catastrophe Theory explain how Gaitonde's consciousness temporarily moved to another branch of history at the critical moment of the accident.

Key Points
  • Based on the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics.
  • Key divergence: Marathas win Battle of Panipat (1761) — India never colonised.
  • In alternate world: Bombay is an independent Indian city; East India Co. is just a trader.
  • Catastrophe Theory + transitional state explain the "jump" between worlds.
  • Professor visits a library and finds history books with different outcomes.
Themes
  • Science fiction meets history: Imagining alternate histories through quantum science.
  • Identity and reality: What makes our world "real"?
  • History and its consequences: One battle can change the fate of a nation.
Science fictionAlternate historyFlashbackAllusion (Battle of Panipat)

Chapter 8: Silk Road

Author: Nick Middleton

Summary

A travelogue about the author's journey from Ravu (Tibet) to Mount Kailash for the holy circumambulation (parikrama) — a sacred Hindu and Buddhist pilgrimage. He crosses the Tibetan plateau, traverses the Tsang Po river, encounters nomadic Tibetans called Drokba (the "Black Tents"), yaks, and vast desolate landscapes. The journey follows part of the ancient Silk Road — the network of trade routes connecting China to the Mediterranean. Despite suffering from nasal congestion, the journey is deeply spiritual and personally enriching.

Key Points
  • Route: Ravu → Mount Kailash; purpose is circumambulation (parikrama).
  • Drokba = Tibetan nomads, the "Black Tents" people.
  • Tsang Po river = Tibetan name of the Brahmaputra.
  • Follows the ancient Silk Road trade route.
  • The landscape is stark, beautiful, and spiritually significant.
Themes
  • Spirituality and pilgrimage: Sacred importance of Mount Kailash.
  • Nature's grandeur: Awe-inspiring yet harsh Tibetan plateau.
  • Cultural diversity: Tibetan nomadic life and ancient traditions.
TravelogueDescriptive imageryFirst-person narrativeHumour
HORNBILL — Poetry

Poem 1: A Photograph

Poet: Shirley Toulson

Central Idea

The poem reflects on the permanence of a photograph contrasted with the transience of life. The speaker looks at an old photograph of her mother as a young girl at the beach with her cousins. The photograph captures a moment of joy, but both the mother and that carefree time are forever gone. The poem explores grief, nostalgia, and how time takes everything away.

Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1: The photograph shows the mother (about 12 years old) standing at the beach with cousins Betty and Dolly. The sea washed their feet. Uncle Percy took the photo.
Stanza 2: The mother used to laugh looking at the photo — at the old-fashioned swimwear and hairstyles. The speaker remembers those shared moments of laughter.
Stanza 3: The mother has been dead for "some twelve years." The speaker is now in silence — unable to express grief. The loss is beyond words.
Key Points
  • Three time frames: mother's childhood → speaker's childhood (mother laughing at photo) → present (mother is dead).
  • The sea = eternal; the photograph = frozen moment in time.
  • "Both the now and then" — the speaker cannot say which loss is greater.
  • Silence at the end is the most powerful expression of grief — beyond language.
Themes
  • Transience of life vs. permanence of memory.
  • Grief and loss.
  • Nostalgia and the unstoppable passage of time.
ImageryEnjambmentContrastIronySymbolism (sea, photograph)

Poem 2: The Laburnum Top

Poet: Ted Hughes

Central Idea

A laburnum tree stands silent and still in September — leaves yellowing, seeds fallen. Suddenly a goldfinch arrives, the tree trembles with life as nestlings chirp excitedly. The bird feeds them and then departs, leaving the tree silent and empty again. The goldfinch is the sole life-giving force; without it, the tree returns to death-like stillness.

Key Points
  • Setting: September — autumn, laburnum yellowing and seed-empty.
  • The goldfinch arrives like a "machine" — alert, purposeful, precise.
  • Tree "trembles and thrills" at the bird's arrival — sudden burst of life.
  • After the goldfinch departs, the tree returns to silence and stillness.
  • The goldfinch is the "engine" of the tree's brief, vibrant life.
Themes
  • Life and death cycle in nature.
  • Dependency and nurturing (mother bird and nestlings).
  • Transience of life and energy.
PersonificationMetaphor (engine)Visual and auditory imageryAlliteration

Poem 3: The Voice of the Rain

Poet: Walt Whitman

Central Idea

The rain speaks and describes itself — it rises from earth and sea as vapour, becomes clouds, then falls back as rain to wash droughts away and give life to seeds and beauty to the land. Just as a song rises from the poet's heart, travels out, and returns to him, rain rises from earth, serves its purpose, and returns. The cycle of rain mirrors the cycle of poetry.

Key Points
  • Rain calls itself the "Poem of Earth."
  • Water cycle compared to the journey of a poem — both arise, serve, and return to source.
  • "Reck'd or unreck'd" — rain does its work whether appreciated or not.
  • Last two lines are the poet's voice linking rain's cycle to a song's journey.
Themes
  • The eternal cycle of nature (water cycle).
  • Poetry and rain as parallel: arise, serve a purpose, return to origin.
  • Life-giving force of rain.
Personification (rain speaks)Extended metaphorApostropheFree verseRepetition

Poem 4: Childhood

Poet: Markus Natten

Central Idea

The poet wonders when exactly he lost his childhood and became an adult. He identifies three turning points: realising Hell and Heaven cannot physically exist (loss of naive religious belief); realising adults preached love but did not practise it (disillusionment with hypocrisy); and discovering his own individual mind. His childhood now lives only in the face of an infant.

Key Points
  • Refrain: "When did my childhood go?" — repeated at the start of each stanza.
  • Three stages of lost innocence: religion → adult hypocrisy → self-awareness/individuality.
  • Childhood "hid in an infant's face" — innocence survives only in babies.
  • Gaining individuality = losing innocence. A bittersweet trade-off.
Themes
  • Loss of innocence and transition to adulthood.
  • Hypocrisy: adults say one thing, do another.
  • Individual identity and self-awareness.
RefrainRhetorical questionIronyImagery

Poem 5: Father to Son

Poet: Elizabeth Jennings

Central Idea

The poem explores the painful gulf between a father and his grown son. They have lived together for years but feel like strangers. The father does not understand the world his son has built for himself. The son wants to forge his own path. Yet both desire reconciliation — they want to speak but cannot break the silence. The poem universalises the generation gap.

Key Points
  • Father has lived with son for years but does not truly "know" him.
  • The son has built his own world — different values, different dreams.
  • "I would have him prodigal" — father wishes the son would return, like the Biblical Prodigal Son.
  • Both want warmth but cannot bridge the silence — mutual pain and love coexist.
  • No spring = no renewal; yet the desire for reconciliation remains alive.
Themes
  • Generation gap and communication breakdown.
  • Love without understanding — love is not enough without dialogue.
  • Desire for reconciliation.
Allusion (Prodigal Son)Metaphor (desert, climate)ImageryRhyme scheme
SNAPSHOTS — Supplementary Reader

Story 1: The Summer of the Beautiful White Horse

Author: William Saroyan

Summary

Mourad, an Armenian boy, wakes his cousin Aram early one morning with a beautiful white horse. Their Garoghlanian tribe is famous for honesty and trustworthiness — even in poverty. Aram suspects Mourad stole it. They ride it secretly every morning for weeks. When the owner, farmer John Byro, meets their uncle Khosrove (who dismisses everything with "It is no harm, pay no attention to it"), he complains about his missing horse. Eventually, Mourad returns the horse. Byro, trusting the family's reputation, says the horse looks like his but must be a different one — allowing the boys to walk away with dignity.

Key Points
  • Garoghlanian tribe: extremely poor but fiercely honest — their reputation is everything.
  • Mourad: impulsive, adventurous; inherits "crazy streak" from Uncle Khosrove.
  • Uncle Khosrove's catchphrase: "It is no harm, pay no attention to it."
  • The horse is returned after several weeks of secret riding.
  • John Byro's trust in the tribe's reputation — the story's moral resolution.
Themes
  • Honesty and the power of a family's reputation.
  • Childhood innocence and love of freedom and beauty.
  • Cultural identity and family values.

Story 2: The Address

Author: Marga Minco

Summary

Set after World War II. A Jewish woman tries to recover her mother's belongings given to Mrs Dorling for safekeeping before the Nazi occupation. On her first visit to 46 Marconi Street, she is coldly turned away. On a second visit she enters and sees all her mother's familiar objects — the silver cutlery, the tablecloth, the green knitted cloth — displayed cheaply and out of context. The objects, separated from their original home and meaning, feel alien. She decides she no longer wants them. The address, and the past it represented, must be forgotten.

Key Points
  • Set during and after Nazi occupation of the Netherlands (WWII).
  • Mrs Dorling had taken the possessions for "safekeeping" — but never returned them.
  • The narrator cannot connect emotionally with the objects in their new setting.
  • Objects without context lose their emotional meaning and value.
  • The narrator decides to forget the address — the past cannot be reclaimed.
Themes
  • Loss and displacement — physical and emotional.
  • Memory and the impossibility of reclaiming the past.
  • Material vs. emotional value of objects.

Story 3: Ranga's Marriage

Author: Masti Venkatesha Iyengar

Summary

Set in Hosahalli village, Karnataka. Ranga, the accountant's son, returns from Bangalore — the first villager to study in the city. He says he will only marry a mature, older woman. The narrator (Shyama) schemes to get Ranga married to his niece Ratna. He engineers a meeting where Ranga hears Ratna sing and is instantly smitten. The astrologer Shastri (coached by Shyama) predicts Ranga will marry a girl whose name relates to the ocean — Ratna fits (ratna = gem, found in the ocean). Ranga marries Ratna. Years later their son is named Shyama — in the narrator's honour.

Key Points
  • First-person narration by Shyama — warm, humorous, and gently satirical.
  • The story mocks social customs around marriage and astrology with gentle irony.
  • Ranga = educated youth; Hosahalli = traditional India — contrast of outlooks.
  • Despite modern views, Ranga succumbs to traditional matchmaking.
  • Celebrates village life and its simple, affectionate social fabric.
Themes
  • Traditional vs. modern outlook on marriage.
  • Village life and its warm social fabric.
  • Gentle humour and situational irony.

Story 4: Albert Einstein at School

Author: Patrick Pringle

Summary

An excerpt from Einstein's biography set in Munich. Einstein rebels against rote learning — he argues with history teacher Mr Braun that memorising dates is pointless; understanding ideas is what matters. He is told he is a disgrace. At home in a rough boarding house, Einstein finds solace only in maths and his violin. His only friend is Yuri, a medical student. His maths teacher Mr Koch appreciates his genius. Einstein plans to leave for Milan and gets a false medical certificate from a doctor Yuri arranges. He then learns he has been expelled anyway — and takes it calmly. He leaves with a glowing reference from Mr Koch.

Key Points
  • Set in Munich; Einstein hates rote-learning methods.
  • Mr Braun (history) clashes with Einstein; Mr Koch (maths) appreciates him.
  • Yuri = Einstein's only real friend; arranges the medical certificate.
  • Einstein is expelled — but accepts it calmly and leaves with dignity.
  • The story exposes the failure of rigid education to nurture creative genius.
Themes
  • Individual thinking vs. mechanical rote learning.
  • The failure of conventional education systems.
  • Perseverance and belief in one's own vision.

Story 5: Mother's Day

Author: J.B. Priestley

Summary

A comic one-act play. Mrs Annie Pearson is a timid housewife taken for granted by her family — husband George, son Cyril, and daughter Doris. Her bold neighbour Mrs Fitzgerald, who practises fortune-telling, proposes a body-swap to teach the family a lesson. They exchange, and "Mrs Pearson" (Fitzgerald's soul in Annie's body) speaks firmly to each family member — refusing to cook or iron, telling them uncomfortable truths. The family is shocked and chastened. The souls re-swap and Annie's family now treats her with respect.

Key Points
  • Comic satire — a one-act play.
  • Annie Pearson: timid, self-sacrificing; taken for granted by her family.
  • Mrs Fitzgerald: bold, direct; serves as Annie's catalyst for change.
  • The body-swap is a comic device to hold the family up to a mirror.
  • Message: Mothers deserve respect; families must not take women for granted.
Themes
  • Domestic exploitation and women's dignity.
  • Need for mutual respect within families.
  • Assertiveness and self-worth.

Story 6: The Ghat of the Only World

Author: Amitav Ghosh

Summary

A personal essay and tribute by novelist Amitav Ghosh to his friend, the celebrated Kashmiri-American poet Agha Shahid Ali, who was dying of a brain tumour. Ghosh writes about their conversations in the months before Shahid's death in December 2001. Shahid was a poet known for bringing the ghazal form to English literature. Despite knowing he was dying, Shahid remained cheerful, witty, and concerned about his legacy. He asked Ghosh to write about him — this essay is the fulfilment of that promise. The title refers to the threshold between life and death.

Key Points
  • Agha Shahid Ali: Kashmiri-American poet; famous for English ghazals.
  • Diagnosed with a brain tumour; died December 2001.
  • Ghosh writes the essay at Shahid's own request.
  • "Ghat of the Only World" = the threshold/doorstep to death.
  • Shahid's courage and wit in the face of death is the essay's emotional core.
Themes
  • Friendship and tribute.
  • Acceptance of death with grace and dignity.
  • The power of art and poetry to outlive the artist.

Story 7: Birth

Author: A.J. Cronin

Summary

Andrew Manson, a young newly qualified doctor in a Welsh mining village, is called late at night to assist in a difficult childbirth at the Morgan household. The baby is born apparently dead — white, limp, not breathing (asphyxia). Andrew refuses to give up. Recalling a technique from a textbook, he alternately immerses the baby in hot and cold water and rubs it vigorously. The baby cries — it is alive. He also saves the mother. Andrew is overwhelmed with a sense of purpose — realising this is "something new," something that makes his personal troubles insignificant.

Key Points
  • Andrew Manson: young, determined, newly qualified doctor — the protagonist.
  • Baby appears stillborn due to asphyxia (lack of oxygen at birth).
  • Technique used: alternating hot/cold water immersion + vigorous rubbing.
  • Both mother and child are saved — a double miracle in a single night.
  • Andrew feels "exalted" — a renewed sense of purpose and calling.
Themes
  • The miracle and infinite value of human life.
  • Dedication and perseverance in the face of despair.
  • Medicine as a noble calling, not just a profession.

Story 8: The Tale of Melon City

Poet: Vikram Seth

Central Idea

A mock-heroic narrative poem written with dry wit and dark humour. A "just and peaceable" king orders an arch to be built across the main road. The arch turns out too low and knocks off the king's crown. Furious, the king orders the chief builder hanged — but blame shifts from builder to architect to workers to king himself. Eventually, an idiot (declared the most peaceful man) is randomly crowned king. Citizens just want a ruler who does not interfere in their lives — so they are happy with a melon as king. A sharp political satire.

Key Points
  • Arch built too low — knocks the king's crown off.
  • Blame chain: engineer → architect → workers → king himself.
  • An idiot points to a melon; the law says "the next person to pass the gate" is king — so the melon becomes king.
  • "Melon City" = the city ruled by a melon (symbol of absurd governance).
  • Citizens prefer a quiet, non-interfering ruler — bitter political observation.
Themes
  • Absurdity of bureaucracy and governance.
  • Political satire: leaders can be as useless as a melon.
  • People value peace over justice or the quality of leadership.
Mock-heroicSatireIronyHumourNarrative poem
