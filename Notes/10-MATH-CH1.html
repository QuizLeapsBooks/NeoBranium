Chapter 1: Real Numbers High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Real Numbers is the foundation of Class 10 Mathematics. It introduces key concepts like Euclid's Division Lemma, the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, HCF and LCM, and irrational numbers. Questions from this chapter are frequently asked in board exams and carry significant weightage.

What are Real Numbers?

All rational numbers and irrational numbers together are called real numbers. In simple terms, every number that can be represented on a number line is a real number. Examples: 2, -5, 3/4, 0.25, √2, √5.

Rational and Irrational Numbers

  • Rational Number: A number that can be written in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0. Examples: 1/2, -3/4, 5, 0, 0.75.
  • Irrational Number: A number that cannot be expressed in the form p/q. Its decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating. Examples: √2, √3, √5, π.
📌 Number System Hierarchy:
  • Natural Numbers (N): {1, 2, 3, 4, ...}
  • Whole Numbers (W): {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}
  • Integers (Z): {..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ...}
  • Rational Numbers (Q): p/q form where q ≠ 0
  • Irrational Numbers (Q'): Cannot be written in p/q form
  • Real Numbers (R): All rational + all irrational numbers

1. Euclid's Division Lemma

Euclid's Division Lemma is a fundamental result in number theory that helps in finding the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two positive integers.

For any two positive integers a and b, there exist unique integers q and r such that:

a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b

Here, a is the dividend, b is the divisor, q is the quotient, and r is the remainder.

Euclid's Division Algorithm

Euclid's Division Algorithm is a technique to compute the HCF of two given positive integers using Euclid's Division Lemma. To find the HCF of two positive integers c and d (c > d), follow these steps:

  1. Apply Euclid's Division Lemma to c and d: c = dq + r, where 0 ≤ r < d.
  2. If r = 0, then d is the HCF of c and d.
  3. If r ≠ 0, apply the division lemma to d and r.
  4. Continue the process until the remainder is zero. The divisor at this stage will be the required HCF.
📌 Important Note:
  • Euclid's Division Lemma is stated for positive integers only, but it can be extended for all integers except zero.
  • Lemma: A proven statement used for proving another statement.
  • Algorithm: A series of well-defined steps which give a procedure for solving a type of problem.

Worked Example: HCF of 135 and 225

Step 1: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90
Step 2: 135 = 90 × 1 + 45
Step 3: 90 = 45 × 2 + 0

Since remainder = 0, the divisor 45 is the HCF.
HCF (135, 225) = 45

2. Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic states that every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.

x = p₁ × p₂ × p₃ × ... × pₙ
where p₁, p₂, ..., pₙ are primes (arranged in ascending order)

Prime Factorisation

Prime factorisation means writing a composite number entirely as a product of prime numbers.

Examples of Prime Factorisation:
  • 140 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 7 = 2² × 5 × 7
  • 156 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 13 = 2² × 3 × 13
  • 3825 = 3 × 3 × 5 × 5 × 17 = 3² × 5² × 17
  • 5005 = 5 × 7 × 11 × 13
  • 7429 = 17 × 19 × 23
Exam Tip: Do not stop a prime factorisation while any factor is still composite. For example, 140 = 4 × 35 is not the final prime factorisation.

Applications of Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

  • To find the HCF and LCM of two or more positive integers.
  • To prove the irrationality of numbers like √2, √3, √5.
  • To determine the nature of the decimal expansion of rational numbers.

3. HCF and LCM

The Highest Common Factor (HCF) is the greatest number that divides all given numbers exactly. The Least Common Multiple (LCM) is the smallest positive number exactly divisible by all given numbers.

📌 Rules for HCF and LCM:
  • HCF Rule: Take the smallest powers of the common prime factors.
  • LCM Rule: Take the greatest powers of all prime factors involved.

Worked Example: HCF and LCM of 12 and 18

Prime Factorisation:
12 = 2² × 3¹
18 = 2¹ × 3²

HCF: 2¹ × 3¹ = 6 (smallest powers of common factors)
LCM: 2² × 3² = 36 (greatest powers of all factors)

Important Relationship

HCF (a, b) × LCM (a, b) = a × b
(For any two positive integers a and b)
Note: This relationship is only valid for two positive integers. For three numbers, HCF(a, b, c) × LCM(a, b, c) ≠ a × b × c.

Finding HCF and LCM of Three Numbers

HCF (a, b, c) = (a × b × c × LCM (a, b, c)) / (LCM (a, b) × LCM (b, c) × LCM (a, c))

4. Irrational Numbers

An irrational number is a number that cannot be expressed in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0. Its decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating.

Examples of Irrational Numbers

  • √2 = 1.41421356...
  • √3 = 1.73205080...
  • √5 = 2.23606797...
  • π = 3.14159265...

Proving Irrationality (Proof by Contradiction)

To prove that a number is irrational, we use the method of proof by contradiction. We assume the opposite (that the number is rational) and then show that this assumption leads to a contradiction.

📌 Key Properties:
  • The sum or difference of a rational and an irrational number is always irrational.
  • The product or quotient of a non-zero rational number and an irrational number is always irrational.
  • If p is a prime number, then √p is irrational.

Worked Example: Prove that √2 is irrational

Proof:

Step 1 (Assumption): Let √2 be rational. Then √2 = a/b, where a and b are coprime integers (b ≠ 0).

Step 2: Squaring both sides: 2 = a²/b² ⟹ a² = 2b²

Step 3: This means 2 divides a², so 2 divides a (by the property: if p divides a², then p divides a).

Step 4: Let a = 2c for some integer c. Then (2c)² = 2b² ⟹ 4c² = 2b² ⟹ b² = 2c²

Step 5: This means 2 divides b², so 2 divides b.

Step 6: But a and b are coprime, so they cannot both be divisible by 2. This is a contradiction.

Conclusion: Our assumption is wrong. Hence, √2 is irrational.

5. Decimal Expansions of Rational Numbers

Rational numbers can be expressed in decimal form. Their decimal expansion can be of two types:

Type 1: Terminating Decimal

A decimal that has a finite number of digits after the decimal point. Example: 0.5, 1.75, -3.25.

Type 2: Non-Terminating Repeating Decimal

A decimal that continues indefinitely but follows a cyclic pattern. Example: 0.333... (1/3), 0.142857... (1/7).

📌 Important Theorem:
  • Let x = p/q be a rational number in its simplest form (p and q are coprime).
  • x is a terminating decimal if the prime factorisation of q is of the form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ, where n and m are non-negative integers.
  • x is a non-terminating repeating decimal if the prime factorisation of q is not of the form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ.

Examples

Terminating: 3/8 = 3/(2³) = 0.375 (denominator is of form 2ⁿ)

Terminating: 7/20 = 7/(2² × 5) = 0.35 (denominator is of form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ)

Non-Terminating Repeating: 1/3 = 0.333... (denominator 3 is not of form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ)

Non-Terminating Repeating: 5/11 = 0.454545... (denominator 11 is not of form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ)

Most Repeated Previous Year Questions

  1. Use Euclid's division algorithm to find the HCF of 135 and 225. (2 Marks) — Answer: 225 = 135 × 1 + 90; 135 = 90 × 1 + 45; 90 = 45 × 2 + 0. HCF = 45.
  2. Express 140 as a product of its prime factors. (1 Mark) — Answer: 140 = 2² × 5 × 7.
  3. Given that HCF (306, 657) = 9, find LCM (306, 657). (2 Marks) — Answer: LCM = (306 × 657) / 9 = 22338.
  4. Prove that √5 is an irrational number. (3 Marks) — Answer: By contradiction, assume √5 is rational. Then √5 = a/b, leading to a contradiction when both a and b become divisible by 5.
  5. Check whether 6ⁿ can end with the digit 0 for any natural number n. (2 Marks) — Answer: No, because 6ⁿ = (2 × 3)ⁿ, and 5 is not a prime factor, so it cannot be divisible by 10.
  6. The LCM of two prime numbers p and q (p > q) is 221. Find the value of 3p - q. (2 Marks) — Answer: p = 17, q = 13. 3(17) - 13 = 38.
  7. Show that 5 × 11 × 17 + 3 × 11 is a composite number. (2 Marks) — Answer: 5 × 11 × 17 + 3 × 11 = 11(5 × 17 + 3) = 11 × 88. Since it has factors other than 1 and itself, it is composite.
  8. Find the HCF of 1260 and 7344 using Euclid's algorithm. (2 Marks) — Answer: HCF = 12.
  9. Prove that 3 + 2√5 is irrational, given that √5 is irrational. (3 Marks) — Answer: By contradiction, assume 3 + 2√5 is rational. Then 2√5 = a/b - 3, leading to √5 being rational, which is a contradiction.
  10. What is the greatest possible speed at which a girl can walk 95 m and 171 m in an exact number of minutes? (2 Marks) — Answer: HCF(95, 171) = 19 m/min.

Quick Revision — At a Glance

🎯 Key Formulas & Concepts:
  • Euclid's Division Lemma: a = bq + r, 0 ≤ r < b
  • HCF × LCM = Product of two numbers
  • Terminating Decimal: Denominator is of form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ
  • Non-Terminating Repeating: Denominator is not of form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ
  • Irrational Numbers: Cannot be written in p/q form, non-terminating non-repeating
  • Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: Every composite number has a unique prime factorisation
🎯 Key Tips for Exam:
  • Always verify that the denominator is in its simplest form before checking for terminating decimal.
  • For irrationality proofs, always use proof by contradiction.
  • Remember: HCF × LCM = a × b only for two numbers.
  • If p is prime and p divides a², then p divides a.
  • The sum of a rational and an irrational number is always irrational.
  • The product of a non-zero rational and an irrational number is always irrational.
Did You Know? The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic was first recorded as Proposition 14 of Book IX in Euclid's Elements. However, the first correct proof was given by Carl Friedrich Gauss, often referred to as the 'Prince of Mathematicians.'
