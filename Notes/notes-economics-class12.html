Chapter 1: National Income and Related Aggregates

Meaning of Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole, dealing with aggregates like national income, general price level, and total employment.

Circular Flow of Income

The flow of money income or flow of goods and services across different sectors of the economy. In a 2-sector model (Households & Firms):

  • Real Flow: Flow of factor services from households to firms, and flow of goods from firms to households.
  • Money Flow: Flow of factor payments from firms to households, and consumption expenditure from households to firms.

Basic Concepts

  • Domestic Territory: Geographical territory plus ships/aircrafts operated by residents, embassies abroad, etc.
  • Normal Resident: A person/institution who ordinarily resides in the country and whose centre of economic interest lies there.
  • Factor Income vs Transfer Income: Factor income is earned for rendering services (Rent, Wages, Interest, Profit). Transfer income is unearned (Gifts, Pensions) - not included in National Income.

Aggregates

  • Gross vs Net: Net = Gross - Depreciation.
  • Domestic vs National: National = Domestic + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA).
  • Market Price (MP) vs Factor Cost (FC): FC = MP - Net Indirect Taxes (Indirect Taxes - Subsidies).

Methods of Calculating National Income (NNP at FC)

  1. Value Added Method: Value of Output - Intermediate Consumption = GDP at MP.
  2. Income Method: Compensation of Employees + Operating Surplus (Rent+Interest+Profit) + Mixed Income = NDP at FC.
  3. Expenditure Method: Private Final Consumption Exp. + Govt. Final Consumption Exp. + Gross Domestic Capital Formation + Net Exports = GDP at MP.

Chapter 2: Money and Banking

Meaning and Functions of Money

Money is anything which is generally accepted as a medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value, and means for standard of deferred payment.

Money Supply

Total stock of money in circulation among the public at a particular point of time (it is a stock concept). Does not include money held by government or banking system.

  • M1 Measure: Currency with public + Demand deposits with commercial banks + Other deposits with RBI.

Commercial Banks and Credit Creation

Commercial banks accept deposits and grant loans. They create credit/money in the economy based on the deposits received and the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR).

Money Multiplier
Money Multiplier = 1 / LRR (Legal Reserve Ratio). Total Credit Created = Initial Deposit × Money Multiplier.

Central Bank (RBI)

The apex body that controls, operates, regulates, and directs the entire banking and monetary structure of the country.

Functions of RBI

  • Currency Authority (Bank of issue).
  • Banker to the Government.
  • Banker's Bank and Supervisor.
  • Controller of Money Supply and Credit.

Tools of Credit Control

  • Quantitative: Repo Rate, Bank Rate, Open Market Operations (OMO), CRR, SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio).
  • Qualitative: Margin Requirements, Moral Suasion.

Chapter 3: Determination of Income and Employment

Aggregate Demand (AD) and Aggregate Supply (AS)

  • AD: Total demand for final goods and services in the economy. AD = C + I + G + (X-M). In a 2-sector model, AD = C + I.
  • AS: Total value of final goods and services planned to be produced. AS = National Income (Y) = C + S.

Consumption Function

Relationship between consumption and income. C = c̅ + bY, where c̅ is autonomous consumption (consumption when Y=0) and b is Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC).

  • APC: Average Propensity to Consume = C/Y.
  • MPC: Change in C / Change in Y.

Saving Function

S = -c̅ + (1-b)Y, where (1-b) is Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS). Note: MPC + MPS = 1, and APC + APS = 1.

Equilibrium Level of Income

Achieved when AD = AS (or C+I = C+S, which means I = S). Planned Investment = Planned Saving.

Investment Multiplier (K)

Ratio of change in income to the initial change in investment. K = ΔY / ΔI. Also, K = 1 / (1-MPC) = 1 / MPS.

Deficient Demand and Excess Demand

  • Deficient Demand: AD < AS at full employment level. Creates a Deflationary Gap. Remedied by expansionary policy (decrease repo rate, CRR, taxes; increase govt spending).
  • Excess Demand: AD > AS at full employment level. Creates an Inflationary Gap. Remedied by contractionary policy (increase repo rate, CRR, taxes; decrease govt spending).

Chapter 4: Government Budget and the Economy

Meaning of Government Budget

An annual statement showing item-wise estimates of expected receipts and anticipated expenditure of the government during a fiscal year.

Objectives of Budget

Reallocation of resources, reducing inequalities in income and wealth, economic stability, management of public enterprises, economic growth.

Components of Budget

  • Revenue Budget: Revenue Receipts and Revenue Expenditure.
  • Capital Budget: Capital Receipts and Capital Expenditure.

Revenue vs Capital Receipts

  • Revenue Receipts: Neither create liability nor reduce assets. (Tax revenue like Income Tax, GST, and Non-Tax revenue like fees, fines).
  • Capital Receipts: Either create a liability (Borrowings) or reduce assets (Disinvestment, Recovery of loans).

Revenue vs Capital Expenditure

  • Revenue Expenditure: Neither creates assets nor reduces liabilities. (Salaries, pensions, interest payments, subsidies).
  • Capital Expenditure: Either creates assets (Building roads, schools) or reduces liabilities (Repayment of loans).

Budget Deficits

  1. Revenue Deficit: Revenue Expenditure - Revenue Receipts. (Indicates inability to meet regular expenses).
  2. Fiscal Deficit: Total Expenditure - Total Receipts excluding borrowings. (Indicates total borrowing requirement).
  3. Primary Deficit: Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments. (Indicates borrowing needed to meet expenses other than interest).

Chapter 5: Balance of Payments

Meaning of Balance of Payments (BoP)

Definition
BoP is a systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world during a given period of time.

Components of BoP

  1. Current Account: Records exports and imports of goods (visible items), services (invisible items), and unilateral transfers (gifts, remittances). It does not affect the asset/liability status of the country.
  2. Capital Account: Records transactions that affect the asset or liability status of the country (e.g., FDI, FII, borrowings, lending).

Balance of Trade (BoT)

Difference between the value of exports and imports of visible goods only. BoT is a part of Current Account.

Foreign Exchange Rate

The rate at which one currency is exchanged for another.

  • Fixed Exchange Rate: Set and maintained by the government/central bank.
  • Flexible Exchange Rate: Determined by market forces of demand and supply of foreign exchange.
  • Managed Floating: Market determines the rate, but central bank intervenes to prevent excessive fluctuations (also called "Dirty Floating").

Appreciation vs Depreciation (Flexible System)

Depreciation of domestic currency means it loses value relative to foreign currency (e.g., $1 goes from ₹70 to ₹75). It makes exports cheaper and imports expensive. Appreciation is the opposite.

Devaluation vs Revaluation (Fixed System)

Devaluation is the intentional reduction in the value of domestic currency by the government. Revaluation is intentional increase.

Chapter 6: Development Experience (1947-90) and Economic Reforms since 1991

Brief Overview

This section is closely related to the Indian Economic Development syllabus taught in Class 11/12 depending on the curriculum year.

Between 1947-1990, India followed an inward-looking, protectionist, state-led model (Five Year Plans, Public Sector dominance, Licence Raj, Import Substitution). This led to slow "Hindu rate of growth" and eventual BoP crisis in 1991.

Economic Reforms of 1991 (LPG)

  • Liberalisation: Freeing the economy from direct controls. Included industrial deregulation, financial sector reforms, tax reforms, and foreign exchange reforms.
  • Privatisation: Transfer of ownership/management of public enterprises to private sector (through disinvestment/denationalisation).
  • Globalisation: Integrating the national economy with the world economy. Removal of trade barriers, encouraging FDI.
Key Impacts
Increased forex reserves, higher GDP growth driven by the service sector (IT/BPO), control of inflation. However, agriculture was largely neglected, leading to jobless growth and increased inequality.

Chapter 7: Current Challenges facing Indian Economy

Human Capital Formation

Investment in education, health, and training to increase the productive capacity of the population.

Rural Development

Focuses on action plans for the development of areas lagging behind. Key issues: Agricultural credit (NABARD, SHGs), Agricultural marketing (APMC, MSP), Diversification into non-farm activities (animal husbandry, fisheries), Organic farming.

Employment

Major issues include jobless growth, informalisation of the workforce (shift from formal secure jobs to informal insecure contracts), and disguised unemployment in agriculture.

Sustainable Economic Development

Development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations. Key environmental issues in India: land degradation, biodiversity loss, air/water pollution, solid waste management.

Chapter 8: Development Experience of India – A Comparison with Neighbours

Comparison: India, China, Pakistan

All three nations started their development journeys around the same time (late 1940s) but diverged significantly.

China

  • Reforms started in 1978 under Deng Xiaoping.
  • Strategy: Focus on manufacturing, export-led growth, Special Economic Zones (SEZs) to attract FDI, dual pricing.
  • Result: Fastest growing economy, massive poverty reduction, high HDI, but political restrictions exist.

Pakistan

  • Reforms started in 1988.
  • Strategy: Relied heavily on foreign aid and remittances, political instability hampered consistent policy.
  • Result: Lower growth rate, severe debt crises, lagging social indicators (education/health).

India

  • Reforms started in 1991.
  • Strategy: Service-sector led growth, democratic setup, strong domestic demand.
  • Result: Strong IT sector, moderate poverty reduction, but manufacturing still lags behind China, and significant inequalities persist.
