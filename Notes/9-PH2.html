Force and Laws of Motion High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Newton's laws are foundational to all of mechanics. Direct application in daily life and numerical problems are highly scored in board exams.

Introduction: Force and Motion

In the previous chapter, we studied motion and its equations. But what causes motion to change? The answer is force. Force is a push or pull that can change the state of rest or motion of an object.

Newton's three laws of motion explain the relationship between force and motion, and they form the foundation of classical mechanics.

1. Force and its Effects

A force is a push or pull on an object that produces acceleration in the body on which it acts. The SI unit of force is Newton (N).

Effects of Force

A force can do the following:

  • Change the speed of a body (make a stationary object move, or stop a moving object)
  • Change the direction of motion of a body
  • Change the shape of a body

Balanced Forces

When two or more forces acting on a body cancel each other out (resultant force = zero), they are called balanced forces. Balanced forces do not change the state of motion of an object.

📝 Example: Tug of War

In a tug of war, if both teams pull with equal force, the rope does not move. The forces are balanced.

Unbalanced Forces

When the resultant of all forces acting on a body is not zero, they are called unbalanced forces. Unbalanced forces cause a change in the state of motion — they can make a stationary object move, or change the speed or direction of a moving object.

Types of Forces

ForceDescription
Muscular ForceForce exerted by muscles in the human body
Gravitational ForceForce that pulls objects toward the Earth
Frictional ForceForce that opposes motion when two surfaces are in contact
Air ResistanceFrictional force exerted by air on a flying object

2. Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)

Statement

Newton's First Law: An object remains in a state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled to change that state by an applied unbalanced force.

This law is also known as the Law of Inertia because it describes the natural tendency of objects to resist changes in their state of motion.

What is Inertia?

Inertia is the natural tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of rest or of uniform motion. Mass is the measure of inertia — a heavier object has more inertia.

Types of Inertia

TypeDescriptionExample
Inertia of Rest Object at rest tends to remain at rest Passenger falls backward when bus starts suddenly
Inertia of Motion Object in motion tends to remain in motion Passenger falls forward when bus stops suddenly
Inertia of Direction Object resists change in direction Water flies off tangentially from a rotating tyre
⚠️ Common Misconception: A moving bicycle stops when you stop pedalling. This does NOT violate Newton's First Law. The bicycle stops because of external forces (friction and air resistance), not because it "needs" a force to keep moving.

📝 Example: Seat Belts

When a car brakes suddenly, passengers tend to fall forward due to inertia of motion. Seat belts apply a backward force to prevent this.

3. Newton's Second Law of Motion

Momentum (p)

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity of an object. It is a vector quantity — its direction is the same as the direction of velocity.

p = m × v

SI Unit: kg·m/s

Example: A cricket ball and a tennis ball moving at the same velocity. The cricket ball has more momentum because it has greater mass.

Statement of Second Law

Newton's Second Law: The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied unbalanced force and takes place in the direction of the force.

Derivation of F = ma

Consider an object of mass m with initial velocity u. A constant force F acts for time t, changing its velocity to v.

Initial momentum: p₁ = mu
Final momentum: p₂ = mv
Change in momentum: Δp = mv − mu = m(v − u)
Rate of change of momentum = m(v − u) / t
Since (v − u)/t = a (acceleration)
Rate of change of momentum = m × a
According to the law: F ∝ m × a
Therefore: F = k × m × a
In SI units, k = 1, so: F = m × a

Definition of 1 Newton

1 Newton is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² on an object of mass 1 kg.

1 N = 1 kg × 1 m/s² = 1 kg·m/s²

📝 Solved Example: Force Calculation

A force of 40 N is applied on a body and it moves with an acceleration of 5 m/s². Find the mass.

Solution: F = 40 N, a = 5 m/s²

m = F/a = 40/5 = 8 kg

📝 Solved Example: Velocity Change

A scooter of mass 80 kg increases its velocity from 5 m/s to 25 m/s in 2 seconds. Find the force required.

Solution: a = (v − u)/t = (25 − 5)/2 = 10 m/s²

F = ma = 80 × 10 = 800 N

Applications of Second Law

  • Catching a cricket ball: A fielder pulls hands backward to increase the time of impact, reducing the force.
  • High jump: Athletes land on cushioned beds to increase time and reduce force.
  • Seat belts: Increase the time of impact during accidents.

4. Law of Conservation of Momentum

Statement

Law of Conservation of Momentum: In the absence of an external unbalanced force, the total momentum of a system remains constant.

Formula for Two Bodies

If two objects of masses m₁ and m₂ collide, with initial velocities u₁ and u₂ and final velocities v₁ and v₂:

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Derivation from Newton's Laws

Consider two objects A and B with masses m₁ and m₂ moving with velocities u₁ and u₂. They collide and their velocities change to v₁ and v₂.

Force on A due to B: F_AB = m₁(v₁ − u₁)/t
Force on B due to A: F_BA = m₂(v₂ − u₂)/t
According to Newton's Third Law: F_AB = −F_BA
m₁(v₁ − u₁)/t = −m₂(v₂ − u₂)/t
m₁v₁ − m₁u₁ = −m₂v₂ + m₂u₂
Rearranging: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Applications

  • Recoil of a gun: When a bullet is fired forward, the gun recoils backward to conserve momentum.
  • Rocket propulsion: Gases are ejected backward, pushing the rocket forward.
  • Collisions: Total momentum before and after collision remains the same.

📝 Example: Recoil of Gun

A bullet of mass 0.01 kg is fired with velocity 400 m/s from a gun of mass 4 kg. Find the recoil velocity.

Solution: Initial momentum = 0

Final momentum: 0.01 × 400 + 4 × v = 0

4v = −4 → v = −1 m/s (recoil velocity)

5. Newton's Third Law of Motion

Statement

Newton's Third Law: To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. The action and reaction forces act on two different bodies.

Key Points

  • Action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude
  • They act in opposite directions
  • They act on different bodies (this is why they do NOT cancel each other)
  • They occur simultaneously — it is wrong to think action happens first, then reaction

Action-Reaction Pairs

ActionReaction
Foot pushes ground backwardGround pushes foot forward
Paddle pushes water backwardWater pushes paddle forward
Rocket pushes gases downwardGases push rocket upward
Gun pushes bullet forwardBullet pushes gun backward (recoil)

Why Walking is Possible

When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backward. The ground exerts an equal and opposite forward force on you, enabling you to move forward.

Why Action and Reaction Do Not Cancel

⚠️ Important: Action and reaction forces act on different bodies. Since they don't act on the same body, they cannot cancel each other's effects.

📝 Example: Balloon Motion

When air rushes out of a balloon (action), the balloon moves in the opposite direction (reaction).

Most Repeated Previous Year Questions

Short Answer (1-2 Marks)

  1. Why do we fall forward when a moving bus applies brakes suddenly? (2 Marks) Most Imp
  2. State the law of conservation of momentum. (2 Marks)
  3. Define 1 Newton of force. (1 Mark)
  4. What is inertia? On what factor does it depend? (2 Marks)

Numericals (3 Marks)

  1. Derive the mathematical formulation of Newton's second law of motion. (3 Marks) Most Imp
  2. A force of 100 N acts on a body of mass 20 kg. Calculate the acceleration produced.
  3. A bullet of mass 10 g is fired from a gun of mass 6 kg with a velocity of 300 m/s. Calculate the recoil velocity of the gun.

Long Answer (5 Marks)

  1. State and explain Newton's three laws of motion with examples.
  2. Derive the law of conservation of momentum from Newton's third law.

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Takeaways:
  • Force = push or pull; SI unit: Newton (N)
  • Balanced forces = zero resultant; no change in motion
  • Unbalanced forces = non-zero resultant; causes acceleration
  • Newton's First Law (Inertia): Object resists change in state of motion; mass = measure of inertia
  • Newton's Second Law: F = ma; also F = m(v−u)/t
  • Momentum: p = mv; SI unit: kg·m/s
  • Newton's Third Law: Action = Reaction; act on different bodies
  • Conservation of Momentum: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
  • 1 Newton = 1 kg × 1 m/s²
