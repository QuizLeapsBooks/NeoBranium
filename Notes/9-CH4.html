Structure of the Atom High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Lays the foundation for chemical bonding and periodicity. Understanding subatomic particles, Rutherford's nuclear model, and Bohr's energy levels is critical for scoring well in board exams.

1. Discovery of Subatomic Particles

John Dalton considered the atom to be indivisible, but this concept was discarded at the end of the 19th century when scientists discovered charged (electrons and protons) and neutral (neutrons) particles inside the atom. These are called subatomic particles [citation:2].

1.1 Discovery of Electrons (1897)

Discoverer: J.J. Thomson, using cathode ray tube experiments. He passed electricity through gases at low pressure and observed a stream of particles moving from the negative electrode (cathode) to the positive electrode (anode). These particles were negatively charged and were named electrons [citation:12].

PropertyValue
Charge on electron−1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (calculated by Robert E. Millikan) [citation:2]
Mass of electron9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg [citation:2]
Relative charge−1

1.2 Discovery of Protons (1886)

Discoverer: E. Goldstein, using anode rays (also called canal rays). These are positively charged particles moving in the opposite direction of cathode rays. They are called canal rays because they pass through holes (canals) in the cathode [citation:11].

PropertyValue
Charge on proton+1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C [citation:2]
Mass of proton1.673 × 10⁻²⁴ g ≈ 1840 × mass of electron [citation:2]
Relative charge+1

1.3 Discovery of Neutrons (1932)

Discoverer: J. Chadwick. He bombarded lighter elements (like lithium, boron) with α-particles and observed emission of new particles having zero charge but mass equal to that of a proton [citation:2].

  • Neutrons are neutral particles (no charge) [citation:12].
  • Neutrons are absent in Protium isotope of hydrogen (¹H₁) [citation:2].
  • Mass of neutron ≈ mass of proton [citation:2].
🎯 Quick Fact: In a neutral atom, Number of protons = Number of electrons. The mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus (protons + neutrons) [citation:2].

2. Atomic Models

Various atomic models were proposed by different scientists to explain the arrangement of subatomic particles inside the atom [citation:2].

2.1 Thomson's Atomic Model (1904) — "Plum Pudding Model"

Thomson proposed that an atom is a positively charged sphere with electrons embedded inside it, just like seeds in a watermelon or plums in a pudding [citation:2][citation:8].

Key Features:

  • The atom consists of a sphere of positive charge [citation:8].
  • Electrons are embedded in this positive sphere [citation:8].
  • The total positive charge balances the total negative charge, making the atom electrically neutral [citation:8].
⚠️ Drawbacks of Thomson's Model:
  • Could not explain the results of Rutherford's α-particle scattering experiment [citation:8].
  • Lacked experimental evidence [citation:8].
  • Failed to explain atomic stability [citation:8].

2.2 Rutherford's Atomic Model (1911) — "Nuclear Model"

Experiment: Rutherford bombarded a thin gold foil with fast-moving α-particles (Helium nuclei, He²⁺) [citation:2].

Observations:

  • Most α-particles passed straight through the gold foil undeflected [citation:2].
  • Some α-particles were deflected by small angles [citation:2].
  • One out of every 12,000 particles rebounded completely [citation:2].

Conclusions:

  • The atom consists predominantly of empty space (because most α-particles passed through) [citation:2].
  • There is a centrally placed positively charged nucleus (because few particles deflected and very few bounced back) [citation:2].
  • The size of the nucleus is about 10⁻⁵ times the size of the atom [citation:2].
  • Whole of the atomic mass is concentrated in the nucleus [citation:2].

Features of Rutherford's Model:

  • There is a positively charged nucleus in an atom. Nearly all the mass resides in the nucleus (protons + neutrons) [citation:2].
  • Electrons revolve round the nucleus in well-defined orbits [citation:2].
  • The size of the nucleus is very small compared to the size of the atom [citation:2].
⚠️ Drawback of Rutherford's Model (Unstability of Atom):

According to Rutherford, electrons revolve round the nucleus in well-defined orbits. But electrons being charged particles will lose their energy and finally fall into the nucleus. This will make the atom highly unstable. This was the major drawback of Rutherford which was unexplained by him [citation:2].

2.3 Bohr's Model of the Atom (1913)

To overcome the drawbacks of Rutherford's Model, Neil Bohr in 1912 proposed a modified model of the structure of the atom [citation:2].

Postulates:

  • Only certain special orbits known as discrete orbits of electrons are allowed inside the atom [citation:17].
  • While revolving in discrete orbits, the electrons do not radiate energy [citation:17].
  • These orbits or shells are called energy levels [citation:17].
  • These orbits or shells are represented by the letters K, L, M, N, or the numbers n = 1, 2, 3, 4 [citation:17].
🎯 Key Point: Bohr's model successfully explained the stability of the hydrogen atom and the discrete nature of atomic spectra [citation:12].

3. Electronic Configuration & Valency

3.1 Bohr-Bury Scheme (Rules for Electron Distribution)

The arrangement of electrons in different shells of an atom follows simple rules proposed by Bohr and Bury [citation:8][citation:14]:

Rule 1: Maximum electrons in a shell

Maximum number of electrons in a shell = 2n²
where n = shell number (1, 2, 3, ...)
ShellnMaximum Electrons = 2n²
K12 × 1² = 2
L22 × 2² = 8
M32 × 3² = 18
N42 × 4² = 32

Rule 2: Outermost shell limit

The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in the outermost orbit is 8 [citation:4][citation:14].

Rule 3: Step-wise filling

Electrons are not accommodated in a given shell unless the inner shells are filled. That is, the shells are filled in a step-wise manner [citation:4][citation:14].

3.3 Valency

Definition: Valency is the combining capacity of an atom — the number of electrons an atom loses, gains, or shares to achieve a fully-filled outermost shell [citation:12][citation:17].

Rules for Determining Valency:

  • If the outermost shell has ≤ 4 electrons: Valency = Number of valence electrons [citation:12].
  • If the outermost shell has > 4 electrons: Valency = 8 − Number of valence electrons [citation:12].
  • Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) have completely filled outermost shells, so their valency is 0 [citation:4].

Examples:

  • Hydrogen (1 electron) → Valency = 1
  • Oxygen (6 electrons) → Valency = 8 − 6 = 2
  • Sodium (1 electron) → Valency = 1

4. Isotopes and Isobars

4.1 Isotopes

Definition: Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers (due to different number of neutrons) [citation:10][citation:15].

Example: Carbon exists as ¹²C and ¹⁴C [citation:15].

Example: Chlorine occurs in nature in two isotopic forms, with masses 35 u and 37 u in the ratio 3:1. The average atomic mass of chlorine is 35.5 u [citation:5].

Properties:

  • Chemical properties of isotopes are similar (because chemical reactions depend on electrons) [citation:5].
  • Physical properties may differ (because masses differ) [citation:5].

Applications of Isotopes:

  • An isotope of uranium is used as a fuel in nuclear reactors [citation:5][citation:10].
  • An isotope of cobalt is used in the treatment of cancer [citation:5][citation:10].
  • An isotope of iodine is used in the treatment of goitre (thyroid disease) [citation:5][citation:10].

4.2 Isobars

Definition: Atoms of different elements having the same mass number but different atomic numbers [citation:10][citation:15].

Example: Calcium (⁴⁰Ca, Z = 20) and Argon (⁴⁰Ar, Z = 18) are isobars because both have mass number 40 [citation:15].

🎯 Quick Difference:
Isotopes: Same Z, Different A (same element).
Isobars: Same A, Different Z (different elements).

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Formulas & Facts:
  • Atomic Number (Z) = Number of Protons = Number of Electrons (in neutral atom) [citation:7].
  • Mass Number (A) = Number of Protons + Number of Neutrons [citation:7].
  • Number of Neutrons = Mass Number (A) − Atomic Number (Z).
  • Maximum electrons in a shell = 2n² [citation:4].
  • Valency of Noble Gases = 0.
  • Isotopes: Same Z, different A. Isobars: Same A, different Z.
  • Electron charge: −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; Mass: 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg [citation:2].
  • Proton mass ≈ 1840 × electron mass [citation:2].
