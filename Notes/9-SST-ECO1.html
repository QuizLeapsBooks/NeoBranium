The Story of Village Palampur High Weightage (Periodic Test)

Why this chapter is important? This chapter introduces the foundational concepts of production, the four factors of production, and the impact of the Green Revolution. It is the base for understanding the Indian rural economy. Questions on factors of production and land distribution are frequently asked in periodic assessments [citation:3].

Introduction to Palampur

Palampur is a hypothetical village used in the NCERT textbook to explain the basic concepts of production and rural economy. It is a small village with about 450 families belonging to different castes [citation:1][citation:2].

Infrastructure and Facilities

Palampur is fairly developed compared to other Indian villages:

  • It is well-connected by an all-weather road to Raiganj and Shahpur (the nearest town) [citation:1].
  • Most houses have electricity, which powers tube wells for irrigation [citation:1].
  • There are two primary schools and one high school [citation:1].
  • It has a government-run primary health centre and a private dispensary [citation:1].

Farming is the main production activity, but non-farm activities like dairy, small-scale manufacturing, and transport also exist on a limited scale [citation:1][citation:7].

1. Organisation of Production

The aim of production is to produce goods and services. There are four essential requirements, known as the Factors of Production [citation:1][citation:7]:

FactorDescription
LandIncludes all natural resources like water, forests, and minerals. It is a passive factor.
LabourPeople who do the work. It includes both manual and skilled workers.
Physical CapitalInputs required at every stage of production. It is of two types: Fixed Capital (tools, machines, buildings) and Working Capital (raw materials, money in hand).
Human CapitalThe knowledge and enterprise needed to combine land, labour, and capital to produce output.
🎯 Key Point: The four factors of production are Land, Labour, Physical Capital, and Human Capital. Fixed capital (like a tractor) is used over many years, while working capital (like seeds) is used up during production [citation:2].

2. Farming in Palampur

Land is Fixed

Farming is the main activity, supporting 75% of the working population [citation:1][citation:2]. However, the land area under cultivation is practically fixed. Since 1960, there has been no expansion of cultivable land. There is no scope to increase production by bringing new land under cultivation [citation:1].

Multiple Cropping

To increase production from the same land, farmers practice multiple cropping, which means growing more than one crop on a piece of land during the year [citation:2]. This is possible due to a well-developed irrigation system.

  • Kharif Season (Rainy): Farmers grow jowar and bajra [citation:1].
  • Between October and December: They grow potatoes [citation:1].
  • Rabi Season (Winter): Fields are sown with wheat [citation:1].
  • A part of the land is also devoted to sugarcane, which is harvested once a year [citation:1].

📝 Example: Why Multiple Cropping Works

Palampur farmers can grow three crops a year primarily because of electricity-powered tube wells that provide reliable irrigation, unlike the old Persian wheels [citation:1][citation:4].

3. Modern Farming and the Green Revolution

Another way to increase production is through modern farming methods, which were introduced during the Green Revolution in the late 1960s [citation:2][citation:4].

Key Features of Modern Farming

  • HYV Seeds: High Yielding Variety seeds were introduced for wheat and rice. These produce more grain on the same land [citation:2].
  • Inputs: HYV seeds require plenty of water, chemical fertilizers, and pesticides to give best results [citation:2].
  • Machinery: Use of tractors and tube wells increased [citation:4].
⚠️ Important: While modern farming increased yields (e.g., wheat yield increased from 1300 kg/ha to 3200 kg/ha in Palampur), it also increased the cost of cultivation because farmers had to buy expensive seeds, fertilizers, and pesticides [citation:2].

4. Sustainability of Land (Environmental Impact)

Modern farming methods have overused the natural resource base, leading to serious environmental concerns [citation:1][citation:2]:

  • Loss of Soil Fertility: Increased use of chemical fertilizers has degraded the soil [citation:1].
  • Depletion of Water Table: Continuous use of groundwater for tube well irrigation has led to a decline in the water table [citation:1][citation:2].
  • Water Pollution: Chemical fertilizers can leach into water sources and pollute them [citation:1].

Scientists warn that if this continues, the land will not be able to sustain production in the long run.

5. Land Distribution and Labour

Unequal Distribution of Land

Land is distributed very unequally in Palampur among the 450 families [citation:1][citation:2]:

CategoryNumber of FamiliesLand Holding
Landless~150 families (mostly Dalits)No land
Small Farmers~240 familiesLess than 2 hectares
Medium & Large Farmers~60 familiesMore than 2 hectares (some have over 10 hectares)

Labour

  • Small farmers cultivate their own fields using family labour [citation:1].
  • Medium and large farmers hire farm labourers [citation:1].
  • Farm labourers come from landless families and are paid wages (in cash or kind). Wages are often lower than the minimum wage set by the government due to heavy competition for work [citation:7].

6. Capital and Sale of Surplus

Capital for Farming

  • Small farmers often borrow money from large farmers, moneylenders, or traders at very high interest rates [citation:1][citation:2].
  • Medium and large farmers usually have their own savings from farming to arrange capital [citation:1].

Sale of Surplus Farm Products

Farmers keep a part of the produce for family consumption and sell the surplus in the market [citation:1].

  • Small farmers have little surplus.
  • Medium and large farmers have substantial surplus and sell it to traders, who then sell it to shopkeepers in towns and cities [citation:1].
  • Earnings from surplus are used for savings, buying more equipment (like tractors), or lending to other farmers [citation:1].

7. Non-Farming Activities in Palampur

Only 25% of the working population is engaged in non-farm activities [citation:2]. These include:

  • Dairy: People rear buffaloes and sell milk in nearby villages and towns [citation:1][citation:4].
  • Small-scale Manufacturing: Very simple production methods, often done at home with family labour (e.g., making jaggery) [citation:2][citation:4].
  • Shopkeeping: Traders buy goods from wholesale markets in cities and sell them in the village [citation:4].
  • Transport: A fast-developing sector. People drive rickshaws, tongas, jeeps, and trucks to transport goods and people for a fee [citation:4].

Most Repeated Previous Year Questions

Short Answer (1-3 Marks)

  1. What is the main economic activity in Palampur? Most Imp
  2. Name the four factors of production. (1 Mark)
  3. What is multiple cropping? (2 Marks)
  4. Why is land said to be a fixed factor in Palampur? (2 Marks)
  5. What are the two types of physical capital? Give examples. (2-3 Marks) Most Imp
  6. How did the spread of electricity help farmers in Palampur? (3 Marks)
  7. What is the Green Revolution? (1-2 Marks)
  8. Mention any two environmental problems caused by modern farming. (2 Marks)

Long Answer (5 Marks)

  1. Explain the distribution of land among the farmers of Palampur. Most Imp
  2. Distinguish between traditional farming and modern farming methods. Most Imp
  3. Describe any four non-farm activities practiced in Palampur.

Quick Revision - At a Glance

  • Palampur is a hypothetical village with 450 families.
  • Farming is the main activity; 75% of people depend on it.
  • Four factors of production: Land, Labour, Physical Capital, Human Capital.
  • Multiple cropping: Growing 2-3 crops a year (jowar/bajra, potato, wheat).
  • Green Revolution (1960s) introduced HYV seeds but caused soil/water degradation.
  • Land distribution is unequal: 150 landless, 240 small, 60 medium/large farmers.
  • Non-farm activities: Dairy, manufacturing, shopkeeping, transport.
