Sound High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Sound characteristics (frequency, amplitude, pitch, loudness) are crucial concepts. Echo numericals are board favorites. SONAR and human ear are frequently asked in 3-5 mark questions.

1. Production and Propagation of Sound

Sound is a form of energy that produces the sensation of hearing. It is produced by vibrating objects. When an object vibrates, it sets the neighbouring particles of the medium into motion, creating a disturbance that travels through the medium [citation:5].

Sound is a mechanical wave and needs a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to propagate. It cannot travel through vacuum because there are no particles to carry the vibrations [citation:1].

🧪 Classic Experiment — Bell Jar: An electric bell is suspended inside an airtight glass jar connected to a vacuum pump. As air is gradually removed, the sound becomes fainter and eventually becomes inaudible, even though the hammer keeps striking. This proves sound needs a medium to travel [citation:2][citation:12].

How Sound Propagates: Compressions & Rarefactions

When a vibrating object (like a tuning fork) moves forward, it pushes the air molecules ahead, creating a region of high pressure called a Compression (C). When it moves backward, it creates a region of low pressure called a Rarefaction (R) [citation:5][citation:12].

As the object vibrates back and forth, a continuous series of compressions and rarefactions travels through the medium. This is how sound waves propagate. The particles of the medium do not move from one place to another—they simply oscillate back and forth about their mean positions, passing the disturbance forward.

📌 Key Point: Sound waves are longitudinal waves because the particles of the medium vibrate in the same direction as the wave propagates [citation:5][citation:6].

2. Characteristics of Sound Wave

There are four main characteristics of a sound wave:

Frequency (ν)

The number of oscillations (vibrations) completed per second is called frequency. Its SI unit is Hertz (Hz). One hertz equals one vibration per second [citation:2][citation:13].

Frequency determines the pitch of the sound. Higher frequency = higher pitch (shrill sound). Lower frequency = lower pitch (deep sound).

Amplitude (A)

The maximum displacement of a particle from its mean position is called amplitude. Its unit is meter (m) [citation:2].

Amplitude determines the loudness of the sound. Greater amplitude = louder sound. Smaller amplitude = fainter sound [citation:3][citation:10].

Time Period (T)

The time taken by two consecutive compressions or rarefactions to cross a fixed point is called the time period. It is the reciprocal of frequency.

Wavelength (λ)

The distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions is called wavelength. It is denoted by the Greek letter lambda (λ). Unit: meter (m).

Speed of Sound (v) = Wavelength (λ) × Frequency (ν)   |   Time Period (T) = 1 / Frequency
🎯 Exam Tip — Loudness vs Pitch:
• Loudness depends on Amplitude (measured in decibels).
• Pitch depends on Frequency (measured in Hz).
• Quality/Timbre helps you distinguish between sounds of the same pitch and loudness (e.g., identifying a friend's voice in a dark room) [citation:3][citation:5].

3. Speed of Sound in Different Media

The speed of sound depends on the elasticity and density of the medium, as well as its temperature [citation:2].

📊 Speed Order: Solids > Liquids > Gases
Reason: In solids, particles are tightly packed, so vibrations transfer very quickly. In gases, particles are far apart, making sound travel the slowest [citation:8].

Effect of Temperature: As temperature increases, the speed of sound in a medium also increases. This is why sound travels faster on a hot summer day than on a cold winter day [citation:6][citation:11].

4. Reflection of Sound and Echo

Sound bounces off solid or liquid surfaces just like light. This phenomenon is called reflection of sound [citation:2].

Laws of Reflection of Sound

Sound follows the same two laws as light [citation:7][citation:9]:

  1. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
  2. The incident sound wave, the reflected sound wave, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.

Echo

An echo is the sound heard after reflection from a distant rigid obstacle (like a cliff or a large wall) [citation:13].

Condition for hearing a distinct echo: The time interval between the original sound and the reflected sound must be at least 0.1 seconds [citation:6].

Since speed of sound in air is approximately 344 m/s, the minimum distance required for an echo is:

Distance = Speed × Time / 2 = (344 × 0.1) / 2 = 17.2 meters

So, to hear an echo, the reflecting surface must be at least 17.2 meters away from the source [citation:6].

🔥 Board Favorite Numericals: A person claps near a cliff and hears the echo after 2 seconds. Distance of cliff = (346 × 2) / 2 = 346 meters.

5. Reverberation

Reverberation is the persistence of sound due to repeated reflections within a closed space, even after the source has stopped producing sound [citation:4].

For example, in an empty room with hard walls, sound keeps bouncing back and forth, making it difficult to hear distinctly.

How to reduce reverberation: By covering walls and ceilings with sound-absorbing materials like fiber boards, curtains, carpets, and acoustic panels [citation:3].

Practical Applications of Multiple Reflection

  • Megaphone / Loudhailer: A funnel-shaped tube prevents sound from spreading in all directions, sending it in a specific direction [citation:3].
  • Stethoscope: Uses multiple reflections of sound to transmit body sounds (like heartbeat) to the doctor's ears [citation:3][citation:7].
  • Curved ceilings of concert halls: Sound waves reflect off curved surfaces and spread uniformly to all corners of the hall [citation:5][citation:6].

6. Range of Hearing

The average human ear can hear sounds with frequencies between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). This is called the audible range [citation:3][citation:5].

🔊 Frequency Ranges:
• Infrasound: Less than 20 Hz. Produced by earthquakes, volcanoes. Some animals like rhinoceroses and whales use infrasound to communicate [citation:10][citation:13].
• Audible range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Humans can hear these.
• Ultrasound: More than 20,000 Hz. Bats, dolphins, and moths use ultrasound for navigation and communication [citation:6][citation:13].

Why can't we hear a pendulum's vibrations? A simple pendulum oscillates at a frequency less than 20 Hz, which is below the audible range, so we cannot hear it [citation:9].

7. Applications of Ultrasound and SONAR

Medical and Industrial Uses of Ultrasound

  • Echocardiography: Ultrasound reflects from various parts of the heart to form an image of the heart [citation:6].
  • Ultrasonography: Used to examine organs, detect abnormalities, and monitor foetal development during pregnancy [citation:6].
  • Industrial flaw detection: Ultrasound is used to detect cracks and flaws in metal blocks without damaging them [citation:6].

SONAR — Sound Navigation and Ranging

SONAR is a technique that uses ultrasonic waves to measure the depth of the sea, locate underwater objects like submarines, icebergs, and sunken ships [citation:6][citation:15].

Working Principle: A transmitter on a ship emits ultrasonic pulses into the water. These pulses reflect off the seabed or objects and return to a detector. The time interval between emission and reception is recorded.

Depth (d) = (Speed of Sound in Water × Time) / 2

The factor of 2 is used because the sound travels to the object and back [citation:5].

8. Structure of the Human Ear

The human ear is a remarkable organ that converts sound wave pressure variations into electrical signals that the brain interprets as sound [citation:8].

Parts and Functions:

  • Outer Ear (Pinna): Collects sound waves and directs them into the ear canal.
  • Ear Canal: Carries sound waves to the eardrum.
  • Eardrum (Tympanic Membrane): Vibrates when sound waves hit it. These vibrations are passed to the middle ear.
  • Middle Ear (Three tiny bones — Hammer, Anvil, Stirrup): Amplifies the vibrations and transmits them to the inner ear [citation:8].
  • Inner Ear (Cochlea): Contains fluid and hair cells. Sound vibrations create pressure waves in the fluid, which stimulate hair cells to generate electrical signals.
  • Auditory Nerve: Carries electrical signals from the cochlea to the brain, which interprets them as sound.

Most Repeated Previous Year Questions

  1. Explain how sound is produced by your school bell. (2 Marks) [Answer: The bell vibrates when struck, causing compressions and rarefactions in the surrounding air, producing sound.]
  2. A person clapped his hands near a cliff and heard the echo after 2 s. What is the distance of the cliff? (Speed of sound = 346 m/s) (3 Marks) [Answer: Distance = (346 × 2) / 2 = 346 m]
  3. What is the audible range of the average human ear? (1 Mark) [Answer: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz]
  4. Why are sound waves called mechanical waves? (2 Marks) [Answer: Because they require a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) for propagation and cannot travel through vacuum.]
  5. Give two practical applications of reflection of sound waves. (2 Marks) [Answer: (i) Megaphone, (ii) Stethoscope. Also SONAR and curved concert hall ceilings.]
  6. Distinguish between loudness and intensity of sound. (2 Marks) [Answer: Loudness is the ear's perception (measured in decibels), while intensity is a physical quantity (energy per unit area per second).]
  7. What is reverberation? How can it be reduced? (2 Marks) [Answer: Persistence of sound due to repeated reflections. Reduced by using sound-absorbing materials like curtains, carpets, and acoustic panels.]
  8. A stone is dropped from the top of a tower 500 m high into a pond. When is the splash heard at the top? (g = 10 m/s², speed of sound = 340 m/s) (5 Marks) [Answer: Time for stone to fall = 10 s, Time for sound to travel back = 500/340 ≈ 1.47 s, Total ≈ 11.47 s]

Quick Revision — At a Glance

🎯 Key Tips:
  • Sound is produced by vibrations and travels as longitudinal waves.
  • Speed of sound: Solid > Liquid > Gas.
  • Pitch depends on frequency; Loudness depends on amplitude; Quality/Timbre helps identify voices.
  • Audible range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Ultrasound: >20,000 Hz. Infrasound: <20 Hz.
  • Minimum distance for echo = 17.2 m (at 25°C in air).
  • SONAR uses ultrasound for underwater navigation and depth measurement.
  • Human ear: Pinna → Ear canal → Eardrum → Middle ear bones → Cochlea → Auditory nerve → Brain.
