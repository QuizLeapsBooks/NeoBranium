Food Security in India High Weightage (Periodic Test)

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains one of the most critical aspects of India's economy—ensuring food for all. It covers the dimensions of food security, the role of the Green Revolution, buffer stock, the Public Distribution System (PDS), and various government schemes. Questions on PDS, buffer stock, and hunger types are frequently asked in exams [citation:1][citation:2].

What is Food Security?

Food security means that all people, at all times, have access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food to meet their basic needs for a healthy life. It is not just about having food available in the country, but also about whether people can afford it and actually get it [citation:1].

🎯 Key Point: Food security is ensured in a country only if (1) enough food is available for all persons, (2) all persons have the capacity to buy food of acceptable quality, and (3) there is no barrier on access to food [citation:5][citation:12].

1. Dimensions of Food Security

Food security has three essential dimensions. All three are important—if even one is missing, food security cannot be achieved [citation:1][citation:2].

DimensionMeaning
Availability of FoodEnough food should be produced or stored in the country. This includes food grown on farms, food stored in government granaries, and food brought in from other places if needed [citation:1].
Accessibility of FoodFood should reach all people, especially those who need it the most. Good transport systems, storage facilities, and fair distribution are necessary so that food can reach remote areas and poor sections of society [citation:1].
Affordability of FoodPeople should have enough money to buy food. If food prices are too high, poor families may not be able to purchase sufficient food even if it is available in the market [citation:1].

2. Why is Food Security Needed?

Food security is essential because food is a basic need for survival, and not everyone gets enough food all the time [citation:1][citation:5].

  • India has a very large population, so it is important to ensure enough food is available for everyone.
  • Natural disasters like floods and droughts can destroy crops and cause food shortages in some areas. Food security allows food grains to be stored and supplied to these areas when needed [citation:1].
  • Poor and low-income families are the most affected when food becomes scarce or expensive. Food security helps ensure they can get basic food items [citation:1].
  • Even persons above the poverty line might become food insecure when the country faces a national disaster or calamity [citation:5].

📝 What Happens During a Calamity?

When a natural calamity like drought occurs, production of food grains decreases. This creates a shortage of food, and prices go up. At high prices, some people cannot afford to buy food. If the calamity happens over a wide area or for a long period, it may cause starvation, which can turn into a famine [citation:5].

⚠️ Important: The most devastating famine in India was the Bengal Famine of 1943, which killed about 30 lakh people [citation:3][citation:5][citation:8].

3. Who are Food Insecure?

A large section of people in India suffers from food and nutrition insecurity. The worst affected groups are [citation:2][citation:5][citation:12]:

AreaFood Insecure Groups
Rural AreasLandless people with little or no land, traditional artisans, providers of traditional services, petty self-employed workers, and destitutes including beggars [citation:2][citation:5].
Urban AreasFamilies whose working members are employed in ill-paid occupations and casual labour markets. They are largely engaged in seasonal activities and are paid very low wages [citation:5].
Social GroupsSCs, STs, and some sections of OBCs who have either poor land base or very low productivity are prone to food insecurity [citation:2][citation:5].
Other Vulnerable GroupsPeople affected by natural disasters who migrate in search of work, pregnant and nursing mothers, and children under the age of 5 years [citation:2][citation:5].
🎯 Key Data: The states of Uttar Pradesh (Eastern and Southern parts), Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, and Maharashtra account for the largest number of food insecure people in the country [citation:2][citation:5].

4. Types of Hunger

Hunger is another aspect indicating food insecurity. It has two dimensions [citation:2][citation:5][citation:12]:

TypeDescriptionExample
Chronic HungerA consequence of diets persistently inadequate in terms of quantity and/or quality. Poor people suffer from chronic hunger because of very low income and inability to buy food even for survival [citation:2][citation:5].Poor families who consistently cannot afford nutritious food.
Seasonal HungerRelated to cycles of food growing and harvesting. Prevalent in rural areas because of the seasonal nature of agricultural activities and in urban areas because of casual labour [citation:2][citation:5].There is less work for casual construction labour during the rainy season [citation:2].

5. Green Revolution and Self-Sufficiency

After independence, Indian policymakers adopted all measures to achieve self-sufficiency in food grains. India adopted a new strategy in agriculture which resulted in the Green Revolution, especially in the production of wheat and rice [citation:5].

Impact of the Green Revolution

  • Introduced High Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds, combined with chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and improved irrigation [citation:2][citation:9].
  • India became self-sufficient in food grains and has avoided famines even in adverse weather conditions [citation:2][citation:9].
  • The highest rate of growth was achieved in Punjab and Haryana [citation:5].
  • In July 1968, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi officially released a special stamp entitled 'Wheat Revolution' [citation:6][citation:8].
⚠️ Important: Despite achieving self-sufficiency, a section of people in India still remain without food. Poor people suffer from chronic hunger because they cannot afford to buy food even when it is available [citation:2].

6. Buffer Stock and Minimum Support Price (MSP)

Buffer Stock

Buffer stock is the stock of food grains, namely wheat and rice, procured by the government through the Food Corporation of India (FCI) [citation:4][citation:12].

  • FCI purchases wheat and rice from farmers in states where there is surplus production.
  • The purchased food grains are stored in government granaries.
  • This stock is used to distribute food grains in deficit areas and among the poorer sections of society at a price lower than the market price [citation:4][citation:8].

Minimum Support Price (MSP)

The farmers are paid a pre-announced price for their crops. This price is called the Minimum Support Price (MSP) [citation:4][citation:12].

  • The MSP is declared by the government every year before the sowing season.
  • Its purpose is to provide incentives to farmers for raising the production of these crops [citation:4].
Buffer Stock → FCI procures at MSP → Stored in granaries → Distributed at Issue Price (lower than market price)

7. Public Distribution System (PDS)

The food procured by the FCI is distributed through government-regulated ration shops among the poorer section of society. This is called the Public Distribution System (PDS) [citation:4][citation:12].

Key Features of PDS

  • Ration shops are also known as Fair Price Shops.
  • They keep stock of food grains, sugar, and kerosene for cooking.
  • These items are sold to people at a price lower than the market price [citation:12].

Evolution of PDS

SchemeYearTarget Group
PDS (Universal)Before 1992All people, no discrimination between poor and non-poor [citation:12].
RPDS (Revamped PDS)1992Remote and backward areas (1,700 blocks) [citation:12].
TPDS (Targeted PDS)1997Poor and non-poor with differential price policy [citation:12].
AAY (Antyodaya Anna Yojana)2000Poorest of the poor [citation:12].
APS (Annapurna Scheme)2000Indigent senior citizens [citation:12].

Problems with PDS

  • Instances of hunger are prevalent despite overflowing granaries.
  • Poor quality of commodities supplied like wheat and rice (sub-standard) [citation:12].
  • Malpractices like diversion of grains to open markets [citation:3].

8. Government Schemes for Food Security

The government has launched several poverty alleviation programmes with a food security component [citation:2][citation:4].

SchemeKey Features
Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY)Launched in December 2000. Targets the poorest families among the Below Poverty Line (BPL) population. Provides highly subsidized food grains [citation:2][citation:9].
Annapurna Scheme (APS)Launched in 2000. Targets indigent senior citizens (aged 65 and above) who are not receiving old-age pensions. Provides 10 kg of food grains per month free of cost [citation:9].
Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS)Introduced in 1975. Aims to improve the nutritional status of children below the age of six [citation:3][citation:4].
Food-for-Work (FFW)Launched in 1977-78. Provides employment in exchange for food [citation:4][citation:11].
Mid-Day Meal SchemeProvides nutritious meals to school children [citation:2].

9. Role of Cooperatives in Food Security

Cooperative societies play an important role in ensuring food security by setting up shops to sell low-priced goods to poor people [citation:12].

  • In Tamil Nadu, around 94% of fair price shops are run by cooperatives [citation:12].
  • In Maharashtra, the Academy of Development Science (ADS) has facilitated a network of NGOs for setting up grain banks in different regions [citation:12].
  • Amul and Mother Dairy are examples of cooperatives that provide milk and vegetables at controlled rates [citation:3].
