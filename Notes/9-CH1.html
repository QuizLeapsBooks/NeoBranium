Matter in Our Surroundings High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Understanding the physical nature of matter, its states, and the effect of temperature and pressure is the starting point for chemistry. Questions on states of matter, evaporation, and interconversion are frequently asked in board exams.

1. Physical Nature of Matter

Everything around us — air, water, food, stones, clouds, stars, plants, and animals — is matter. Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space (volume).

Characteristics of Particles of Matter

  • Particles are very small: They are so tiny that they cannot be seen with the naked eye. Example: A drop of ink spreads in water because its particles are extremely small.
  • Particles have space between them: This is why sugar dissolves in water without increasing the water level significantly.
  • Particles are continuously moving: They possess kinetic energy. This is why the smell of incense spreads across a room.
  • Particles attract each other: The force of attraction varies — strongest in solids, weakest in gases.
📌 Diffusion: The intermixing of particles of two different types of matter on their own is called diffusion. The rate of diffusion increases with temperature because particles gain kinetic energy.

Evidence of Particles of Matter

  • Brownian Motion: The zig-zag movement of particles in a fluid observed under a microscope.
  • Diffusion: A drop of perfume spreads in the entire room.
  • Dissolution: Salt or sugar dissolves in water, occupying the spaces between water particles.

2. States of Matter

Matter exists in three main physical states: Solid, Liquid, and Gas. These differ in the arrangement of particles and the forces of attraction between them.

A. Solid State

  • Have a definite shape and definite volume.
  • Particles are tightly packed — highest force of attraction.
  • Very low compressibility.
  • Particles only vibrate about their mean position.
  • Examples: Ice, Wood, Iron, Salt.

B. Liquid State

  • Have a definite volume but no definite shape — takes the shape of the container.
  • Particles are loosely packed compared to solids.
  • Moderately compressible.
  • Particles can flow and slide over each other.
  • Examples: Water, Milk, Oil, Alcohol.

C. Gaseous State

  • Have neither definite shape nor definite volume.
  • Particles are very loosely packed — weakest force of attraction.
  • Highly compressible.
  • Particles move randomly at very high speeds.
  • Examples: Air, Oxygen, Carbon dioxide, Hydrogen.
Solid > Liquid > Gas (Force of Attraction)
Gas > Liquid > Solid (Kinetic Energy of Particles)
🔑 Key Comparison Table (Exam Favourite):
  • Shape: Solid (Fixed) | Liquid (Not Fixed) | Gas (Not Fixed)
  • Volume: Solid (Fixed) | Liquid (Fixed) | Gas (Not Fixed)
  • Compressibility: Solid (Low) | Liquid (Moderate) | Gas (High)
  • Diffusion: Solid (Slowest) | Liquid (Moderate) | Gas (Fastest)

Why Do Solids Have Fixed Shape?

Because the particles in solids are very closely packed and have strong forces of attraction holding them in fixed positions — so they can only vibrate, not move freely.

3. Change of State

Matter can change from one state to another by changing temperature or pressure. This is called interconversion of states of matter.

Terms to Know

  • Melting (Fusion): Solid → Liquid. Example: Ice → Water.
  • Boiling: Liquid → Gas. Example: Water → Steam.
  • Condensation (Liquefaction): Gas → Liquid. Example: Steam → Water.
  • Freezing (Solidification): Liquid → Solid. Example: Water → Ice.
  • Sublimation: Solid → Gas directly. Example: Camphor, Naphthalene, Dry ice.
  • Deposition: Gas → Solid directly. Example: Frost formation.

Melting Point

The temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid at atmospheric pressure is called its melting point.

  • Melting point of ice = 273.15 K (0°C).
  • During melting, temperature remains constant — heat is absorbed but used to break the forces of attraction (Latent Heat of Fusion).

Boiling Point

The temperature at which a liquid starts boiling at atmospheric pressure is called its boiling point.

  • Boiling point of water = 373 K (100°C).
  • During boiling, temperature remains constant — heat is absorbed to convert liquid into vapor (Latent Heat of Vaporization).
📌 Latent Heat:
  • Latent Heat of Fusion: Heat required to convert 1 kg of solid into liquid at its melting point.
  • Latent Heat of Vaporization: Heat required to convert 1 kg of liquid into vapor at its boiling point.

Effect of Pressure on Change of State

  • Increasing pressure brings particles closer, causing gases to liquefy.
  • Example: LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas) is stored in cylinders under high pressure.
  • Increasing pressure lowers the melting point of ice (ice melts faster under pressure).

Sublimation

The change of state directly from solid to gas without passing through the liquid state is called sublimation.

  • Examples: Camphor, Naphthalene balls, Dry ice (solid CO₂), Iodine.

4. Evaporation

Evaporation is the phenomenon of change of a liquid into vapors at any temperature below its boiling point, from the surface of the liquid.

Factors Affecting Evaporation

  • Surface Area: Greater surface area → faster evaporation. (Why we spread clothes to dry.)
  • Temperature: Higher temperature → faster evaporation. (Why water dries faster in summer.)
  • Humidity: Higher humidity → slower evaporation. (Why clothes dry slowly on a rainy day.)
  • Wind Speed: Higher wind speed → faster evaporation. (Why clothes dry faster on a windy day.)
❄️ Why Does Evaporation Cause Cooling?
During evaporation, the particles at the surface absorb energy (heat) from the surroundings to overcome the forces of attraction and escape into the atmosphere. This absorption of heat from the surroundings causes cooling.
  • Example 1: Our palm feels cold when we put acetone or petrol on it.
  • Example 2: Water in an earthen pot (matka) stays cool because water seeps through small pores and evaporates, causing cooling.
  • Example 3: We sweat during summer; as sweat evaporates, it cools our body.

Evaporation vs Boiling

  • Evaporation occurs at any temperature; Boiling occurs at a specific temperature (boiling point).
  • Evaporation is a surface phenomenon; Boiling is a bulk phenomenon.
  • Evaporation is slow; Boiling is fast.

Most Repeated Previous Year Questions

  1. Why does our palm feel cold when we put some acetone or petrol on it? (2 Marks) — Answer: Acetone/petrol evaporates by absorbing heat (latent heat) from our palm, causing cooling.
  2. What is latent heat of vaporization? (2 Marks) — Answer: The heat energy required to convert 1 kg of a liquid into vapor at its boiling point without any change in temperature.
  3. Convert 25°C to Kelvin scale. (1 Mark) — Answer: K = 25 + 273 = 298 K.
  4. Why is ice at 273 K more effective in cooling than water at the same temperature? (2 Marks) — Answer: Ice absorbs latent heat of fusion (3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg) when melting, giving more cooling effect than water at the same temperature.
  5. What produces more severe burns — boiling water or steam? (2 Marks) — Answer: Steam produces more severe burns because it releases latent heat of vaporization (2.25 × 10⁶ J/kg) when condensing on the skin.
  6. Define sublimation with two examples. (2 Marks) — Answer: The change of state directly from solid to gas without passing through liquid state. Examples: Camphor, Naphthalene.
  7. Name the factors affecting evaporation. (2 Marks) — Answer: Surface area, temperature, humidity, and wind speed.
  8. Arrange the three states of matter in increasing order of: (a) force of attraction (b) kinetic energy. (2 Marks) — Answer: (a) Gas < Liquid < Solid (b) Solid < Liquid < Gas.
  9. Why does a desert cooler cool better on a hot dry day? (2 Marks) — Answer: On a hot dry day, temperature is high and humidity is low, which increases the rate of evaporation, thus providing better cooling.
  10. What is the physical state of water at: (a) 25°C (b) 0°C (c) 100°C? (3 Marks) — Answer: (a) Liquid (b) Solid/Liquid (at melting point) (c) Liquid/Gas (at boiling point).

Quick Revision — At a Glance

🎯 Key Formulas & Conversions:
  • Temperature Conversion: K = °C + 273.15
  • Melting point of ice: 273.15 K (0°C)
  • Boiling point of water: 373 K (100°C)
  • Latent Heat of Fusion of ice: 3.34 × 10⁵ J/kg
  • Latent Heat of Vaporization of water: 2.25 × 10⁶ J/kg
  • Normal atmospheric pressure: 1 atm = 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa
🎯 Key Tips for Exam:
  • Evaporation always causes cooling because particles absorb energy from surroundings to overcome forces of attraction.
  • During melting and boiling, temperature remains constant even though heat is supplied.
  • Solids have the strongest intermolecular forces; gases have the weakest.
  • Diffusion is fastest in gases and slowest in solids.
  • Sublimation: Solid → Gas directly (Camphor, Naphthalene, Dry ice).
  • Increasing pressure decreases the melting point of ice (ice melts faster under pressure).
  • Steam causes more severe burns than boiling water due to latent heat of vaporization.
Did You Know? The three states of matter were first described by Robert Boyle, and the particle nature of matter was proposed by John Dalton in his atomic theory (1808). The fourth state of matter — Plasma — exists in stars and lightning!
