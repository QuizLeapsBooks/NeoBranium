Atoms and Molecules High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter lays the foundation for chemical formulas and stoichiometry. The mole concept and laws of chemical combination are extremely important for board exams and higher classes.

1. Laws of Chemical Combination

Antoine Lavoisier and Joseph L. Proust established two fundamental laws of chemical combination through experiments.

A. Law of Conservation of Mass

Statement: Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

  • In any chemical reaction, the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.
  • Example: When 12 g of carbon reacts with 32 g of oxygen, 44 g of carbon dioxide is formed.
    C + O₂ → CO₂
    12 g + 32 g = 44 g

B. Law of Constant Proportions (Law of Definite Proportions)

Statement: In a chemical substance, elements are always present in definite proportions by mass.

  • Water (H₂O) always contains hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 1:8 by mass, regardless of its source.
  • Example: If 9 g of water is decomposed, it gives 1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen.
📌 Exam Tip: These two laws are the foundation of Dalton's Atomic Theory. Questions asking to "state and explain" these laws are very common.

2. Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

John Dalton proposed the first scientific theory about the nature of matter. Its main postulates are:

  • All matter is made up of very small indivisible particles called atoms.
  • Atoms cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties.
  • Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties.
  • Atoms combine in small whole-number ratios to form compounds.
  • Atoms are the smallest unit that take part in a chemical reaction.
🔑 Drawbacks of Dalton's Theory:
  • Atoms are not indivisible — they contain protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Atoms of the same element may have different masses (isotopes, e.g., Cl-35 and Cl-37).
  • Atoms of different elements may have same mass (isobars, e.g., Ar-40 and Ca-40).

3. Atoms & Atomic Mass

What is an Atom?

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that retains all the chemical properties of that element. Atoms are the building blocks of all matter.

  • Atomic radius: Measured in nanometers (10⁻⁹ m).
  • Hydrogen atom is the smallest atom.
  • Symbols: Each element has a unique symbol (H, O, C, N, etc.) based on IUPAC rules.

Atomic Mass

Since atoms are extremely small, their masses are measured in atomic mass units (u).

1 u = 1/12 × mass of one Carbon-12 atom
1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g

Atomic Mass of Common Elements

  • Hydrogen (H) = 1 u
  • Carbon (C) = 12 u
  • Nitrogen (N) = 14 u
  • Oxygen (O) = 16 u
  • Sodium (Na) = 23 u
  • Sulphur (S) = 32 u
  • Chlorine (Cl) = 35.5 u
📌 Average Atomic Mass: If an element has isotopes, the average atomic mass is the weighted average of their masses.
Example: Chlorine = (75% × 35) + (25% × 37) = 35.5 u

4. Molecules & Ions

Molecule

A molecule is a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together. It is the smallest particle of a substance (element or compound) that can exist independently.

  • Molecule of an element: Atoms of the same element combine. Examples: H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, O₃
  • Molecule of a compound: Atoms of different elements combine. Examples: H₂O, CO₂, NaCl, NH₃

Atomicity

The number of atoms present in a molecule is called its atomicity.

  • Monoatomic: He, Ne, Ar (1 atom)
  • Diatomic: H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂ (2 atoms)
  • Triatomic: O₃ (3 atoms)
  • Tetraatomic: P₄ (4 atoms)
  • Polyatomic: S₈ (8 atoms)

Ions

An ion is an atom or group of atoms that carries a positive or negative charge.

  • Cation (Positive ion): Formed by loss of electrons. Examples: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, NH₄⁺
  • Anion (Negative ion): Formed by gain of electrons. Examples: Cl⁻, O²⁻, S²⁻, OH⁻, SO₄²⁻
🔑 Common Polyatomic Ions to Memorize:
  • Ammonium: NH₄⁺
  • Hydroxide: OH⁻
  • Nitrate: NO₃⁻
  • Sulphate: SO₄²⁻
  • Carbonate: CO₃²⁻
  • Phosphate: PO₄³⁻

5. Chemical Formulas

A chemical formula is a symbolic representation of the composition of a compound using element symbols and subscripts.

Rules for Writing Chemical Formulas

  1. Write the symbols of the elements side by side.
  2. Write the valencies below each symbol.
  3. Cross-multiply the valencies (exchange the numbers).
  4. If the valency is 1, no subscript is written.
  5. Simplify the formula if possible (divide by common factor).

Example: Aluminium Chloride

Al (valency 3)   Cl (valency 1)
Al¹ Cl³ → AlCl₃

Molecular Mass

The molecular mass of a substance is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule.

Molecular mass of H₂O = (2 × 1) + (1 × 16) = 18 u
Molecular mass of CO₂ = (1 × 12) + (2 × 16) = 44 u

Formula Unit Mass

Used for ionic compounds (which do not exist as molecules). Example: NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u.

6. Mole Concept

A mole is the amount of substance that contains as many particles (atoms, molecules, or ions) as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of Carbon-12.

1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles
This number is called Avogadro's Number (N₀)

Molar Mass

The mass of 1 mole of a substance is called its molar mass. It is numerically equal to the atomic or molecular mass in grams.

  • Molar mass of H₂O = 18 g/mol
  • Molar mass of CO₂ = 44 g/mol
  • Molar mass of O₂ = 32 g/mol
  • Molar mass of NaCl = 58.5 g/mol

Important Formulas

Number of moles (n) = Given Mass (m) / Molar Mass (M)

Number of moles (n) = Number of particles (N) / Avogadro's number (N₀)

Number of particles = Number of moles × 6.022 × 10²³
🎯 Molar Volume: At STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure: 273 K and 1 atm), 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L.

Solved Example

Q: Calculate the number of moles in 52 g of Helium (He). Atomic mass of He = 4 u.

Solution: n = m / M = 52 / 4 = 13 moles

Most Repeated Previous Year Questions

  1. State the law of constant proportions. (1 Mark) — Answer: In a chemical substance, elements are always present in definite proportions by mass.
  2. Write the chemical formula for Aluminium chloride. (1 Mark) — Answer: AlCl₃
  3. Calculate the number of moles in 52g of He. (Atomic mass of He = 4u) (2 Marks) — Answer: n = 52/4 = 13 moles.
  4. What is the mass of 0.5 mole of water molecules? (2 Marks) — Answer: Mass = 0.5 × 18 = 9 g.
  5. Write the postulates of Dalton's atomic theory. (3 Marks) — Answer: All matter is made of atoms; atoms are indivisible; atoms of same element are identical; atoms combine in small whole-number ratios.
  6. Calculate the molecular mass of HNO₃. (2 Marks) — Answer: (1×1) + (1×14) + (3×16) = 1 + 14 + 48 = 63 u.
  7. Define atomicity. Give one example each of monoatomic, diatomic, and polyatomic molecules. (3 Marks) — Answer: Number of atoms in a molecule. Monoatomic: He; Diatomic: O₂; Polyatomic: S₈.
  8. How many atoms are present in 1 mole of oxygen gas (O₂)? (2 Marks) — Answer: 1 mole of O₂ = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.2044 × 10²⁴ atoms.
  9. What is the difference between an atom and a molecule? (2 Marks) — Answer: An atom is the smallest particle of an element; a molecule is a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded.
  10. Calculate the number of particles in 46 g of sodium (Na). Atomic mass of Na = 23 u. (2 Marks) — Answer: n = 46/23 = 2 moles; particles = 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.2044 × 10²⁴.

Quick Revision — At a Glance

🎯 Key Formulas to Memorize:
  • Number of moles (n) = Given Mass (m) / Molar Mass (M)
  • Number of moles (n) = Number of particles (N) / 6.022 × 10²³
  • Number of particles = n × 6.022 × 10²³
  • 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g
  • Avogadro's Number (N₀) = 6.022 × 10²³
  • Molar Volume at STP = 22.4 L
🎯 Key Tips for Exam:
  • Molar mass of H₂O = 18 g; CO₂ = 44 g; O₂ = 32 g; NaCl = 58.5 g.
  • To write chemical formulas, cross-multiply the valencies.
  • Atoms are indivisible — but this postulate of Dalton was later proven wrong.
  • Cations are positive (lost electrons); anions are negative (gained electrons).
  • Law of Conservation of Mass: Mass of reactants = Mass of products.
  • Law of Constant Proportions: Elements combine in fixed mass ratios.
Did You Know? The word "atom" comes from the Greek word "atomos", meaning "indivisible". It was first proposed by the Greek philosopher Democritus around 400 BCE — over 2,000 years before Dalton's theory!
