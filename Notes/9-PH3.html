Gravitation High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Understanding universal gravitation and gravity formulas is key for class 11 physics. Derivations and numericals are frequent in board exams.

Introduction: What is Gravitation?

We know that the Earth attracts every object towards it. But have you ever wondered whether this attraction is mutual? Does an apple attract the Earth as well? The answer is yes. In 1687, Isaac Newton gave the Universal Law of Gravitation, which states that every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force [citation:9].

This gravitational force explains a wide range of phenomena:

  • The motion of the Moon around the Earth
  • The motion of planets around the Sun
  • The occurrence of high and low tides
  • The presence of an atmosphere around the Earth [citation:6]

1. Universal Law of Gravitation

Statement

Universal Law of Gravitation: Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force which is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres. The force acts along the line joining the centres of the two objects [citation:1].

Mathematical Formulation

F = G × (M × m) / d²

Where:

  • F = Gravitational force between the two objects (in Newton, N)
  • M and m = Masses of the two objects (in kg)
  • d = Distance between the centres of the two objects (in metres)
  • G = Universal Gravitation Constant = 6.673 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg²

Why is G called the Universal Constant?

G is called the universal constant because its value remains the same throughout the universe and is independent of the masses of the objects, the distance between them, or the medium in which they are placed [citation:3].

Effects of Changing Mass and Distance

ChangeEffect on Gravitational Force
Mass of one object doubledForce doubles (F ∝ M)
Distance between objects doubledForce becomes one-fourth (F ∝ 1/d²)
Both masses doubled AND distance doubledForce remains unchanged [citation:3]
Distance reduced to halfForce becomes four times larger [citation:1]

📝 Solved Example: Gravitational Force Between Earth and a 1 kg Object

Question: What is the magnitude of the gravitational force between the Earth and a 1 kg object on its surface? (Mass of Earth = 6 × 10²⁴ kg, Radius of Earth = 6.4 × 10⁶ m)

Solution:

M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg, m = 1 kg, d = 6.4 × 10⁶ m, G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg²

F = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6 × 10²⁴ × 1) / (6.4 × 10⁶)²

F = (6.67 × 6 × 10¹³) / (40.96 × 10¹²)

F = 9.8 N [citation:1]

📝 Solved Example: Force Between Two Small Spheres

Question: What is the force of gravitation between two point masses of 1 kg and 2 kg kept 1 m apart?

Solution: F = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 1 × 2) / (1)² = 13.34 × 10⁻¹¹ N. This is an extremely small force [citation:4].

Important Conceptual Points

  • Equal Force: The Earth attracts the Moon with the same force with which the Moon attracts the Earth [citation:1].
  • Why doesn't Earth move towards Moon? Because the Earth's mass is much larger, its acceleration (a = F/M) is negligible. The Moon accelerates much more [citation:1].
  • Why don't we feel gravitational force between small objects? Because G is extremely small, the force between ordinary objects is negligible.

2. Free Fall and Acceleration Due to Gravity (g)

What is Free Fall?

When an object falls towards the Earth under the influence of gravitational force alone (without any air resistance), the motion is called free fall [citation:1].

Acceleration Due to Gravity (g)

When an object is in free fall, it experiences an acceleration due to the Earth's gravitational pull. This acceleration is called acceleration due to gravity (g).

g = G × M / R²

Where:

  • M = Mass of the Earth = 6 × 10²⁴ kg
  • R = Radius of the Earth = 6.4 × 10⁶ m
  • G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg²

The value of g on the surface of the Earth is 9.8 m/s² [citation:1].

Key Points About g

  • g is independent of mass: A heavy object does NOT fall faster than a light object (in the absence of air resistance). Both fall with the same acceleration [citation:1].
  • g varies with location: It is maximum at the poles and minimum at the equator. It decreases with height and depth.
  • g on Moon: The value of g on the Moon is about 1/6th of its value on Earth (≈ 1.63 m/s²).
🎯 Important: The equations of motion (v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as) can be used for freely falling bodies by replacing a with g. For upward motion, g is taken as negative (−g). For downward motion, g is taken as positive (+g) [citation:4].

📝 Solved Example: Velocity of a Falling Ball

Question: A ball is dropped from the top of a tower 40 m high. What is its velocity when it has covered 20 m? (Take g = 10 m/s²)

Solution: u = 0, a = g = 10 m/s², s = 20 m

v² = u² + 2as = 0 + 2 × 10 × 20 = 400

v = 20 m/s [citation:4]

📝 Solved Example: Ball Thrown Upwards

Question: A particle is thrown up vertically with a velocity of 50 m/s. (a) What is its velocity at the highest point? (b) How high does it rise? (c) What time does it take to reach the highest point?

Solution:

  • (a) At the highest point, velocity = 0 [citation:3]
  • (b) v² = u² − 2gs → 0 = 50² − 2 × 9.8 × s → s = 127.5 m
  • (c) v = u − gt → 0 = 50 − 9.8t → t = 5.1 s [citation:4]

📝 Solved Example: Value of g at Height

Question: Calculate the value of g at a height of 3200 km from the Earth's surface. (Me = 6 × 10²⁴ kg, Re = 6400 km)

Solution: r = Re + h = 6400 + 3200 = 9600 km = 9.6 × 10⁶ m

g = GM/r² = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6 × 10²⁴) / (9.6 × 10⁶)²

g = 4.3 m/s² [citation:10]

3. Mass and Weight

Mass

Mass is the quantity of matter contained in a body. It is a measure of the body's inertia [citation:3].

  • Mass is a scalar quantity (only magnitude)
  • SI unit: kilogram (kg)
  • Mass remains constant everywhere in the universe

Weight

Weight is the force with which the Earth attracts an object. It is the gravitational force acting on the body [citation:3].

W = m × g
  • Weight is a vector quantity (directed towards the centre of the Earth)
  • SI unit: Newton (N)
  • Weight varies from place to place (depends on g)

Comparison: Mass vs Weight

MassWeight
Quantity of matterForce of gravity on the object
Scalar quantityVector quantity
SI unit: kgSI unit: N
Constant everywhereVaries with location
Measured by beam balanceMeasured by spring balance

Weight on the Moon

The mass of the Moon is much less than the Earth's mass, and its radius is about 1/4th of Earth's radius. Due to this, the value of g on the Moon is about 1/6th of its value on Earth [citation:3].

📌 Key Point: Weight on Moon = (1/6) × Weight on Earth. For example, if your weight on Earth is 60 N, on the Moon it will be 10 N.

📝 Solved Example: Weight on Neptune

Question: The ratio of gravitational force on Neptune to the gravitational force on Earth is 9:8. Ramesh weighs 792 N on Earth. Calculate his weight on Neptune.

Solution: Weight on Neptune = (9/8) × 792 = 891 N [citation:3]

📝 Solved Example: Weight on a Different Planet

Question: The weight of a body on the surface of the Earth is 392 N. What will be its weight on a planet whose mass is double that of Earth and radius is four times that of Earth?

Solution: g_planet / g_earth = (M_p/M_e) × (R_e/R_p)² = 2 × (1/4)² = 2/16 = 1/8

Weight on planet = 392 × (1/8) = 49 N [citation:6]

4. Thrust and Pressure

Thrust

Thrust is the force acting on a body perpendicular to its surface. It is a vector quantity and its SI unit is Newton (N) [citation:5].

Pressure

Pressure is the thrust per unit area. It is a scalar quantity [citation:5].

Pressure = Thrust / Area   |   SI Unit: Pascal (Pa)

1 Pascal = 1 N/m². It is the pressure exerted when a force of 1 N acts on an area of 1 m² [citation:3].

Effect of Area on Pressure

AreaPressureExample
Smaller areaLarger pressureNail has a pointed tip
Larger areaSmaller pressureBuildings have wide foundations

📝 Solved Example: Pressure Calculation

Question: A force of 200 N is applied perpendicular to a surface having an area of 4 square metres. Calculate the pressure.

Solution: Pressure = Thrust/Area = 200/4 = 50 Pa [citation:4]

5. Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

Buoyancy (Upthrust)

When a body is partially or wholly immersed in a fluid (liquid or gas), it experiences an upward force. This upward force is called buoyant force or upthrust. The phenomenon is called buoyancy [citation:5][citation:8].

Factors Affecting Buoyant Force

The buoyant force depends on [citation:2]:

  • Volume of the body submerged: Greater the volume submerged, greater the upthrust.
  • Density of the fluid: Greater the density of the fluid, greater the upthrust.

Why Does Buoyancy Occur?

Pressure in a liquid increases with depth. The pressure on the bottom of a submerged object is greater than the pressure on the top. This pressure difference creates a net upward force — the upthrust [citation:2].

Archimedes' Principle

Archimedes' Principle: When an object is immersed wholly or partially in a fluid, it experiences an upthrust which is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it [citation:2][citation:5].
Buoyant Force = Weight of Fluid Displaced = ρ × V × g

Conditions for Floating and Sinking

ConditionResult
Weight of body < Buoyant force (W < FB)Body floats (partially submerged)
Weight of body = Buoyant force (W = FB)Body floats (fully submerged)
Weight of body > Buoyant force (W > FB)Body sinks [citation:2]

Why Does a Ship Float but a Steel Needle Sinks?

A steel needle sinks because the density of steel is greater than the density of water. A ship is made of steel but is given a hollow shape. This increases the volume of water displaced, making the effective density of the ship less than that of water. Hence, the buoyant force becomes greater than the weight of the ship, and it floats [citation:3].

📝 Solved Example: Will a Plastic Bottle Float?

Question: A plastic bottle of 500 g has a volume of 450 cm³. Will it float or sink in water? (Density of water = 1 g/cm³)

Solution: Density of bottle = 500/450 = 1.11 g/cm³

Since density of bottle (1.11 g/cm³) > density of water (1 g/cm³), the bottle will sink [citation:4].

6. Density and Relative Density

Density

Density of a substance is defined as its mass per unit volume [citation:2].

Density (ρ) = Mass (M) / Volume (V)
  • SI unit: kg/m³
  • CGS unit: g/cm³
  • 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³ [citation:3]

Relative Density

Relative Density of a substance is the ratio of its density to the density of water at 4°C [citation:2].

Relative Density = Density of substance / Density of water
  • Relative density has no unit (it is a ratio)
  • If relative density > 1, the substance sinks in water
  • If relative density < 1, the substance floats in water

📝 Solved Example: Density Conversion

Question: The density of an object is 1.8 g/cm³. Express it in kg/m³.

Solution: 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³

1.8 g/cm³ = 1.8 × 1000 = 1800 kg/m³ [citation:3]

📝 Solved Example: Density of Iron

Question: The relative density of iron is 7. Calculate the density of iron in SI units. (Density of water = 1000 kg/m³)

Solution: Density of iron = Relative Density × Density of water = 7 × 1000 = 7000 kg/m³ [citation:4]

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Takeaways:
  • Universal Law of Gravitation: F = G(Mm)/d²
  • G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg² (constant everywhere)
  • g = GM/R² = 9.8 m/s² on Earth's surface
  • Mass = scalar, constant; Weight = vector, varies (W = mg)
  • Weight on Moon = (1/6) × Weight on Earth
  • Pressure = Thrust/Area; SI unit: Pascal (Pa)
  • Archimedes' Principle: Buoyant force = Weight of fluid displaced
  • Relative Density = Density of substance / Density of water (no unit)
  • Equations of motion for free fall: replace a with g
