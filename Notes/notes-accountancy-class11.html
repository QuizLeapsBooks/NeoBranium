Chapter 1: Introduction to Accounting

What is Accounting?

Definition
Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, and communicating financial information to enable informed decisions by users.

Objectives of Accounting

  • Maintain systematic records of business transactions.
  • Ascertain the profit or loss for a given period.
  • Determine the financial position (assets and liabilities).
  • Provide information to users for decision-making.
  • Ensure legal compliance.

Users of Accounting Information

Internal UsersExternal Users
Owners / ShareholdersCreditors / Lenders
ManagementGovernment / Tax Authorities
EmployeesInvestors / Potential Investors
Customers, Public

Branches of Accounting

  • Financial Accounting: Records and reports past transactions.
  • Cost Accounting: Determines cost of production.
  • Management Accounting: Helps management in planning and control.

Accounting as a Science and Art

Accounting is a science (based on principles) and an art (requires skill and judgement in application).

Key Terms
  • Transaction: Any business event that can be measured in money.
  • Capital: Amount invested by the owner.
  • Liability: Amount owed to outsiders.
  • Asset: Resources owned by the business.
  • Revenue: Income earned from business activities.
  • Expense: Cost incurred to earn revenue.

Accounting Equation

Basic Accounting Equation
Assets = Capital + Liabilities
OR
Capital = Assets − Liabilities

Chapter 2: Theory Base of Accounting

Accounting Assumptions (GAAP)

Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) are the standard rules and guidelines for financial reporting.

Basic Accounting Assumptions

  • Going Concern Assumption: The business will continue to operate indefinitely (not wind up soon).
  • Consistency Assumption: Same accounting methods are used from year to year.
  • Accrual Assumption: Revenues and expenses are recorded when earned/incurred, not when cash is received/paid.

Accounting Principles

PrincipleMeaning
Revenue RecognitionRevenue is recorded when earned, regardless of cash receipt.
Matching PrincipleExpenses are matched with related revenues in the same period.
Full DisclosureAll material information must be disclosed in financial statements.
Cost PrincipleAssets are recorded at historical cost, not market value.
Objectivity PrincipleEntries should be based on verifiable evidence.
MaterialityOnly material (significant) items need to be disclosed separately.
Conservatism (Prudence)Anticipate no profit, but provide for all possible losses.
Dual AspectEvery transaction has two aspects — debit and credit (equal and opposite).

Accounting Standards

Accounting Standards (AS) are written statements issued by the ICAI (Institute of Chartered Accountants of India) to ensure uniformity in financial reporting. India follows Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS) aligned with IFRS.

Difference: Assumptions vs. Principles vs. Conventions
  • Assumptions: Fundamental truths taken for granted (Going Concern, Consistency, Accrual).
  • Principles: Rules derived from assumptions (Matching, Revenue Recognition).
  • Conventions: Customs followed in practice (Conservatism, Materiality, Full Disclosure).

Systems of Accounting

  • Cash Basis: Transactions recorded only when cash is received or paid. Simple but incomplete.
  • Accrual Basis: Transactions recorded when they occur, regardless of cash flow. Gives a true picture.

Chapter 3: Recording of Transactions — I

Source Documents

Source documents are the original records of a transaction: Cash Memo, Invoice, Receipt, Debit Note, Credit Note, Pay-in-Slip, Cheque, etc.

Accounting Voucher

A voucher is a written document prepared on the basis of source documents, authorising an entry. Types: Cash Voucher (payment/receipt), Non-Cash / Transfer Voucher (journal entry).

Double Entry System

Principle
Every transaction has two effects — a debit (Dr.) and a credit (Cr.) of equal amounts. Total Debits = Total Credits always.

Rules of Debit and Credit

Account TypeDebit (Dr.)Credit (Cr.)
Personal AccountReceiverGiver
Real AccountWhat comes inWhat goes out
Nominal AccountExpenses & LossesIncomes & Gains

Types of Accounts

  • Personal Accounts: Accounts of persons/firms/companies (e.g., Ram's A/c, Bank A/c).
  • Real Accounts: Tangible and intangible assets (Cash, Land, Goodwill).
  • Nominal Accounts: Expenses, incomes, gains, losses (Salary, Rent, Sales).

Journal

The Journal (or Day Book) is the book of original entry where all transactions are first recorded chronologically.

Journal Entry Format
Date | Particulars | L.F. | Dr. Amount | Cr. Amount
Example
Purchased goods for cash ₹10,000:
Purchases A/c Dr. ₹10,000
    To Cash A/c ₹10,000
(Goods purchased for cash)

Ledger

The Ledger is the principal book of accounts. Each account has a separate page (T-account / account). Posting = transferring journal entries to the ledger.

Balancing of Ledger Accounts

At the end of the period, each account is balanced: Debit side total − Credit side total = Balance. If Dr. > Cr. → Debit Balance; if Cr. > Dr. → Credit Balance.

Key Points
  • Journal → Ledger → Trial Balance → Financial Statements (the accounting cycle).
  • Narration is a brief explanation written below each journal entry.
  • L.F. (Ledger Folio) = page number of the ledger account.

Chapter 4: Recording of Transactions — II

Cash Book

Definition
A Cash Book is a special journal that records all cash receipts and payments. It serves the dual purpose of a Journal and a Ledger for cash.

Types of Cash Book

  • Simple (Single Column) Cash Book: Records only cash transactions.
  • Double Column Cash Book: Records cash + bank transactions (two columns).
  • Triple Column Cash Book: Cash + Bank + Discount columns.

Petty Cash Book

Records small, frequent cash payments (stationery, postage, refreshments). Imprest System: A fixed amount is given to the petty cashier at the start; at the end of the period, the amount spent is reimbursed, restoring the imprest amount.

Special Purpose / Subsidiary Books

BookRecords
Purchases BookCredit purchases of goods only
Sales BookCredit sales of goods only
Purchases Return BookGoods returned to suppliers
Sales Return BookGoods returned by customers
Bills Receivable BookBills received from debtors
Bills Payable BookBills accepted in favour of creditors
Journal ProperTransactions not recorded in any other book

Contra Entry

A contra entry affects both Cash and Bank columns — both debit and credit appear in the same cash book. Example: Cash deposited into bank, or cash withdrawn from bank.

Key Points
  • Credit purchases of goods → Purchases Book; cash purchases → Cash Book.
  • Cash Book always has a debit balance (or nil) — never a credit balance.
  • Discount Allowed (to debtors) → Dr. side; Discount Received (from creditors) → Cr. side.

Chapter 5: Bank Reconciliation Statement

What is Bank Reconciliation Statement (BRS)?

Definition
BRS is a statement prepared to reconcile (explain the difference between) the balance as per the Cash Book (Bank column) and the balance as per the Bank Passbook (Statement) on a given date.

Why Does a Difference Arise?

  • Cheques issued but not yet presented for payment.
  • Cheques deposited but not yet cleared/credited by the bank.
  • Bank charges/interest debited by bank but not yet recorded in Cash Book.
  • Interest credited by bank but not yet recorded in Cash Book.
  • Direct payments/receipts by bank not intimated to customer.
  • Errors in Cash Book or Passbook.

Favourable vs. Unfavourable Balance

ConditionCash BookPassbook
Favourable (Dr. balance in CB)Debit balanceCredit balance
Unfavourable (overdraft)Credit balanceDebit balance

Preparation of BRS

Starting from Cash Book Balance (Favourable)
Balance as per Cash Book (Dr.)          ₹ ___
Add: Cheques deposited but not yet cleared   ₹ ___
Less: Cheques issued but not yet presented   ₹ ___
Add: Bank charges not entered in CB        ₹ ___
Add/Less: Errors                                ₹ ___
= Balance as per Passbook                  ₹ ___
Key Points
  • BRS is not a part of the accounting books — it is just a reconciliation statement.
  • BRS is usually prepared at the end of a month.
  • Bank's books and the firm's books are maintained independently — hence differences.

Chapter 6: Trial Balance and Rectification of Errors

Trial Balance

Definition
A Trial Balance is a list of all ledger account balances prepared at the end of an accounting period to verify the arithmetical accuracy of the ledger. Total of Dr. balances = Total of Cr. balances.

Objectives of Trial Balance

  • Verify arithmetical accuracy of accounting records.
  • Detect errors (some types).
  • Help in preparation of financial statements.

Limitations: Errors NOT revealed by Trial Balance

  • Error of Omission — transaction completely omitted.
  • Error of Commission — wrong amount posted to wrong account of same type.
  • Error of Principle — wrong type of account used.
  • Compensating Errors — errors that cancel each other out.

Types of Errors

TypeExampleRevealed by TB?
Error of OmissionTransaction not recorded at allNo
Error of CommissionWrong amount enteredOnly if one-sided
Error of PrincipleRevenue treated as capitalNo
Compensating ErrorTwo errors cancel outNo
Errors of DuplicationSame entry made twiceNo

Suspense Account

When a Trial Balance does not agree, the difference is temporarily placed in a Suspense Account to make it balance. As errors are found and corrected, the Suspense Account is closed.

Rectification of Errors

Example — Error of Omission Rectification
Goods purchased ₹5,000 was not recorded at all.
Rectification: Purchases A/c Dr. ₹5,000 / To Creditor A/c ₹5,000
Key Points
  • Errors before Trial Balance: corrected by journal entry.
  • Errors after Trial Balance: corrected via Suspense Account entries.
  • Errors affecting one account → one-sided error → affect Trial Balance.

Chapter 7: Depreciation, Provisions and Reserves

Depreciation

Definition
Depreciation is the gradual, permanent decrease in the value of a fixed (tangible) asset due to use, passage of time, obsolescence, or wear and tear.

Causes of Depreciation

  • Physical wear and tear from use.
  • Passage of time (even if unused).
  • Obsolescence (new technology making old assets outdated).
  • Depletion (for natural resources like mines).

Methods of Depreciation

FeatureStraight Line Method (SLM)Written Down Value Method (WDV)
Depreciation amountFixed every yearDecreasing every year
Calculated onOriginal/Historical costBook value (reducing balance)
Book value at endReaches zero (or scrap value)Never reaches zero
Also calledFixed Instalment MethodDiminishing Balance Method
SLM Formula
Annual Depreciation = (Cost − Scrap Value) / Useful Life
Rate % = (Annual Depreciation / Cost) × 100
WDV Formula
Depreciation = Book Value at beginning of year × Rate %

Provisions

A provision is a charge against profit set aside for a known liability of uncertain amount. Example: Provision for Bad Debts, Provision for Taxation.

Reserves

A reserve is an appropriation of profit (not a charge) set aside to strengthen the financial position. Example: General Reserve, Capital Reserve, Dividend Equalisation Reserve.

ProvisionReserve
Charge against profit (compulsory)Appropriation of profit (voluntary)
Known liability, uncertain amountStrengthen financial position
Cannot be distributed as dividendRevenue reserves can be distributed
Key Points
  • Accumulated Depreciation Method: Depreciation is credited to a separate Accumulated Depreciation A/c, not the asset A/c.
  • Revenue Reserve: General Reserve, Dividend Equalisation Reserve — created from revenue profits.
  • Capital Reserve: Share Premium, Profit on sale of fixed assets — cannot be distributed as dividend.

Chapter 8: Bills of Exchange

Bill of Exchange

Definition (Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881)
A Bill of Exchange is an unconditional order in writing, addressed by one person to another, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer of the instrument.

Parties to a Bill

  • Drawer: The creditor who draws (creates) the bill. (Seller)
  • Drawee/Acceptor: The debtor on whom the bill is drawn. (Buyer) — becomes Acceptor after signing.
  • Payee: The person to whom payment is to be made (often the drawer).

Promissory Note vs. Bill of Exchange

FeatureBill of ExchangePromissory Note
Drawn byCreditor (Drawer)Debtor (Maker)
PartiesThree (Drawer, Drawee, Payee)Two (Maker, Payee)
AcceptanceRequiredNot required

Days of Grace

3 days of grace are added to the stated period. E.g., if a 2-month bill is drawn on Jan 1, due date = Mar 1 + 3 days = March 4.

Discounting a Bill

The drawer can discount the bill with a bank before due date — the bank pays the face value less discount charges.

Endorsement

The payee can transfer the bill to a third party by endorsement (signing on the back).

Dishonour of Bill

If the drawee refuses to pay on the due date, the bill is said to be dishonoured. A Noting Charge is paid to a Notary Public as evidence of dishonour.

Key Journal Entries
  • Bill drawn: Bills Receivable A/c Dr. / To Debtor A/c
  • Bill accepted: Creditor A/c Dr. / To Bills Payable A/c
  • Bill discounted: Bank A/c Dr. / Discount A/c Dr. / To Bills Receivable A/c
  • Bill dishonoured: Debtor A/c Dr. / To Bills Receivable A/c (+ Noting Charges)

Chapter 9: Financial Statements — I

Financial Statements

Financial statements are formal reports that summarise the financial activities and position of a business. For a sole proprietorship / partnership:

  • Trading Account: Shows Gross Profit / Gross Loss.
  • Profit & Loss Account: Shows Net Profit / Net Loss.
  • Balance Sheet: Shows financial position (Assets = Liabilities + Capital).

Trading Account

Gross Profit Formula
Gross Profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold
COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases − Purchase Returns − Closing Stock + Direct Expenses

Profit & Loss Account

Net Profit Formula
Net Profit = Gross Profit + Other Incomes − Indirect Expenses

Indirect expenses include: Salaries, Rent, Advertising, Depreciation, Bad Debts, Insurance, etc.

Adjustments in Financial Statements

AdjustmentTrading / P&L EffectBalance Sheet Effect
Closing StockCredit in Trading A/cAsset side
Outstanding ExpensesAdd to respective expenseLiability side
Prepaid ExpensesDeduct from respective expenseAsset side
Accrued IncomeAdd to respective incomeAsset side
Income received in advanceDeduct from respective incomeLiability side
DepreciationDebit in P&L A/cDeduct from asset
Bad DebtsDebit in P&L A/cDeduct from debtors
Key Points
  • Every adjustment appears at two places in Final Accounts.
  • Direct expenses (Carriage Inward, Wages, Factory Expenses) go to Trading A/c.
  • Indirect expenses (Office, Selling, Finance) go to P&L A/c.

Chapter 10: Financial Statements — II

Balance Sheet

Definition
A Balance Sheet is a statement of assets and liabilities of a business at a particular date. It shows the financial position of the business. Assets always equal Capital + Liabilities.

Marshalling of Balance Sheet

  • Order of Liquidity: Most liquid assets first (Cash, Debtors, Stock...). Liabilities in order of urgency of repayment.
  • Order of Permanence: Most permanent assets first (Fixed Assets first).

Classification of Assets

Fixed AssetsCurrent Assets
Land & Building, Plant, MachineryCash, Bank, Debtors, Stock
Furniture, VehiclesBills Receivable, Prepaid Expenses
Goodwill, Patents (Intangible)Short-term Investments

Classification of Liabilities

Long-term LiabilitiesCurrent Liabilities
Capital, Long-term LoansCreditors, Bank Overdraft
DebenturesBills Payable, Outstanding Expenses

Important Adjustments

  • Provision for Bad and Doubtful Debts: Deducted from Debtors in B/S; debited in P&L.
  • Manager's Commission: Shown as outstanding expense on liability side.
  • Interest on Capital: Credited to capital; debited in P&L.
  • Drawings: Deducted from Capital on B/S.
Key Points
  • Balance Sheet is NOT an account — it has no debit/credit, just Assets and Liabilities sides.
  • Balance Sheet is prepared AS ON a date (snapshot); P&L is FOR a period.
  • Both sides of Balance Sheet must always be equal.

Chapter 11: Accounts from Incomplete Records

Incomplete Records / Single Entry System

Definition
When a business does not maintain full double-entry records, it is called Incomplete Records or Single Entry System. Only personal accounts (Cash Book, Debtors, Creditors) are usually maintained.

Limitations of Single Entry System

  • Not systematic; no arithmetic check possible.
  • Cannot ascertain true profit/loss.
  • Cannot prepare proper Balance Sheet.
  • Not accepted for tax purposes.

Ascertainment of Profit — Statement of Affairs Method

Profit Calculation
Opening Capital = Opening Assets − Opening Liabilities
Closing Capital = Closing Assets − Closing Liabilities
Profit = Closing Capital − Opening Capital + Drawings − Additional Capital

Conversion Method — Finding Missing Figures

To FindUse
Credit SalesTotal Debtors A/c — fill missing figure
Credit PurchasesTotal Creditors A/c — fill missing figure
Cash payments / receiptsCash A/c — find missing figure
Total Debtors A/c Template
Dr: Opening Debtors + Credit Sales = Cr: Cash received + Discount allowed + Returns + Closing Debtors
Key Points
  • Statement of Affairs ≠ Balance Sheet (SA is not formal; BS is based on complete records).
  • Drawings reduce closing capital; additional capital injected increases it.
  • Conversion Method is more accurate than Statement of Affairs Method.

Chapter 12: Applications of Computers in Accounting

Computers in Accounting

Computers have transformed accounting from manual ledger books to automated, accurate, and real-time financial processing. Computerised Accounting System (CAS) uses software to record, classify, and summarise transactions.

Advantages of Computerised Accounting

  • Speed and accuracy — eliminates human calculation errors.
  • Automatic generation of Trial Balance, P&L, Balance Sheet.
  • Easy storage, retrieval, and backup of data.
  • Real-time financial information.
  • Facilitates audit trails.

Components of Computerised Accounting

  • Hardware: Computer, printer, storage devices.
  • Software: Tally, QuickBooks, SAP, Busy, etc.
  • Data: Financial transactions and records.
  • People: Users who operate the system.
  • Procedures: Standard operating procedures for data entry.

Database Management System (DBMS) in Accounting

A DBMS stores all accounting data in an organised, relational database. It eliminates redundancy, allows multiple users, and ensures data integrity. Accounting software like Tally uses DBMS internally.

Tally — Popular Accounting Software

  • Used widely by Indian businesses for accounting, GST, payroll, inventory.
  • Follows double-entry system automatically.
  • Generates all financial statements with one click.

Manual vs. Computerised Accounting

FeatureManualComputerised
SpeedSlowVery fast
AccuracyError-proneHighly accurate
CostLow initial costHigher initial cost
StoragePhysical booksDigital (cloud/disk)
ReportsManual preparationInstant generation
Key Points
  • ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) integrates all business functions including accounting.
  • GST compliance is fully automated through computerised accounting software.
  • Data security (passwords, backups) is critical in computerised systems.
