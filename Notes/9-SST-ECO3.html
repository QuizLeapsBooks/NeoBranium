Poverty as a Challenge High Weightage (Periodic Test)

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains one of the biggest challenges of independent India—poverty. It covers how poverty is measured, which groups are most vulnerable, the causes of poverty, and the government's anti-poverty measures. Questions on poverty line estimation and vulnerable groups are frequently asked [citation:1][citation:3].

What is Poverty?

Poverty is a situation where a person is unable to fulfill their basic needs of food, clothing, and shelter [citation:1]. It is not just about low income—poverty also means lack of access to healthcare, education, clean water, sanitation, and job opportunities [citation:1][citation:5].

🎯 Key Point: One of the biggest challenges of independent India has been to bring millions of its people out of abject poverty. Poverty is a multi-dimensional concept that goes beyond just income levels [citation:1].

1. Poverty as Seen by Social Scientists

Social scientists look at poverty through various social indicators beyond income [citation:1]:

  • Illiteracy level: Lack of education limits opportunities.
  • Malnutrition: Lack of general resistance due to inadequate food.
  • Lack of healthcare access: Unable to afford medical treatment.
  • Lack of job opportunities: No stable source of income.
  • Lack of safe drinking water and sanitation: Basic amenities missing.

Social Exclusion

Social exclusion refers to the situation where poor people live only in poor surroundings, excluded from the social equality enjoyed by better-off people. The caste system in India is a prime example of social exclusion [citation:1][citation:2].

Vulnerability

Vulnerability describes the greater probability of certain communities (like backward castes) or individuals (like widows or physically handicapped persons) becoming or remaining poor in the coming years. Vulnerable groups suffer more when disasters like floods or earthquakes occur [citation:1][citation:2].

2. Poverty Line

The poverty line is a common method used to measure poverty based on income or consumption levels. A person is considered poor if their income or consumption falls below a given "minimum level" necessary to fulfill basic needs [citation:1][citation:5].

How is Poverty Line Determined in India?

  • A minimum level of food requirement, clothing, footwear, fuel and light, educational and medical requirements is determined for subsistence [citation:1].
  • These physical quantities are multiplied by their prices in rupees [citation:1].
  • The food requirement is based on calorie requirements [citation:1].
Calorie Requirement:

Rural Areas: 2,400 calories per person per day

Urban Areas: 2,100 calories per person per day
⚠️ Important: The calorie requirement is higher in rural areas because people do more physical work. However, the poverty line amount is higher in urban areas because the cost of essential goods is higher there [citation:1].

The poverty line is updated periodically (normally every 5 years) through sample surveys conducted by the National Sample Survey Organisation (NSSO) [citation:1][citation:3].

3. Poverty Estimates in India

There has been a substantial decline in poverty ratios in India over the decades [citation:1][citation:5]:

YearPoverty Ratio
1973~55%
1993-94~36%
2004-05~37.2%
2011-12~22%

📝 Important Note:

While the percentage of poor people has declined, the number of poor people remained stable at around 320 million for a long time due to population growth. Recent estimates show a decline to about 260 million [citation:7][citation:10].

4. Vulnerable Groups

The proportion of people below the poverty line is not the same for all social groups and economic categories in India [citation:1][citation:3].

CategoryMost Vulnerable Groups
Social GroupsScheduled Caste (SC) and Scheduled Tribe (ST) households
Economic GroupsRural agricultural labour households and urban casual labour households
🎯 Key Data: 51 out of 100 people belonging to Scheduled Tribes are not able to meet their basic needs. About 50% of casual workers in urban areas are below the poverty line. About 50% of landless agricultural workers and 43% of Scheduled Castes are also poor [citation:1].

Apart from these groups, women, elderly people, and female infants are systematically denied equal access to resources available to the family. This group is considered the poorest of the poor [citation:1].

5. Inter-State Disparities

Poverty is not the same in every state of India. State-level poverty has declined, but the success rate varies [citation:1][citation:5].

States with High Poverty

  • Odisha and Bihar continue to be the two poorest states (with poverty ratios of 47% and 43% respectively in earlier estimates) [citation:1].
  • Poverty is also a serious problem in Assam, Tripura, and Uttar Pradesh [citation:1].

States with Significant Decline in Poverty

  • Kerala: Focused on human resource development (education and health) [citation:5].
  • West Bengal: Implemented land reform measures [citation:5].
  • Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu: Improved the Public Distribution System (PDS) [citation:5].
  • Punjab and Haryana: High agricultural growth rates [citation:1].

6. Global Poverty Scenario

Poverty has declined substantially in China and Southeast Asian countries due to rapid economic growth and massive investments in human resource development [citation:1][citation:4].

  • China: Poverty fell from 85% in 1981 to 14% in 2008 [citation:4].
  • South Asia: Decline has been less rapid compared to China.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: Poverty has risen (from 41% in 1981 to 46% in 2001) [citation:9].
  • Latin America: Poverty declined from 11% in 1981 to 6.4% in 2008 [citation:4].

The World Bank defines extreme economic poverty as living on less than $1.90 per day (updated from $1 per day). This has fallen from 36% in 1990 to 10% in 2015 [citation:8].

7. Causes of Poverty in India

Historical Reasons

  • Colonial Rule: Low level of economic development under British administration. Policies ruined traditional handicrafts and discouraged industries like textiles [citation:3][citation:5].

Economic Reasons

  • Low economic growth: Persisted until the 1980s, resulting in fewer job opportunities [citation:5].
  • High population growth: Combined with low growth, this made per capita income very low [citation:3].
  • Unequal distribution of land and resources: Huge income inequalities [citation:3][citation:5].
  • Lack of land resources: Rural poor depend on agricultural income, which is low [citation:4].

Social Reasons

  • Caste system and social exclusion kept certain sections in poverty [citation:3].

8. Anti-Poverty Measures

The government's anti-poverty strategy is based on two main planks [citation:4][citation:8]:

1. Promotion of Economic Growth

Economic growth widens opportunities and provides resources to invest in human development. India's growth rate jumped from about 3.5% per year in the 1970s to about 6% in the 1980s and 1990s [citation:4].

2. Targeted Anti-Poverty Programmes

ProgrammeKey Features
MGNREGA (2005)Provides 100 days of assured employment to every rural household. One-third of jobs reserved for women. Aimed at sustainable development [citation:3].
Prime Minister Rozgar Yojana (PMRY)Started in 1993 to create self-employment opportunities for educated unemployed youth in rural areas and small towns [citation:8].
Swarna Jayanti Gram Swarozgar Yojana (SGSY)Aims to bring poor families above the poverty line by organizing them into self-help groups [citation:8].
Public Distribution System (PDS)Provides food grains at subsidized rates to the poor [citation:5].
⚠️ Important: Despite these programmes, poverty alleviation has not been fully successful due to lack of proper implementation, right targeting, and overlapping of schemes [citation:9].
