Power Sharing Board Exam Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains why sharing power is essential in a democracy. It uses the contrasting examples of Belgium (accommodation) and Sri Lanka (majoritarianism) to show how power sharing prevents conflict and maintains stability. Board exams frequently test the Belgium model, Sri Lankan majoritarianism, and the four forms of power sharing.

1.1 What is Power Sharing?

Power sharing is the distribution of political authority among different institutions, levels of government, or social groups. It is the opposite of concentrating power in one hand.

1.2 Why is Power Sharing Important?

In a democracy, people have diverse preferences, identities, and interests. Power sharing ensures that all groups have a voice in governance, preventing conflicts and promoting stability.

🎯 Key Point: Power sharing is the very spirit of democracy. A legitimate government is one where citizens, through participation, acquire a stake in the system.

2. Belgium: A Model of Accommodation

2.1 Ethnic Composition of Belgium

Belgium is a small country in Europe with a complex ethnic composition:

CommunityPercentageRegion
Dutch-speaking (Flemish)59%Northern region of Flanders
French-speaking (Walloons)40%Southern region of Wallonia
German-speaking1%Eastern region

2.2 Tensions in Belgium

In Brussels, the capital city, the French-speaking community was relatively rich and powerful, while the Dutch-speaking community got the benefit of economic development and education much later. This led to tensions between the two communities during the 1950s and 1960s.

2.3 Belgian Power Sharing Model

The Belgian leaders amended their constitution four times between 1970 and 1993 to work out an arrangement that would enable everyone to live together peacefully:

FeatureDescription
Equal Ministers in Central GovernmentThe Constitution prescribes that the number of Dutch and French-speaking ministers shall be equal in the central government. No single community can make decisions unilaterally.
State Government AutonomyMany powers of the central government were given to State governments of the two regions. These State governments are not subordinate to the Central government.
Brussels Special ArrangementBrussels has a separate government in which both communities have equal representation, even though the French-speaking community is in majority in Brussels.
Community GovernmentApart from the Central and State governments, there is a third kind of government called the 'Community Government' elected by people belonging to one language community. It has powers regarding cultural, educational, and language-related issues.
💡 Outcome: The Belgian model successfully accommodated regional and cultural diversities. It prevented civil war and maintained unity in the country.
🧠 Mnemonic: BEAD holds Belgium together
  • B — Brussels special status (equal representation)
  • E — Equal ministers in central government
  • A — Area-wise State governments (autonomous)
  • D — Different Community governments (language-based)

3. Sri Lanka: A Model of Majoritarianism

3.1 Ethnic Composition of Sri Lanka

CommunityPercentageReligion
Sinhalese74%Buddhism
Sri Lankan Tamils18%Hinduism and Islam
Indian Tamils—Hinduism

Tamils are divided into two groups: Sri Lankan Tamils (native to the country) and Indian Tamils (descendants of labourers brought by the British from India).

3.2 Majoritarian Measures (1956 onwards)

After independence in 1948, the Sinhalese leaders adopted a series of majoritarian measures to establish Sinhala supremacy:

YearMeasureImpact
1956An Act was passed to recognise Sinhala as the only official language, disregarding Tamil.Tamils felt alienated.
—Governments followed preferential policies that favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs.Tamils felt ousted from opportunities.
—The Constitution was amended to protect and foster Buddhism.Non-Buddhist Tamils felt discriminated against.

3.3 Consequences

  • Sri Lankan Tamils felt that the Constitution and policies denied them equal political rights, discriminated against them in jobs, and ignored their interests.
  • They demanded recognition of Tamil as an official language, equality in education and jobs, and autonomy in Tamil-majority regions.
  • Their demands were repeatedly denied.
  • By the 1980s, several political organisations were formed demanding an independent Tamil Eelam (state of Tamil people) in northern and eastern parts of Sri Lanka.
  • This led to a civil war that lasted from 1983 to 2009, causing massive loss of life and economic damage.
⚠️ Lesson from Sri Lanka: Majoritarianism — the belief that the majority community should rule however it wants — undermines the unity of the nation. It leads to alienation of minorities and eventually civil conflict.
🧠 Mnemonic: SOFT rules crushed minorities
  • S — Sinhala Only Act (1956)
  • O — One religion preference (Buddhism)
  • F — Favour in jobs and education
  • T — Tamil regions neglected

4. Why Power Sharing is Desirable?

There are two main reasons why power sharing is desirable:

ReasonExplanation
Prudential ReasonPower sharing helps reduce the possibility of conflict between social groups. It ensures political stability. Since social conflict often leads to violence and political instability, power sharing is a good way to ensure stability. For example, reserving constituencies for minorities prevents the kind of civil unrest seen in Sri Lanka.
Moral ReasonPower sharing is the very spirit of democracy. A legitimate government is one where citizens, through participation, acquire a stake in the system. People have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed.
🎯 Key Point: Prudential reasons focus on practical benefits (stability, conflict avoidance). Moral reasons focus on democratic principles (participation, consent, legitimacy).

5. Forms of Power Sharing

In modern democracies, power sharing arrangements can take many forms:

5.1 Horizontal Distribution of Power

  • Power is shared among different organs of government — Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary — placed at the same level.
  • Each organ exercises different powers. The Legislature makes laws, the Executive implements them, and the Judiciary interprets them.
  • This system is called a system of checks and balances because each organ checks the others. No organ can exercise unlimited power.
🧠 Mnemonic: LEJ keeps power in check — Legislature, Executive, Judiciary.

5.2 Vertical Distribution of Power

  • Power is shared among governments at different levels — the Central/Union Government for the entire country, State Governments for regions, and Local Governments (Panchayats and Municipalities).
  • This is also called federal division of power.
  • The Constitution clearly lays down the powers of each level, ensuring no tension between governments.
🧠 Mnemonic: CSL — Country runs Step by Level — Central, State, Local.

5.3 Power Sharing Among Social Groups

  • Power may be shared among different social groups such as religious and linguistic groups.
  • In India, the system of reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, and women is an example of this type of power sharing.
  • This gives space to diverse social groups who otherwise would feel alienated from the government.
  • The Community Government in Belgium is another example.

5.4 Power Sharing Among Political Parties, Pressure Groups, and Movements

  • In a democracy, power is shared among different political parties that represent different ideologies and social groups.
  • Competition among parties ensures that power does not remain in one hand.
  • Sometimes, parties form coalition governments when no single party gets a majority.
  • Pressure groups like trade unions, business associations, and farmer organisations also influence decision-making and share power.
🧠 Mnemonic: HVSP — How Very Smart Politics works — Horizontal, Vertical, Social groups, Political parties.

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Terms:
  • Power Sharing: Distribution of political authority among institutions, levels, or groups
  • Majoritarianism: Belief that the majority community should rule however it wants
  • Accommodation: Recognising and protecting interests of multiple groups
  • Community Government: Elected by one language community to handle cultural/educational issues (Belgium)
  • Horizontal Distribution: Sharing among Legislature, Executive, Judiciary
  • Vertical Division: Sharing among Central, State, Local governments
  • Prudential Reason: Practical benefit — avoids conflict, ensures stability
  • Moral Reason: Democratic principle — participation, consent, legitimacy
🎯 Key Comparisons:
  • Belgium: Accommodation → equal representation, community government, stability
  • Sri Lanka: Majoritarianism → Sinhala-only, Buddhism favoured, civil war
🎯 Four Forms of Power Sharing:
  • Horizontal: Legislature, Executive, Judiciary
  • Vertical: Central, State, Local
  • Social Groups: Community government, reserved constituencies
  • Political Parties: Coalition governments, pressure groups
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing Belgium (accommodation) with Sri Lanka (majoritarianism)
  • Forgetting that 1956 Act made Sinhala the only official language in Sri Lanka
  • Mixing up prudential reason (practical) with moral reason (democratic principle)
  • Not mentioning Community Government when discussing Belgium's power sharing model
  • Confusing horizontal (among organs) with vertical (among levels) power sharing
