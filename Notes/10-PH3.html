Electricity High Weightage Chapter

Complete detailed notes covering electric current, potential difference, Ohm's law, resistance, resistors in series and parallel, heating effect of electric current, Joule's law, electric power, and previous year numericals with solved examples.

Why this chapter is important? Every year 4-6 questions appear including numerical problems on Ohm's law, equivalent resistance in series/parallel, heat produced, and electric power calculation. This chapter has the highest weightage for numericals in board exams.

1. Electric Current and Charge

Electric Current (I): Flow of electric charge through a conductor. Current flows from positive to negative terminal (conventional direction).

📐 Formula

I = Q / t
Where: I = Current (Amperes/A), Q = Charge (Coulombs/C), t = Time (seconds/s)
1 Ampere = 1 Coulomb / 1 second

Potential Difference (V): Work done to move a unit charge from one point to another.

V = W / Q
Unit: Volt (V) = Joule/Coulomb
1 Volt = 1 Joule / 1 Coulomb
⭐ Battery provides potential difference (electromotive force) that drives current through a circuit.

2. Ohm's Law Most Repeated

At constant temperature, the current (I) flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference (V) across its ends.

V ∝ I → V = I × R
Where R = Resistance (Ohms/Ω)
📌 Important: Ohm's law is valid only for metallic conductors at constant temperature. Does NOT apply to semiconductors, diodes, or electrolytes.
🔢 Solved Example: A current of 0.5 A flows through a resistor when a potential difference of 10 V is applied. Calculate the resistance.
Solution: R = V/I = 10/0.5 = 20 Ω

3. Resistance and Resistivity

Resistance (R): Property of a conductor to oppose the flow of current. Depends on:

  • Length (l): R ∝ l (longer wire → more resistance)
  • Area (A): R ∝ 1/A (thicker wire → less resistance)
  • Material (ρ): Resistivity (nature of material)
R = ρ × l / A
Where ρ (rho) = Resistivity (Ω·m), l = length (m), A = cross-sectional area (m²)

Resistivity (ρ): Resistance of a conductor of unit length and unit area of cross-section.
Factors affecting resistivity: Nature of material and temperature. Does NOT depend on length or area.

⭐ Good conductors (Cu, Al) have low resistivity (~1.6 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m). Insulators (Rubber, Glass) have very high resistivity (>10¹² Ω·m).

4. Resistors in Series and Parallel Board Favourite

🔗 Series Combination

Resistors connected end-to-end. Same current flows through all resistors.

Rs = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...
V = V1 + V2 + V3 (Voltage divides)
Current I is same throughout
Example: Three resistors 2Ω, 3Ω, 5Ω in series. Equivalent resistance = 2+3+5 = 10Ω

🔀 Parallel Combination

Resistors connected across the same two points. Same voltage across all resistors.

1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...
I = I1 + I2 + I3 (Current divides)
Voltage V is same across all
Example: Two resistors 4Ω and 6Ω in parallel.
1/Rp = 1/4 + 1/6 = (3+2)/12 = 5/12 → Rp = 2.4Ω
⚠️ Common Mistake: For parallel combination, Rp is always less than the smallest individual resistance. Don't forget to invert after adding fractions!

5. Heating Effect of Electric Current (Joule's Law)

When current flows through a resistor, electrical energy is converted into heat energy.

H = I² × R × t
Where: H = Heat produced (Joules), I = Current (A), R = Resistance (Ω), t = Time (s)

Alternative formulas (using Ohm's law):
H = V × I × t or H = (V²/R) × t

Applications of Heating Effect:

  • Electric Iron, Heater, Toaster: Nichrome alloy used (high resistivity, high melting point)
  • Electric Bulb: Tungsten filament (high melting point, glows at high temperature)
  • Fuse Wire: Low melting point alloy — melts on overloading, protecting circuit
🔢 Solved Example: A current of 2 A flows through a 10 Ω resistor for 5 minutes. Calculate heat produced.
Solution: t = 5 × 60 = 300 s
H = I²Rt = (2)² × 10 × 300 = 4 × 10 × 300 = 12,000 J

6. Electric Power

Electric Power (P): Rate at which electrical energy is consumed or dissipated.

P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R
Unit: Watt (W) = Joule/second
1 kilowatt (kW) = 1000 W

Electrical Energy Consumed: E = P × t

Commercial Unit: Kilowatt-hour (kWh)
1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
1 kWh = 1 unit (electricity bill calculation)
🔢 Solved Example: A 100 W bulb is used for 10 hours daily. Calculate energy consumed in kWh and cost at ₹5 per unit.
Solution:
Energy = (100/1000) kW × 10 h = 1 kWh per day
Monthly consumption (30 days) = 30 kWh
Cost = 30 × ₹5 = ₹150
⭐ A device with higher power consumes more energy in the same time. Power rating is usually written on electrical appliances (e.g., 60W bulb, 2000W heater).

7. Most Repeated Previous Year Numericals (2020-2025)

Top Board Numericals

  1. Calculate the resistance of a copper wire of length 2 m and area 0.5 mm². Resistivity of copper = 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m.
    Answer: R = ρl/A = (1.6×10⁻⁸ × 2) / (0.5×10⁻⁶) = 0.064 Ω
  2. Three resistors 2Ω, 3Ω, and 5Ω are connected in series. Find equivalent resistance and current when connected to a 10V battery.
    Answer: Rₛ = 2+3+5=10Ω; I = V/R = 10/10 = 1A
  3. Two resistors 4Ω and 6Ω are connected in parallel. Calculate equivalent resistance and total current when 12V is applied.
    Answer: 1/Rₚ = 1/4+1/6=5/12 → Rₚ=2.4Ω; I = V/R = 12/2.4 = 5A
  4. A 100W, 200V bulb is connected to a 200V supply. Calculate the current drawn and resistance of the bulb.
    Answer: I = P/V = 100/200 = 0.5A; R = V/I = 200/0.5 = 400Ω
  5. An electric iron consumes energy at 1500W. How many units does it consume in 2 hours?
    Answer: Energy = (1500/1000)kW × 2h = 3 kWh = 3 units
  6. A current of 2A passes through a resistor of 5Ω for 10 minutes. Calculate heat produced.
    Answer: H = I²Rt = 4 × 5 × 600 = 12,000 J

🎯 Quick Exam Tips:
• In series: Current same, Voltage divides, Equivalent R increases
• In parallel: Voltage same, Current divides, Equivalent R decreases
• Fuse wire has low melting point → melts on overloading
• Tungsten used in bulbs → high melting point, glows without melting
• Nichrome used in heaters → high resistivity, does not oxidize easily
