Forest and Wildlife Resources High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains India's rich biodiversity, the threats it faces, and the conservation efforts made to protect it. It covers forest classification, species categories, Project Tiger, and the crucial role of local communities in conservation. Board exams frequently test biodiversity importance, depletion causes, and conservation strategies.

1.1 What is Biodiversity?

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life forms on Earth, encompassing variation at the ecosystem, species, and genetic levels.

Tropical regions are particularly rich in biodiversity — they cover only 10% of the Earth's surface but host 90% of the world's species.

1.2 India's Rich Biodiversity

India is one of the world's richest countries in terms of biological diversity. It is home to a wide variety of flora and fauna, many of which are unique to specific regions. The number of species found in India is estimated to be two to three times the number that has been formally discovered so far.

💡 Why is biodiversity important for human lives?
  • Agriculture: A variety of plant species meet our food needs and provide genetic diversity for crop resilience
  • Business and Industry: Biodiversity supplies raw materials and inspires innovations in medicine and biotechnology
  • Leisure Activities: Nature tourism, wildlife viewing, and recreation
  • Ecological Services: Pollination, nutrient cycling, pest control, clean air, water, and soil

2. Flora and Fauna Categories (IUCN Classification)

The International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources (IUCN) classifies species into different categories based on their population status:

CategoryDefinitionExamples
Normal SpeciesPopulations are at healthy levels for survivalCattle, sal, pine
Endangered SpeciesIn danger of extinction; if negative factors continue, survival is difficultBlack buck, Indian rhino, lion-tailed macaque
Vulnerable SpeciesPopulation has declined so much that they may become endangered soonBlue sheep, Gangetic dolphin
Rare SpeciesSmall population; can move into endangered or vulnerable category if negative factors persistHimalayan brown bear, desert fox
Endemic SpeciesFound only in specific geographical areas, isolated by natural barriersAndaman teal, Nicobar pigeon
Extinct SpeciesNo longer exist anywhereAsiatic cheetah, pink-headed duck
🎯 Quick Distinction:
  • Endangered: High risk of extinction (black buck)
  • Vulnerable: Declined, likely to become endangered (blue sheep)
  • Rare: Small population, restricted area (Himalayan brown bear)
  • Endemic: Found only in one specific area (Nicobar pigeon)

3. Causes of Depletion of Flora and Fauna

Human activities have caused massive depletion of India's forest and wildlife resources. The major causes are:

Human ActivityImpact on Biodiversity
Agricultural ExpansionConversion of forest land into agricultural areas; shifting cultivation
Large-Scale Development ProjectsRiver valley projects, roads, and dams submerge forests and fragment habitats
Mining and QuarryingIllegal mining destroys forests, causes ecological damage, and blocks migration routes
Commercial ForestryExtensive tree felling for timber, paper, and other products
Poaching and Illegal Wildlife TradeHunting for skins, bones, tusks, horns; trade in traditional medicines
OvergrazingDamage to young shoots and seedlings, prevents natural regeneration
Environmental PollutionIndustrial effluents, chemicals, and wastes poison water bodies and soil
Forest FiresOften induced by shifting cultivation, wiping out valuable forests and wildlife
⚠️ Case Study — Buxa Tiger Reserve (West Bengal): Dolomite mining has caused ecological damage, blocked migration routes of several species, and disturbed the natural habitat of many species.

3.1 Development Projects — A Major Threat

  • Since 1951, over 5,000 sq km of forests have been cleared for river valley projects
  • Clearing for the Narmada Sagar Project would inundate 40,000 hectares of forest
  • Multi-purpose projects have led to social movements like Narmada Bachao Andolan and Tehri Dam Andolan
💡 Human-Wildlife Conflict: As human populations grow and expand into forest areas, conflicts between humans and animals increase, threatening both human safety and animal survival.

4. Types and Distribution of Forests in India

The Forest Department of India manages forest resources under three broad categories:

CategoryDescriptionShare
Reserved ForestsRegarded as the most valuable for conservation of forest and wildlife resources; strictly protectedMore than half of total forest land
Protected ForestsProtected from any further depletion by the Forest DepartmentAlmost one-third of total forest area
Unclassed ForestsOther forests and wastelands belonging to government, communities, and private individualsRemaining area
🎯 Memory Trick:
  • Reserved: Most valuable — "Reserve the best"
  • Protected: Stop further damage — "Protect from depletion"
  • Unclassed: Everything else — "No class, all owners"

Permanent Forest Estates: Reserved and Protected forests together are called permanent forest estates. They are maintained for protective purposes and for producing timber and other forest products.

5. Conservation of Forest and Wildlife in India

5.1 Why Conserve?

  • Conservation preserves ecological diversity and our life-support systems — water, air, and soil
  • It preserves genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth and breeding
  • Agriculture still depends on traditional crop varieties; fisheries depend on aquatic biodiversity
  • Many species can move into vulnerable and endangered categories if protection is not given

5.2 Government Conservation Measures

MeasureYearKey Features
Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act1972Legal protection to wildlife and habitats; bans hunting; restricts wildlife trade; establishes national parks and sanctuaries
Project Tiger1973Tiger conservation through habitat protection and anti-poaching measures
Wildlife Acts1980, 1986Added butterflies, moths, beetles, and one dragonfly to protected species list
Plant Protection1991For the first time, six plant species were added to the conservation list
National Wildlife Action Plan2002Guidelines for wildlife conservation and habitat restoration

5.3 Species-Specific Projects

The central government announced several projects for protecting specific animals that were gravely threatened:

  • Tiger
  • One-horned rhinoceros
  • Kashmir stag (Hangul)
  • Three types of crocodiles — freshwater crocodile, saltwater crocodile, and Gharial
  • Asiatic lion
  • Indian elephant, black buck, snow leopard (partial or full legal protection)

5.4 Protected Areas

TypeDescriptionExample
National ParksFully protected areas; human activity restrictedJim Corbett National Park (Uttarakhand)
Wildlife SanctuariesNo hunting allowed; some human activity permittedSariska Wildlife Sanctuary (Rajasthan)
Biosphere ReservesZones for conservation and researchNilgiri Biosphere Reserve

6. Project Tiger — A Landmark Conservation Effort

6.1 Background

Project Tiger was launched in 1973 and was one of the most well-publicized wildlife campaigns in the world.

6.2 Why Was Project Tiger Needed?

  • By 1973, the tiger population had declined to 1,827 from an estimated 55,000 at the beginning of the century
  • Tigers faced threats from:
    • Poaching for trade in skins and bones (used in traditional medicines, especially in Asian countries)
    • Shrinking habitat due to growing human population
    • Depletion of prey base species

6.3 Objectives of Project Tiger

  • Not only an effort to save tigers but also to preserve biotypes of sizeable magnitude
  • Protecting tiger habitats and the prey species on which tigers depend
  • Conserving the wider ecological system and its biodiversity

7. Community and Conservation

Local communities across India have played a vital role in conserving forests and wildlife through traditions, movements, and direct action.

7.1 Sacred Groves

Sacred Groves are patches of forest that have been left untouched by local communities due to religious beliefs. They are considered the "forests of God and Goddesses."

  • Any interference with them is banned
  • They preserve several virgin forests in pristine form
  • Examples: Mundas and Santhals worship mahua and kadamba trees; tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship tamarind and mango trees during weddings
  • Peepal and banyan trees are considered sacred by many

7.2 Bishnoi Villages

In and around Bishnoi villages in Rajasthan, herds of blackbuck (chinkara), nilgai, and peacocks can be seen as an integral part of the community. Nobody harms them due to the Bishnoi community's strong tradition of protecting wildlife.

7.3 Chipko Movement

The Chipko Movement started in the 1970s in the Kumaon region (Uttarakhand).

  • Villagers, especially women, hugged trees to prevent them from being cut down
  • Successfully resisted deforestation in several areas
  • Demonstrated that community afforestation with indigenous species can be enormously successful
  • Highlighted the link between forests and livelihoods

7.4 Sariska Tiger Reserve

In Sariska Tiger Reserve, Rajasthan, villagers have fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act. They have actively opposed government involvement in favor of protecting their habitats.

7.5 Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'

The inhabitants of five villages in Alwar district, Rajasthan have declared 1,200 hectares of forest as the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'.

  • They have established their own set of rules and regulations
  • Hunting is prohibited
  • Wildlife is protected against any outside encroachments

7.6 Beej Bachao Andolan

The Beej Bachao Andolan in Tehri and Navdanya has promoted the use of diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals. Farmers and citizen groups demonstrated that sufficient crop harvest can be produced without chemical fertilizers.

8. Joint Forest Management (JFM)

8.1 What is JFM?

Joint Forest Management (JFM) is a programme that involves local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests.

8.2 How JFM Works

  • Local (village) institutions undertake protection activities on degraded forest land managed by the Forest Department
  • In return, these communities are entitled to intermediary benefits like non-timber forest products and a share in the timber harvested through successful protection

8.3 History of JFM

  • The programme has been in formal existence since 1988
  • Odisha was the first state to pass a resolution for Joint Forest Management
🎯 Key Lesson from India's Experience:

Local communities everywhere must be involved in natural resource management. We should accept only those economic or developmental activities that are people-centric, environment-friendly, and economically rewarding.

Quick Revision - At a Glance

  • Biodiversity: Variety of life forms at ecosystem, species, and genetic levels
  • Endangered: High risk of extinction (black buck)
  • Vulnerable: Declined, likely to become endangered (blue sheep)
  • Endemic: Found only in specific areas (Nicobar pigeon)
  • Sacred Groves: Forests protected by communities for religious reasons
  • JFM: Joint Forest Management — communities manage degraded forests with Forest Department
🎯 Key Dates & Facts:
  • 1972: Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act
  • 1973: Project Tiger launched
  • 1980, 1986: Wildlife Acts added insects to protected list
  • 1988: Joint Forest Management formally started (Odisha first)
  • 1991: Six plant species added to protection list
🎯 Three Forest Categories:
  • Reserved: Most valuable, strictly protected
  • Protected: Protected from further depletion
  • Unclassed: Government, community, or private ownership
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing "Endangered" with "Vulnerable" — Endangered is more severe
  • Forgetting that Reserved Forests are the most valuable for conservation
  • Mixing up the year of Project Tiger (1973) with Wildlife Protection Act (1972)
  • Not giving state-specific examples when asked about community conservation
  • Confusing Joint Forest Management (JFM) with Chipko Movement
