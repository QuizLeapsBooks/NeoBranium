Minerals and Energy Resources High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains the types of minerals found in India, their distribution, and the energy resources that power our economy. It covers ferrous and non-ferrous minerals, conventional and non-conventional energy sources, and the urgent need for conservation. Board exams frequently test mineral types, iron ore varieties, coal distribution, and the advantages of non-conventional energy.

1.1 What is a Mineral?

A mineral is a homogeneous, naturally occurring substance with a definable internal structure. Minerals are found in varied forms in nature, ranging from the hardest diamond to the softest talc.

1.2 Why Are Minerals Important?

Minerals are an indispensable part of our lives. Almost everything we use, from a tiny pin to a towering building or a big ship, is made from minerals. Even the food we eat contains minerals. Life processes cannot occur without minerals.

🎯 Key Point: Minerals are non-renewable and take millions of years to form. They are valuable and short-lived possessions of a country.

2. Occurrence of Minerals

Minerals generally occur in the following forms:

FormDescriptionExamples
Veins and LodesIn igneous and metamorphic rocks, minerals occur in cracks, crevices, faults, or joints. Smaller occurrences are veins; larger are lodes.Tin, copper, zinc, lead
Beds and LayersIn sedimentary rocks, minerals occur in beds or layers formed by deposition, accumulation, and concentration.Coal, iron ore, gypsum, potash, salt
Decomposition of RocksSoluble contents are removed, leaving a residual mass of weathered material.Bauxite
Placer DepositsMinerals occur as alluvial deposits in sands of valley floors and base of hills. Minerals not corroded by water.Gold, silver, tin, platinum
Ocean WatersVast quantities of minerals are dissolved in ocean water.Common salt, magnesium, bromine
💡 Rat-Hole Mining: In Jowai and Cherrapunji (Meghalaya), coal mining is done by family members in the form of long narrow tunnels, known as rat-hole mining.

3. Ferrous Minerals

Ferrous minerals are metallic minerals that contain iron. They account for about three-fourths of the total value of production of metallic minerals in India and provide a strong base for the development of metallurgical industries.

3.1 Iron Ore

Iron ore is the basic mineral and the backbone of industrial development. India is endowed with fairly abundant resources of iron ore and is rich in good quality iron ores.

TypeIron ContentCharacteristics
MagnetiteUp to 70%Finest iron ore, excellent magnetic qualities, especially valuable in the electrical industry
Hematite50-60%Most important industrial iron ore in terms of quantity used, slightly lower iron content

Distribution: In 2018-19, almost the entire production of iron ore (97%) accrued from Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka, and Jharkhand. The remaining 3% came from other states.

Important Belts:

  • Odisha: Gorumahisani, Badampahar (Mayurbhanj)
  • Jharkhand: Singhbhum, Noamundi
  • Chhattisgarh: Dalli-Rajhara, Bailadila (Bastar)
  • Karnataka: Kudremukh (Chikmagalur), Bellary
  • Ballari-Chitradurga-Chikkamagaluru-Tumakuru belt: Large reserves; Kudremukh mines are a 100% export unit

3.2 Manganese

Manganese is used in the manufacture of steel and ferro-manganese alloy. It is also used in manufacturing bleaching powder, insecticides, and paints.

Odisha is the largest producer of manganese in India.

4. Non-Ferrous Minerals

Non-ferrous minerals are metallic minerals that do not contain iron. Examples include copper, bauxite, lead, zinc, and gold.

4.1 Copper

Copper is a ductile metal and a good conductor, making it ideal for making electric wires. It is also used in the electronics and chemical industries.

Distribution: Rajasthan (Khetri), Jharkhand (Singhbhum), Madhya Pradesh (Balaghat).

4.2 Bauxite

Bauxite is a rock consisting of aluminium oxides. Aluminium is light, resistant to corrosion, and combines the strength of metals such as iron. Odisha is the leading state producing bauxite (Koraput, Sambalpur).

🎯 Quick Reference:
  • Iron Ore: Backbone of industrial development; Magnetite (70% iron) and Hematite (50-60%)
  • Manganese: Used in steel; Odisha largest producer
  • Copper: Electric wires; Khetri (Rajasthan)
  • Bauxite: Aluminium ore; Odisha leading producer

5. Non-Metallic Minerals

5.1 Mica

Mica is a non-conductor of electricity. Due to its excellent di-electric strength, low power loss factor, insulating properties, and resistance to high voltage, mica is indispensable for the electrical and electronics industries.

Distribution: The Koderma-Gaya-Hazaribagh belt in Jharkhand is an important mica-producing region. India is the leading producer of mica.

5.2 Limestone

Limestone is a key raw material for the cement industry. It is found in Andhra Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh.

🎯 Mica — The Indispensable Mineral: Mica's electrical insulating properties make it essential for electronics. Koderma (Jharkhand) is the leading producer.

6. Conventional Sources of Energy

Conventional sources have been in use for a long time. They include firewood, cattle dung cake, coal, petroleum, natural gas, and electricity (thermal and hydel).

6.1 Coal

Coal is the most abundantly available fossil fuel in India. It is used for power generation, industry, and domestic needs.

Coal occurs in India in two main geological ages:

TypeAgeLocation
Gondwana CoalOver 200 million years oldDamodar Valley (Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro), Godavari, Mahanadi, Son, Wardha valleys
Tertiary CoalAbout 55 million years oldNorth-Eastern states

Types of Coal: Anthracite (highest carbon content), Bituminous (widely used), Lignite (low-grade brown coal, Neyveli in Tamil Nadu).

6.2 Petroleum

Petroleum is the lifeline of modern transportation. It is found in Assam, Gujarat, Mumbai High, and the Krishna-Godavari basin.

6.3 Natural Gas

Natural gas is a clean and efficient energy source. It is found in Assam, Gujarat, and offshore areas of Krishna-Godavari.

6.4 Electricity

Electricity is generated mainly through thermal power (using coal) and hydroelectric power (using water). It is a crucial input for industry, agriculture, and domestic use.

7. Non-Conventional Energy Sources

Non-conventional sources are renewable, pollution-free, and have been identified in recent times. They include solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas, and atomic energy.

7.1 Solar Energy

India is a tropical country with abundant sunshine. Solar energy is inexhaustible, freely available, and eco-friendly. Photovoltaic technology converts sunlight directly into electricity.

Solar-rich states: Rajasthan, Gujarat.

India's largest solar power facility is at Madhapur near Bhuj.

💡 Why Solar Energy Has a Bright Future:
  • Abundant sunshine in India
  • Inexhaustible and free
  • Cheaper in the long run
  • Reduces dependence on firewood and dung cakes in rural areas
  • Environment-friendly

7.2 Wind Energy

Wind energy is generated by harnessing the power of wind. India is the second largest country in wind power generation.

Major wind energy producers: Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Gujarat.

7.3 Biogas

Biogas is produced from organic waste and is widely used in rural areas. Gobar gas plants provide twin benefits: energy and manure.

7.4 Nuclear or Atomic Energy

Nuclear energy is obtained by altering the structure of atoms. The fuel used is uranium and thorium. Monazite sands of Kerala are rich in thorium.

Nuclear power plants: Kota (Rajasthan), Narora (Uttar Pradesh), Kalpakkam (Tamil Nadu).

7.5 Tidal and Geothermal Energy

Tidal energy: Generated by exploiting tidal waves; yet to be tapped due to lack of cost-effective technology.

Geothermal energy: Heat energy generated and stored under the Earth's surface.

🎯 Conventional vs Non-Conventional:
  • Conventional: Exhaustible, polluting, expensive, long transmission distances (coal, petroleum, natural gas)
  • Non-Conventional: Inexhaustible, pollution-free, low expenditure, local use (solar, wind, biogas, tidal)

8. Conservation of Minerals and Energy Resources

8.1 Why Conserve Minerals?

  • Minerals are finite and non-renewable — they take millions of years to form
  • The rate of replenishment is very slow compared to consumption
  • Continued extraction leads to increasing costs as they are taken from greater depths with decreasing quality
  • Minerals are valuable and short-lived possessions of a country

8.2 Methods of Conserving Minerals

  • Minerals must be used in a planned and sustainable manner
  • Improved technologies should be evolved to use low-grade ores at low costs
  • Recycling of metals, using scrap metals and other substitutes
  • Better mining methods to reduce wastage

8.3 Why Conserve Energy?

  • Energy is a basic requirement for economic development
  • Every sector — agriculture, industry, transport, commerce — needs greater inputs of energy
  • Growing consumption has made India increasingly dependent on fossil fuels
  • Rising prices and potential shortages of oil and gas create uncertainty
  • Increasing use of fossil fuels causes serious environmental degradation

8.4 Ways to Conserve Energy

  • Use public transport instead of individual vehicles
  • Switch off electrical devices when not in use
  • Use power-saving devices
  • Use non-conventional sources like solar and wind energy
  • Get power equipment regularly checked to detect damages and leakages
🎯 "Energy Saved is Energy Produced": This statement emphasizes that conserving energy is as important as generating it. By using energy efficiently, we reduce the need for additional power generation and conserve our limited resources for future generations.

8.5 Sustainable Development

Sustainable development practices balance current needs with the well-being of future generations. Using renewable energy, adopting eco-friendly mining, and reducing waste are essential.

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Terms:
  • Mineral: Homogeneous, naturally occurring substance with definable internal structure
  • Ferrous Minerals: Contain iron (iron ore, manganese)
  • Non-Ferrous Minerals: Do not contain iron (copper, bauxite)
  • Veins: Smaller occurrences in igneous/metamorphic rocks
  • Lodes: Larger occurrences in igneous/metamorphic rocks
  • Placer Deposits: Alluvial deposits (gold, silver, tin)
🎯 Key Minerals Quick Reference:
  • Iron Ore: Magnetite (70% iron, best), Hematite (50-60%, most used)
  • Manganese: Used in steel; Odisha largest producer
  • Copper: Electric wires; Khetri (Rajasthan)
  • Bauxite: Aluminium ore; Odisha leading
  • Mica: Electrical insulator; Koderma (Jharkhand)
  • Coal: Damodar Valley (Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro)
🎯 Energy Sources Quick Reference:
  • Conventional: Coal, petroleum, natural gas, firewood
  • Non-Conventional: Solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas, nuclear
  • Solar: Rajasthan, Gujarat; Madhapur (largest facility)
  • Wind: Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Gujarat
  • Nuclear: Kota, Narora, Kalpakkam
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing Magnetite (finest, 70% iron, magnetic) with Hematite (most used, 50-60% iron)
  • Forgetting that Odisha is the largest producer of iron ore, not Jharkhand
  • Mixing up Koderma (mica) with Khetri (copper)
  • Not mentioning rat-hole mining when asked about coal mining in Meghalaya
  • Confusing conventional (exhaustible) with non-conventional (renewable) sources
  • Forgetting that Monazite sands of Kerala contain Thorium, not Uranium
