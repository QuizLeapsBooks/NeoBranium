Chapter 1: Chemical Reactions and Equations

A study of how chemicals interact and transform through balanced equations.

Key Concepts

  • Chemical Equation: Representation of a reaction using symbols. It must be balanced to satisfy the Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • Types of Reactions:
    • Combination: Two or more reactants form one product (A + B → AB).
    • Decomposition: A single reactant breaks down into two or more products (AB → A + B). Types: Thermal, Electrolytic, Photolytic.
    • Displacement: A more reactive element displaces a less reactive one (A + BC → AC + B).
    • Double Displacement: Exchange of ions between two compounds (AB + CD → AD + CB).
    • Redox: Simultaneous Oxidation (gain of oxygen/loss of electrons) and Reduction (loss of oxygen/gain of electrons).
  • Corrosion: Degradation of metals by air/moisture (e.g., Rusting of iron).
  • Rancidity: Oxidation of fats and oils in food, leading to bad smell/taste.

Important Definitions

  • Precipitate: An insoluble solid that emerges from a liquid solution.
  • Exothermic Reaction: Heat is released along with products (e.g., Respiration).
  • Endothermic Reaction: Heat is absorbed to carry out the reaction (e.g., Photosynthesis).

Important Equations to Remember

  • Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 12H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O
  • Rusting: 4Fe + 3O2 + xH2O → 2Fe2O3.xH2O

Real-life Examples

  • Burning of Natural Gas: An exothermic reaction that provides energy.
  • Digestion: Decomposition of complex food molecules into simpler ones.

Common Mistakes

When balancing equations, NEVER change the subscript (the small number) of a molecule. Only change the coefficient (the number in front).

Important Questions

  1. Why should a magnesium ribbon be cleaned before burning in air?
  2. Define Redox reaction with an example. Identify the substance oxidized and reduced.
  3. What happens when dilute hydrochloric acid is added to iron filings?

Chapter 2: Acids, Bases and Salts

Understanding the chemical properties of acids and bases and the magic of the pH scale.

Key Concepts

  • Acids: Sour taste, turn blue litmus red, release H+ ions.
  • Bases: Bitter taste, soapy touch, turn red litmus blue, release OH- ions.
  • pH Scale: Ranges from 0 to 14.
    • 0-7: Acidic
    • 7: Neutral (Pure Water)
    • 7-14: Basic
  • Neutralization: Acid + Base → Salt + Water.
  • Chlor-alkali Process: Electrolysis of brine (NaCl) to produce Chlorine, Hydrogen, and NaOH.

Important Definitions

  • Olfactory Indicators: Substances whose odor changes in acidic or basic media (e.g., Onion, Vanilla).
  • Water of Crystallization: Fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt (e.g., CuSO4.5H2O).
  • Universal Indicator: A mixture of several indicators that shows different colors at different concentrations of hydrogen ions.

Important Salts and Formulas

  • Baking Soda: NaHCO3 (Sodium Hydrogen Carbonate)
  • Washing Soda: Na2CO3.10H2O
  • Plaster of Paris: CaSO4.½H2O (Calcium Sulphate Hemihydrate)
  • Bleaching Powder: CaOCl2

Real-life Examples

  • Antacids: Milk of Magnesia used to cure acidity (Neutralization).
  • Tooth Decay: Starts when mouth pH is lower than 5.5.

Common Mistakes

Always add Acid to Water slowly with stirring, NEVER Water to Acid. Adding water to acid can cause the mixture to splash out due to extreme heat.

Important Questions

  1. Why does dry HCl gas not change the color of dry litmus paper?
  2. What is the 'common name' of Calcium Sulphate Hemihydrate? Why should it be stored in moisture-proof containers?
  3. Fresh milk has a pH of 6. How does its pH change as it turns into curd?

Chapter 3: Metals and Non-metals

The world of elements, their reactivity, and how we extract them for use.

Key Concepts

  • Properties: Metals are malleable, ductile, and good conductors. Non-metals are usually brittle and insulators.
  • Chemical Properties:
    • Metals react with oxygen to form Basic Oxides.
    • Amphoteric Oxides: React with both acids and bases (e.g., Al2O3).
  • Reactivity Series: A list of metals arranged in decreasing order of reactivity (K > Na > Ca > ... > Au).
  • Ionic Compounds: Formed by transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal. They have high melting points and conduct electricity in molten state.
  • Metallurgy: Processes like Roasting (heating in excess air) and Calcination (heating in limited air).

Important Definitions

  • Malleability: Ability of metals to be beaten into thin sheets.
  • Ductility: Ability of metals to be drawn into wires (Gold is the most ductile).
  • Aqua Regia: Freshly prepared mixture of conc. HCl and conc. HNO3 in 3:1 ratio (can dissolve gold).
  • Alloy: Homogeneous mixture of two or more metals or a metal and a non-metal (e.g., Steel, Brass).

Extraction Step-by-Step

  • Enrichment: Removing impurities (gangue) from ore.
  • Reduction: Converting metal oxides into metal using carbon or electrolysis.
  • Refining: Electrolytic refining to get 99.9% pure metal.

Common Mistakes

Iron is a metal, but it doesn't react with cold or hot water—it only reacts with Steam to form oxide and hydrogen.

Important Questions

  1. What are amphoteric oxides? Give two examples.
  2. Explain the process of electrolytic refining of Copper with a diagram description.
  3. Why are ionic compounds solid and have high melting points?

Chapter 4: Carbon and its Compounds

Exploring the versatile nature of carbon and the foundation of organic life.

Key Concepts

  • Covalent Bonding: Sharing of electrons between atoms. Carbon always forms 4 bonds (Tetravalency).
  • Versatile Nature: Due to Catenation (self-linking) and Tetravalency, carbon forms millions of compounds.
  • Hydrocarbons:
    • Saturated: Alkanes (CnH2n+2) - Single bonds.
    • Unsaturated: Alkenes (double bond) and Alkynes (triple bond).
  • Homologous Series: Group of compounds with same functional group where adjacent members differ by -CH2 group.
  • Soaps vs Detergents: Soaps are sodium salts of long-chain fatty acids (don't work in hard water). Detergents work in both soft and hard water.

Important Definitions

  • Isomers: Compounds with same molecular formula but different structural formulas.
  • Esterification: Reaction between alcohol and carboxylic acid to form a sweet-smelling ester.
  • Saponification: Alkaline hydrolysis of an ester to form soap and alcohol.

Chemical Properties

  • Combustion: Carbon compounds burn in oxygen to give CO2, H2O, and heat.
  • Addition Reaction: Unsaturated hydrocarbons become saturated (e.g., Hydrogenation of vegetable oils).

Real-life Examples

  • Ethanol (Alcohol): Used as a solvent and in medicines like Cough Syrup.
  • Vinegar: 5-8% solution of Ethanoic Acid in water.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse Ethane (C2H6) with Ethene (C2H4). Always count the bonds—carbon must ALWAYS have exactly 4 lines around it!

Important Questions

  1. What is a homologous series? State its any two characteristics.
  2. Explain the mechanism of the cleaning action of soaps (Micelle formation).
  3. Why does carbon form compounds mainly by covalent bonding?

Chapter 1: Life Processes

The essential functions like nutrition, respiration, and transportation that keep us alive.

Key Concepts

  • Nutrition:
    • Autotrophic: Making food from inorganic sources (Photosynthesis).
    • Heterotrophic: Depending on others (Holozoic, Saprophytic, Parasitic).
  • Respiration: Breaking down food to release energy.
    • Aerobic: In presence of oxygen (yields more ATP).
    • Anaerobic: In absence of oxygen (e.g., in yeast or muscle cells).
  • Transportation:
    • Human: Heart acts as a pump; Double Circulation ensures efficiency.
    • Plants: Xylem (Water) and Phloem (Food/Translocation).
  • Excretion: Removal of metabolic waste. The Nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney.

Important Definitions

  • Villi: Finger-like projections in the small intestine that increase surface area for absorption.
  • Translocation: Transport of soluble products of photosynthesis through phloem.
  • Hemodialysis: Artificial removal of waste from blood when kidneys fail.

Real-life Examples

  • Cramps: Caused by the buildup of Lactic Acid during sudden heavy exercise (anaerobic respiration).
  • Heart Valve: Prevents backflow of blood, ensuring one-way flow.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse Photosynthesis (food making) with Respiration (energy releasing). Plants do BOTH!

Important Questions

  1. How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?
  2. What is the role of acid in our stomach?
  3. Describe double circulation in human beings. Why is it necessary?

Chapter 2: Control and Coordination

How our body talks to itself and responds to the environment.

Key Concepts

  • Nervous System: Composed of neurons. Signals travel as electrical impulses across a Synapse.
  • Human Brain:
    • Forebrain (Cerebrum): Thinking, memory, logic.
    • Midbrain & Hindbrain: Involuntary actions (Cerebellum for balance, Medulla for BP/Vomiting).
  • Plant Coordination: Hormones like Auxin (growth), Gibberellins (stem growth), Cytokinins (cell division), and Abscisic Acid (inhibiting growth/wilting).
  • Animal Hormones: Secreted by endocrine glands (Thyroid → Thyroxine, Pancreas → Insulin).

Important Definitions

  • Reflex Action: Sudden, involuntary response to a stimulus (e.g., blinking).
  • Phototropism: Growth of plant parts towards light.
  • Geotropism: Growth of roots towards gravity.

Real-life Examples

  • Adrenaline Rush: The "Fight or Flight" hormone that prepares your body for emergencies.
  • Diabetes: Caused by insufficient production of Insulin by the pancreas.

Common Mistakes

The Spinal Cord handles reflex actions, but the Brain is still informed. Reflexes are faster because they don't wait for "thinking" time.

Important Questions

  1. What is a synapse? What happens at the synapse between two neurons?
  2. Compare and contrast nervous and hormonal mechanisms for control and coordination.
  3. How does the "Touch-me-not" plant (Mimosa pudica) respond to touch?

Chapter 3: How do Organisms Reproduce?

The process of creating new life and ensuring the continuity of species.

Key Concepts

  • Asexual Reproduction: Fission (Amoeba), Budding (Hydra), Spore formation (Rhizopus), Fragmentation (Spirogyra), and Vegetative Propagation.
  • Sexual Reproduction in Plants: Involves Pollen (male) and Ovule (female). Double fertilization leads to seed formation.
  • Human Reproduction:
    • Male: Testes produce sperm and testosterone.
    • Female: Ovaries produce eggs, estrogen, and progesterone. Menstrual cycle occurs if egg isn't fertilized.
  • Reproductive Health: Methods of contraception (Mechanical barriers, Hormonal pills, IUCDs, Surgical methods).

Important Definitions

  • Placenta: Disc-like tissue that provides nutrition and oxygen to the embryo from the mother's blood.
  • Puberty: Period during which reproductive organs become functional and secondary sexual characters appear.
  • DNA Copying: Basic process in reproduction that ensures inheritance with some variation.

Real-life Examples

  • Grafting/Layering: Used in agriculture to grow seedless plants like roses or bananas.
  • IUCD (Copper-T): A contraceptive device placed in the uterus to prevent pregnancy.

Common Mistakes

In flowering plants, Pollination is just the transfer of pollen, whereas Fertilization is the actual fusion of male and female gametes.

Important Questions

  1. What is the importance of DNA copying in reproduction?
  2. How does the embryo get nourishment inside the mother’s body?
  3. What are the different methods of contraception?

Chapter 4: Heredity

Understanding how traits are passed from parents to children through genes.

Key Concepts

  • Mendel’s Contribution: Used pea plants (Pisum sativum) to study inheritance.
    • Law of Dominance: One trait masks the other in the first generation (F1).
    • Monohybrid Cross: Ratio 3:1 (Phenotype) and 1:2:1 (Genotype).
  • Sex Determination: In humans, males have XY and females have XX chromosomes. The father determines the sex of the child.
  • Acquired vs Inherited Traits: Acquired traits (e.g., learning a language) aren't passed on; only genetic changes (Inherited) are.

Important Definitions

  • Gene: A segment of DNA that provides information for one protein/trait.
  • Phenotype: Observable physical characteristics (e.g., tallness).
  • Genotype: Genetic makeup of an organism (e.g., TT or Tt).

Real-life Examples

  • Free/Attached Earlobes: A common example of inherited traits in humans.
  • Identical Twins: Have the same DNA because they come from the same zygote.

Common Mistakes

The mother is NOT responsible for the sex of the baby. Since she only has X chromosomes, the baby's sex depends on whether the father's sperm carries an X or a Y chromosome.

Important Questions

  1. How do Mendel’s experiments show that traits may be dominant or recessive?
  2. How is the sex of the child determined in human beings?
  3. What is a monohybrid cross? Give its phenotypic and genotypic ratios.

Chapter 5: Our Environment

The delicate balance of ecosystems and our impact on the planet.

Key Concepts

  • Ecosystem: Interaction of biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) components.
  • Food Chain & Web: Transfer of energy from one organism to another. Energy flow is unidirectional.
  • 10% Law: Only 10% of energy is transferred to the next trophic level.
  • Ozone Layer: Protects Earth from UV rays. Depleted by CFCs (Chlorofluorocarbons).
  • Waste Management: Reducing plastic, using biodegradable materials, and the 3 Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle).

Important Definitions

  • Biomagnification: Increase in concentration of harmful chemicals (like pesticides) at higher trophic levels.
  • Trophic Level: Each step or level of the food chain.
  • Biodegradable: Substances that can be broken down by bacteria/fungi.

Real-life Examples

  • DDT in Fish: An example of biomagnification where top predators have the highest toxin levels.
  • Global Warming: Warming of Earth due to excessive CO2 trapping heat.

Common Mistakes

The Ozone Layer depletion is NOT the same as Global Warming. Ozone depletion allows UV rays in, while Global Warming traps heat inside.

Important Questions

  1. What are trophic levels? Give an example of a food chain and state the different trophic levels in it.
  2. What is the 10% law in an ecosystem?
  3. What is ozone and how does it affect any ecosystem?

Chapter 1: Light - Reflection and Refraction

The fundamental behavior of light and its interaction with mirrors and lenses.

Key Concepts

  • Reflection: Bouncing back of light. Laws of reflection apply to all surfaces.
  • Spherical Mirrors:
    • Concave: Converging mirror (used in solar furnaces, torches).
    • Convex: Diverging mirror (used as rear-view mirrors).
  • Refraction: Bending of light when passing from one medium to another.
  • Refractive Index: Measure of how much light slows down in a medium (n = c/v).
  • Lenses:
    • Convex: Converging lens.
    • Concave: Diverging lens.

Important Definitions

  • Principal Focus: The point where parallel rays meet (or appear to meet) after reflection/refraction.
  • Power of Lens (P): Reciprocal of focal length (P = 1/f). Unit: Dioptre (D).
  • Magnification (m): Ratio of height of image to height of object.

Important Formulas

  • Mirror Formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f
  • Lens Formula: 1/v - 1/u = 1/f
  • Refractive Index (n₂₁): v₁ / v₂

Real-life Examples

  • Dentist Mirrors: Use concave mirrors to see larger images of teeth.
  • Pencils in Water: Appear bent due to refraction.

Common Mistakes

Always follow the Cartesian Sign Convention. Distance 'u' is ALWAYS negative. For a convex lens, 'f' is positive; for a concave lens, 'f' is negative.

Important Questions

  1. Define the principal focus of a concave mirror.
  2. A ray of light traveling in air enters obliquely into water. Does it bend towards or away from the normal? (Answer: Towards).
  3. What is the power of a lens of focal length -2.0 m? (Answer: -0.5 D).

Chapter 2: The Human Eye and the Colourful World

How we see the world and the beautiful phenomena of light in the atmosphere.

Key Concepts

  • Power of Accommodation: Ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length.
  • Defects of Vision:
    • Myopia: Near-sightedness (corrected by concave lens).
    • Hypermetropia: Far-sightedness (corrected by convex lens).
    • Presbyopia: Old age defect (corrected by bifocal lens).
  • Dispersion: Splitting of white light into VIBGYOR through a prism.
  • Atmospheric Refraction: Twinkling of stars, advanced sunrise/delayed sunset.
  • Scattering (Tyndall Effect): Scattering of light by particles in its path.

Important Definitions

  • Least Distance of Distinct Vision: 25 cm for a normal eye.
  • Persistence of Vision: Image stays on the retina for 1/16th of a second.
  • Spectrum: The band of colored components of a light beam.

Real-life Examples

  • Rainbow: Caused by dispersion, refraction, and internal reflection of sunlight by water droplets.
  • Blue Sky: Blue light has shorter wavelength and is scattered most by air molecules.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse Refraction with Scattering. Stars twinkle because of refraction, but the sky is blue because of scattering.

Important Questions

  1. What is myopia? How can it be corrected?
  2. Why do stars twinkle but planets do not?
  3. Explain the formation of a rainbow with a diagram description.

Chapter 3: Electricity

The flow of charges, circuits, and the thermal effects of electric current.

Key Concepts

  • Ohm's Law: Current (I) is directly proportional to Potential Difference (V) at constant temperature (V = IR).
  • Resistance (R): Property to resist flow of current. Depends on length, area, and material (R = ρl/A).
  • Circuit Connections:
    • Series: Rₛ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ (Current remains same).
    • Parallel: 1/Rₚ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ (Voltage remains same).
  • Heating Effect: H = I²Rt (Joule's Law of Heating).
  • Electric Power: Rate at which electrical energy is consumed (P = VI).

Important Definitions

  • Ampere (A): SI unit of current. Flow of 1 Coulomb of charge per second.
  • Resistivity (ρ): Resistance of a conductor of unit length and unit area of cross-section.
  • Kilowatt-hour (kWh): Commercial unit of electrical energy (1 unit = 3.6 × 10⁶ J).

Important Formulas

  • V = I × R
  • H = I² × R × t
  • P = V × I = I²R = V²/R

Solved Example

Q: A current of 0.5 A is drawn by a filament of an electric bulb for 10 minutes. Find the amount of electric charge that flows through the circuit.

Solution:
I = 0.5 A, t = 10 mins = 600 s
Q = I × t = 0.5 × 600 = 300 C

Real-life Examples

  • Tungsten Filament: Used in bulbs due to high melting point.
  • Fuse Wire: Protects circuits by melting during overloading (Low melting point).

Common Mistakes

While calculating resistance in Parallel, students often forget to invert the final result. If 1/Rp = 1/2, then Rp = 2 Ω.

Important Questions

  1. On what factors does the resistance of a conductor depend?
  2. Why are coils of electric toasters made of an alloy rather than a pure metal?
  3. State Joule's law of heating.

Chapter 4: Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

Understanding how electricity creates magnetism and the principles behind motors and safety.

Key Concepts

  • Magnetic Field: Region around a magnet where force can be detected. Field lines emerge from North and enter South.
  • Right-Hand Thumb Rule: Used to find the direction of magnetic field around a straight conductor.
  • Solenoid: A coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire. Acts like a bar magnet.
  • Fleming's Left-Hand Rule: Used for Electric Motors (Force on a current-carrying conductor).
  • Domestic Circuits: Live wire (Red), Neutral (Black), and Earth (Green). Fuse and Earthing are key safety measures.

Important Definitions

  • Electromagnet: A temporary magnet formed by placing a soft iron core inside a solenoid.
  • Short Circuit: When Live and Neutral wires come in direct contact.
  • Overloading: Connecting too many appliances to a single socket.

Real-life Examples

  • Electric Motor: Converts electrical energy to mechanical energy (used in fans, mixers).
  • Earthing: Protects us from electric shocks by providing a low-resistance path to the ground.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse the Left-Hand Rule (Force/Motor) with the Right-Hand Rule (Induced Current). Remember: Left is for Load (Motor).

Important Questions

  1. Why do two magnetic field lines never intersect each other?
  2. State Fleming’s Left-Hand Rule.
  3. What is the function of an earth wire? Why is it necessary to earth metallic appliances?
