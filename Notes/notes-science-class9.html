Chapter 1: Matter in Our Surroundings

A deep dive into what everything is made of and how temperature/pressure changes its state.

Key Concepts

  • Matter: Anything that has mass and occupies space. It is made up of tiny particles.
  • Characteristics of Particles: They are very small, have spaces between them, are continuously moving, and attract each other.
  • States of Matter:
    • Solid: Fixed shape, fixed volume, high density.
    • Liquid: No fixed shape, fixed volume, can flow.
    • Gas: No fixed shape or volume, highly compressible.
  • Sublimation: Change from solid directly to gas (e.g., Camphor).
  • Evaporation: A surface phenomenon where liquid changes to vapor below its boiling point. Factors affecting it: Surface area, Temperature, Humidity, Wind speed.

Important Definitions

  • Diffusion: The intermixing of particles of two different types of matter on their own.
  • Latent Heat: The heat energy required to change the state of a substance without changing its temperature.
  • Boiling Point: Temperature at which a liquid starts boiling at atmospheric pressure (Water: 100°C / 373K).

Important Formulas

Temperature Conversion: K = °C + 273.15

Real-life Examples

  • Hot Sizzling Food: We smell it from a distance because the rate of diffusion increases with temperature.
  • Earthen Pot (Matka): Water stays cool because of evaporation through pores.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse Evaporation with Boiling. Evaporation happens at any temperature, while boiling only happens at a fixed boiling point.

Important Questions

  1. What is the physical state of water at 250°C and 100°C?
  2. Why does our palm feel cold when we put some acetone or petrol on it?
  3. Convert 300 K to the Celsius scale.

Chapter 2: Is Matter Around Us Pure?

Understanding the difference between pure substances and mixtures we see daily.

Key Concepts

  • Pure Substances: Consist of a single type of particles (Elements and Compounds).
  • Mixtures: Contain more than one substance mixed in any proportion.
    • Homogeneous: Uniform composition (e.g., Salt in water).
    • Heterogeneous: Non-uniform composition (e.g., Sand in water).
  • Tyndall Effect: Scattering of a beam of light by colloidal particles.
  • Colloids: Appear homogeneous but are heterogeneous (e.g., Milk, Fog).
  • Elements: Cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions.

Important Definitions

  • Solution: A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.
  • Saturated Solution: A solution in which no more solute can be dissolved at a given temperature.
  • Solubility: Amount of solute present in a saturated solution at a specific temperature.

Important Formulas

Concentration of Solution (%) = (Mass of Solute / Mass of Solution) × 100

Real-life Examples

  • Air: A homogeneous mixture of various gases like Nitrogen, Oxygen, etc.
  • Alloys: Solid mixtures of metals (e.g., Brass is a mixture of Zinc and Copper).

Common Mistakes

Students often think Milk is a pure substance. It is actually a mixture of water, fats, and proteins (a colloid).

Important Questions

  1. Differentiate between homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures with examples.
  2. How can we separate a mixture of salt and ammonium chloride? (Answer: Sublimation)
  3. What is the difference between a physical change and a chemical change?

Chapter 3: Atoms and Molecules

Master the laws that govern chemical reactions and the tiny particles behind them.

Key Concepts

  • Law of Conservation of Mass: Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • Law of Constant Proportions: In a chemical substance, elements are always present in definite proportions by mass.
  • Atom: The smallest building block of matter.
  • Molecule: A group of two or more atoms that are chemically bonded together.
  • Valency: The combining capacity of an atom.

Important Definitions

  • Atomic Mass Unit (u): A mass unit equal to exactly 1/12th the mass of one atom of Carbon-12.
  • Polyatomic Ion: A cluster of atoms that acts as an ion (e.g., Ammonium NH4+).
  • Mole: 1 mole of any species is that quantity in number having a mass equal to its atomic or molecular mass in grams.

Important Formulas

  • Number of Moles (n) = Given Mass (m) / Molar Mass (M)
  • Avogadro Number = 6.022 × 1023

Real-life Examples

  • Water (H2O): Always contains Hydrogen and Oxygen in a 1:8 ratio by mass, no matter where you get it from.

Common Mistakes

When writing chemical formulas, students often forget to cross-over the valencies. Example: Magnesium Chloride is MgCl2, not MgCl.

Important Questions

  1. Calculate the molecular mass of H2O and HNO3.
  2. What is the mass of 0.5 mole of nitrogen atoms?
  3. Define the Avogadro constant and state its value.

Chapter 4: Structure of the Atom

The inner world of an atom: Electrons, Protons, and Neutrons.

Key Concepts

  • Subatomic Particles: Electrons (-ve), Protons (+ve), and Neutrons (neutral).
  • Thomson’s Model: Atom is like a "Watermelon" or "Plum Pudding" with electrons embedded in a positive sphere.
  • Rutherford’s Model: Discovered the Nucleus using the alpha-particle scattering experiment.
  • Bohr’s Model: Electrons revolve in discrete orbits (K, L, M, N) without radiating energy.
  • Valency: The number of electrons gained, lost, or shared to achieve an octet in the outermost shell.

Important Definitions

  • Atomic Number (Z): Total number of protons in an atom.
  • Mass Number (A): Sum of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
  • Isotopes: Atoms of the same element with same atomic number but different mass numbers (e.g., 12C and 14C).
  • Isobars: Atoms of different elements with same mass number but different atomic numbers (e.g., Calcium and Argon both have mass 40).

Important Formulas

  • Mass Number (A) = Number of Protons + Number of Neutrons
  • Max electrons in a shell = 2n2 (where n is the orbit number).

Real-life Examples

  • Isotopes in Medicine: An isotope of Iodine is used for the treatment of goiter; an isotope of Cobalt is used for cancer treatment.

Common Mistakes

Remember that the outmost shell can hold a maximum of 8 electrons, even if the 2n² rule allows for more.

Important Questions

  1. Compare the models of an atom proposed by Thomson, Rutherford, and Bohr.
  2. Define Valency by taking examples of Magnesium and Aluminum.
  3. What are the limitations of Rutherford’s model of the atom?

Chapter 1: The Fundamental Unit of Life

Exploring the cell, the building block of all living organisms.

Key Concepts

  • Cell Theory: All living things are composed of cells, and the cell is the basic unit of life (Proposed by Schleiden and Schwann).
  • Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells:
    • Prokaryotic: Small, no well-defined nucleus (e.g., Bacteria).
    • Eukaryotic: Large, with a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (e.g., Human cells).
  • Plasma Membrane: The outermost covering of the cell that allows the entry and exit of some materials (Selective Permeability).
  • Cell Organelles:
    • Mitochondria: Powerhouse of the cell (produces ATP).
    • Nucleus: Brain of the cell (contains DNA).
    • Ribosomes: Protein synthesis factories.
    • Lysosomes: Suicide bags (digest waste).

Important Definitions

  • Osmosis: Diffusion of water through a semi-permeable membrane.
  • Plasmolysis: Shrinkage of cell contents away from the cell wall when a plant cell loses water.
  • Protoplasm: The entire living part of the cell, including cytoplasm and nucleus.

Cell Division Step-by-Step

  • Mitosis: One parent cell divides into 2 identical daughter cells (For growth).
  • Meiosis: One cell divides into 4 daughter cells with half the chromosomes (For reproduction).

Real-life Examples

  • Raisins in Water: They swell up due to Endosmosis (water moving in).
  • Amoeba: Captures food through a process called Endocytosis.

Common Mistakes

Don't forget: Cell Walls are found only in plant cells, fungi, and bacteria, but NEVER in animal cells.

Important Questions

  1. Why is the plasma membrane called a selectively permeable membrane?
  2. Differentiate between Plant Cells and Animal Cells.
  3. Why are Lysosomes known as suicide bags?

Chapter 2: Tissues

Understanding how groups of cells work together to perform specific functions.

Key Concepts

  • Plant Tissues:
    • Meristematic: Growth tissues (Apical, Lateral, Intercalary).
    • Permanent: Simple (Parenchyma, Collenchyma, Sclerenchyma) and Complex (Xylem, Phloem).
  • Animal Tissues:
    • Epithelial: For protection (e.g., Skin, lining of blood vessels).
    • Connective: For binding (e.g., Blood, Bone, Cartilage, Ligament).
    • Muscular: For movement (Striated, Unstriated, Cardiac).
    • Nervous: For control and coordination (Brain, Spinal cord, Neurons).

Important Definitions

  • Differentiation: The process by which cells take up a permanent shape, size, and function.
  • Ligament: Connective tissue that joins bone to bone.
  • Tendon: Connective tissue that joins muscle to bone.

Transportation in Plants

  • Xylem: Transports water and minerals vertically. (Mostly dead cells).
  • Phloem: Transports food (sugar) in both directions. (Mostly living cells).

Real-life Examples

  • Bark of a tree: Made of Sclerenchyma, giving it strength and protection.
  • Nerve Impulse: How you pull your hand back from a hot object (Nervous tissue).

Common Mistakes

Confusing Ligaments and Tendons is very common. Use this trick: BLB (Bone-Ligament-Bone) and BMT (Bone-Muscle-Tendon).

Important Questions

  1. What are the three types of muscular tissues?
  2. What is the function of areolar tissue?
  3. Differentiate between Xylem and Phloem based on structure and function.

Chapter 3: Diversity in Living Organisms

A systematic study of the millions of life forms on Earth.

Key Concepts

  • Five Kingdom Classification: Proposed by Robert Whittaker (Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia).
  • Hierarchy of Classification: Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
  • Kingdom Plantae: Divided into Thallophyta, Bryophyta, Pteridophyta, Gymnosperms, and Angiosperms.
  • Kingdom Animalia: Categorized by body symmetry, body cavity (coelom), and presence of backbone (Chordata).

Important Definitions

  • Binomial Nomenclature: The scientific system of naming organisms using two words (Genus and Species), proposed by Carolus Linnaeus.
  • Coelom: The body cavity present between the gut and the body wall.
  • Cold-blooded: Animals whose body temperature changes with the environment (e.g., Reptiles).

Plant Classification Step-by-Step

  • Thallophyta: Simplest, no distinct body (Algae).
  • Bryophyta: Amphibians of the plant kingdom.
  • Angiosperms: Flowering plants where seeds are inside fruit.

Real-life Examples

  • Mushroom: A member of Kingdom Fungi.
  • Whale: Though it looks like a fish, it belongs to Class Mammalia because it breathes air and gives milk.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse Protista (eukaryotic) with Monera (prokaryotic). Both are unicellular, but their cell structure is different.

Important Questions

  1. What are the advantages of classifying organisms?
  2. State the main characteristics of Mammals.
  3. What is the difference between Gymnosperms and Angiosperms?

Chapter 4: Why Do We Fall Ill?

Understanding health, diseases, and how to prevent them.

Key Concepts

  • Health: A state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being.
  • Acute vs Chronic Diseases:
    • Acute: Last for a short time (e.g., Common cold).
    • Chronic: Last for a long time, even a lifetime (e.g., Diabetes, TB).
  • Infectious vs Non-infectious:
    • Infectious: Caused by microbes, spread through air, water, contact (e.g., Flu).
    • Non-infectious: Internal causes, not spreadable (e.g., Cancer, High BP).
  • Immunization: Strengthening the immune system by using vaccines.

Important Definitions

  • Vector: Organisms that carry disease-causing microbes (e.g., Mosquitoes).
  • Antibiotics: Medicines that block biochemical pathways in bacteria to kill them.
  • Symptom: An indication that something is wrong with the body (e.g., Fever).

Principles of Prevention

  • General Ways: Clean environment, safe drinking water, proper nutrition.
  • Specific Ways: Vaccination to develop immunity against specific diseases.

Real-life Examples

  • ORSaline: Used for dehydration during diarrhea to restore salts and water.
  • Pulse Polio: A government initiative to immunize all children against Polio.

Common Mistakes

Remember: Antibiotics do not work against viruses (like Common Cold or COVID) because viruses don't have the same biochemical pathways as bacteria.

Important Questions

  1. What is the difference between being 'healthy' and being 'disease-free'?
  2. What are the different means by which infectious diseases are spread?
  3. Why is it difficult to make antiviral medicines compared to antibacterial medicines?

Chapter 5: Natural Resources

The balance of nature and the resources that sustain life on Earth.

Key Concepts

  • The Atmosphere: Acts as a blanket, regulating Earth's temperature.
  • Water: A vital resource. Pollution leads to death of aquatic life and diseases.
  • Soil: Formed by weathering of rocks over thousands of years. Contains humus and microbes.
  • Greenhouse Effect: Warming of Earth due to gases like CO2 trapping heat. (Necessary in limits, but dangerous when excessive).
  • Ozone Layer: Found in the stratosphere, protects us from harmful UV rays.

Important Definitions

  • Humus: Decayed organic matter in the soil that makes it fertile.
  • Global Warming: The increase in Earth's average temperature due to greenhouse gases.
  • Biogeochemical Cycle: The constant interaction between biotic and abiotic components of the biosphere.

The Nitrogen Cycle Step-by-Step

  1. Nitrogen Fixation: Bacteria in soil or lightning converts N2 gas into nitrates.
  2. Nitrification: Converting ammonia into nitrites and nitrates.
  3. Assimilation: Plants absorb nitrates to make proteins.
  4. Denitrification: Converting nitrates back into N2 gas by bacteria.

Real-life Examples

  • Lichens: These are very sensitive to air pollution (SO2) and are used as pollution indicators.
  • Soil Erosion: Prevented by planting trees (afforestation) as roots hold the soil tightly.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse Greenhouse Effect (global warming) with Ozone Depletion (UV rays). They are caused by different gases and have different effects.

Important Questions

  1. How is our atmosphere different from the atmosphere on Venus and Mars?
  2. What is the role of the atmosphere in climate control?
  3. Draw and explain the Carbon Cycle in nature.

Chapter 1: Motion

Understanding how objects move, distance, speed, and the equations that govern them.

Key Concepts

  • Distance vs Displacement: Distance is the total path covered (scalar). Displacement is the shortest distance between start and end (vector).
  • Speed vs Velocity: Speed is distance/time (scalar). Velocity is displacement/time (vector).
  • Acceleration: Rate of change of velocity (a = (v-u)/t).
  • Uniform Circular Motion: Motion of an object in a circular path at constant speed.

Important Definitions

  • Vector Quantity: Has both magnitude and direction.
  • Scalar Quantity: Has only magnitude.
  • Uniform Motion: When an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.

Important Formulas

  • v = u + at
  • s = ut + ½ at²
  • v² - u² = 2as

Where: u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, s = distance/displacement.

Solved Example

Q: A car accelerates from 18 km/h to 36 km/h in 5 seconds. Find the acceleration.

Solution:
u = 18 km/h = 18 × 5/18 = 5 m/s
v = 36 km/h = 36 × 5/18 = 10 m/s
t = 5 s
a = (v - u) / t = (10 - 5) / 5 = 1 m/s²

Real-life Applications

  • Odometer: Measures distance in vehicles.
  • Speedometer: Measures instantaneous speed.

Common Mistakes

Always convert units to SI (m/s) before solving. Remember: 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s.

Important Questions

  1. Distinguish between speed and velocity.
  2. Under what condition is the magnitude of average velocity equal to average speed?
  3. What does the slope of a distance-time graph represent? (Answer: Speed)

Chapter 2: Force and Laws of Motion

The push and pull that drives the universe: Newton's Laws.

Key Concepts

  • First Law (Inertia): An object stays at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a net force.
  • Second Law: Rate of change of momentum is proportional to applied force (F = ma).
  • Third Law: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  • Conservation of Momentum: Total momentum remains constant in an isolated system.

Important Definitions

  • Inertia: Natural tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of motion. Mass is the measure of inertia.
  • Momentum (p): Product of mass and velocity (p = mv).
  • Impulse: Large force acting for a short time.

Important Formulas

  • F = ma (Force = mass × acceleration)
  • p = mv (Momentum = mass × velocity)
  • m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ (Conservation of Momentum)

Solved Example

Q: What force is required to produce an acceleration of 2 m/s² in a body of mass 5 kg?

Solution:
m = 5 kg, a = 2 m/s²
F = m × a = 5 × 2 = 10 N

Real-life Applications

  • Seat belts: Protect us during sudden braking due to inertia.
  • Recoil of Gun: Demonstrates the 3rd Law of Motion.

Common Mistakes

Inertia is NOT a force. It is a property of matter. Heavier objects have more inertia because they have more mass.

Important Questions

  1. Define inertia and state its relationship with mass.
  2. Why is it advised to tie any luggage kept on the roof of a bus with a rope?
  3. Explain why a fielder pulls his hand back while catching a fast-moving cricket ball.

Chapter 3: Gravitation

From falling apples to orbiting planets: The force of gravity.

Key Concepts

  • Universal Law of Gravitation: Every mass attracts every other mass. F = G(Mm/d²).
  • Free Fall: When an object falls only under the influence of gravity.
  • Mass vs Weight: Mass is constant (kg); Weight is a force (N) and changes with 'g'.
  • Thrust and Pressure: Pressure is force per unit area.
  • Archimedes' Principle: Upward buoyant force equals the weight of fluid displaced.

Important Definitions

  • G (Gravitational Constant): 6.673 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg².
  • g (Acceleration due to gravity): ~9.8 m/s² on Earth.
  • Buoyancy: The upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object.

Important Formulas

  • F = G × (M × m) / r²
  • W = m × g (Weight)
  • P = F / A (Pressure)

Solved Example

Q: Mass of an object is 10 kg. What is its weight on Earth?

Solution:
m = 10 kg, g = 9.8 m/s²
W = m × g = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N

Real-life Applications

  • Tides: Caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun.
  • Ships: Float because they displace a weight of water equal to their own weight.

Common Mistakes

Don't confuse 'G' (Universal Constant) with 'g' (Acceleration due to gravity). 'G' is the same everywhere, but 'g' changes from planet to planet.

Important Questions

  1. What is the difference between mass and weight?
  2. Why does an object float or sink when placed on the surface of water?
  3. State the Universal Law of Gravitation.

Chapter 4: Work and Energy

The science of doing things and the capacity to do them.

Key Concepts

  • Work: Done when force causes displacement in the direction of force (W = Fs).
  • Kinetic Energy (K.E): Energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
  • Potential Energy (P.E): Energy possessed due to its position or configuration.
  • Law of Conservation of Energy: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed.
  • Power: Rate of doing work.

Important Definitions

  • Joule (J): SI unit of work and energy. 1 J = 1 N × 1 m.
  • Watt (W): SI unit of power. 1 W = 1 J/s.
  • Commercial Unit of Energy: 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

Important Formulas

  • W = F × s
  • K.E = ½ mv²
  • P.E = mgh
  • P = W / t

Solved Example

Q: A body of mass 2 kg is dropped from a height of 10 m. Find its potential energy.

Solution:
m = 2 kg, g = 9.8 m/s², h = 10 m
P.E = mgh = 2 × 9.8 × 10 = 196 J

Real-life Applications

  • Roller Coasters: Demonstrate the continuous conversion of P.E to K.E.
  • Electric Bills: Calculated in kWh (units of energy).

Common Mistakes

If you push a wall, work is ZERO because there is no displacement. Displacement is mandatory for work to be done.

Important Questions

  1. What is the work done by a person carrying a suitcase on his head and walking horizontally? (Answer: 0, because Force and Displacement are perpendicular).
  2. Define 1 Watt of power.
  3. State the Law of Conservation of Energy.

Chapter 5: Sound

The energy that travels as waves through air, water, and solids.

Key Concepts

  • Nature of Sound: Longitudinal mechanical waves; requires a medium to travel.
  • Characteristics:
    • Pitch: Depends on Frequency.
    • Loudness: Depends on Amplitude.
    • Quality/Timbre: Depends on the shape of the wave.
  • Echo: Reflection of sound heard after a delay (Min distance 17.2m).
  • Range of Hearing: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz for humans.
  • Ultrasound: Sounds above 20 kHz (Used in SONAR, Medical imaging).

Important Definitions

  • Wavelength (λ): Distance between two consecutive compressions.
  • Frequency (f/ν): Number of oscillations per second. Unit: Hertz (Hz).
  • Time Period (T): Time taken for one complete oscillation (T = 1/f).

Important Formulas

  • v = f × λ (Speed = Frequency × Wavelength)
  • v = 2d / t (For Echo/SONAR)

Solved Example

Q: A sound wave has a frequency of 2 kHz and wavelength 35 cm. How long will it take to travel 1.5 km?

Solution:
f = 2000 Hz, λ = 0.35 m
v = f × λ = 2000 × 0.35 = 700 m/s
Time = Distance / Speed = 1500 / 700 = 2.14 s

Real-life Applications

  • SONAR: Used by ships to measure ocean depth.
  • Stethoscope: Works on the principle of multiple reflection of sound.

Common Mistakes

Sound cannot travel in a vacuum. Remember that speed of sound is maximum in solids and minimum in gases.

Important Questions

  1. What is the difference between loudness and pitch?
  2. What is reverberation? How can it be reduced?
  3. What are the practical applications of ultrasound?
