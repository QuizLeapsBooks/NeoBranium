Federalism Board Exam Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Federalism is a system of government where power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units. This chapter explains how India manages its diversity through power-sharing. Board exams frequently test the features of federalism, the three lists of powers, decentralisation, and linguistic reorganisation.

1.1 What is Federalism?

Federalism is a system of government in which the power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units of the country [citation:1][citation:7].

Usually, a federation has two levels of government:

  • One is the government for the entire country that is usually responsible for a few subjects of common national interest
  • The others are governments at the level of provinces or states that look after much of the day-to-day administering of their state

Both these levels of governments enjoy their power independent of the other [citation:11].

1.2 Federalism vs Unitary System

FeatureFederal SystemUnitary System
Levels of GovernmentTwo or more levelsOnly one level (sub-units are subordinate)
Power DistributionPower divided between central and state governmentsPower centralised with the central government
IndependenceBoth levels enjoy power independentlySub-units work under the central government
ExampleIndia, USA, AustraliaSri Lanka, United Kingdom

1.3 Dual Objectives of Federalism

The federal system has dual objectives:

  • To safeguard and promote the unity of the country
  • To accommodate regional diversity

Two aspects are crucial for the institutions and practice of federalism: governments at different levels should agree to some rules of power-sharing, and they should trust that each would abide by its part of the agreement [citation:11].

🎯 Key Point: An ideal federal system has both aspects — mutual trust and agreement to live together.

2. Key Features of Federalism

However a federation forms, these features define one [citation:7][citation:18]:

FeatureExplanation
Two or More LevelsThere are two or more levels (tiers) of government
Same Citizens, Different JurisdictionDifferent tiers govern the same citizens, but each tier has its own jurisdiction in specific matters of legislation, taxation and administration
Constitutional GuaranteeThe jurisdictions of respective levels are specified in the Constitution. The existence and authority of each tier is constitutionally guaranteed
Rigid Amendment ProcessFundamental provisions of the Constitution cannot be unilaterally changed by one level. Such changes require the consent of both levels
Judicial OversightCourts have the power to interpret the Constitution and powers of different levels of government
Defined Revenue SourcesSources of revenue for each level of government are clearly specified to ensure its financial autonomy
Dual ObjectivesThe federal system has dual objectives — to safeguard and promote unity of the country, while accommodating regional diversity
🧠 Mnemonic: TCDRJDD
  • T — Two or more levels
  • C — Constitutional guarantee
  • D — Different jurisdictions
  • R — Rigid amendment process
  • J — Judicial oversight
  • D — Defined revenue sources
  • D — Dual objectives

3. Routes to Federation — Coming Together vs Holding Together

Federations are formed through two different routes [citation:1][citation:4]:

BasisComing Together FederationHolding Together Federation
How it formsIndependent states come together voluntarily to form a bigger unitA large country decides to divide its power between constituent states and national government
PurposePooling sovereignty and retaining identity to increase securityAccommodating regional and cultural diversity
Balance of PowerConstituent states usually have equal power and are strong vis-à-vis the federal governmentCentral government tends to be more powerful vis-à-vis the states
ExamplesUSA, Switzerland, AustraliaIndia, Spain, Belgium
💡 India's Position: India is a holding together federation. It was a large, diverse country that chose to share power with states to accommodate regional diversity and maintain unity. The Central government tends to be more powerful, and some states have special provisions (like Jammu and Kashmir had its own Constitution) [citation:1][citation:4].

4. What Makes India a Federal Country?

4.1 Constitutional Provisions

The Constitution originally provided for a two-tier system of government — the Union Government (Central Government) representing the Union of India and the State Governments. Later, a third tier was added in the form of Panchayats and Municipalities [citation:2][citation:7].

4.2 Three-Fold Distribution of Powers

The Constitution clearly divides powers between the Union and State Governments through three lists [citation:1][citation:8]:

ListWhat It CoversWho LegislatesExamples
Union ListSubjects of national importanceOnly Union GovernmentDefence, Foreign Affairs, Banking, Currency, Communications
State ListSubjects of State and local importanceOnly State GovernmentsPolice, Trade, Agriculture, Irrigation
Concurrent ListSubjects of common interest to bothBoth levels; Union law prevails in conflictEducation, Forests, Marriage, Adoption, Trade Unions
Residuary PowersSubjects not in any of the three listsOnly Union GovernmentNew/unforeseen subjects (e.g., computer software)
🧠 Mnemonic: USC-R
  • Union List — "Uniform national policy"
  • State List — "State and local matters"
  • Concurrent List — "Common interest, Centre wins conflict"
  • Residuary — "Rest go to Union"

4.3 Other Constitutional Safeguards

  • All states of India do not have equal powers (e.g., some have special provisions)
  • The Parliament cannot on its own change power-sharing; changes need approval of both Houses with at least two-thirds majority [citation:7]
  • The judiciary oversees the implementation of constitutional provisions and procedures

5. How Federalism is Practised — Linguistic States & Language Policy

5.1 Linguistic Reorganisation of States

After Independence in 1947, state boundaries of several old states were changed to create new states based on language. This was done to ensure that people who spoke the same language lived in the same state [citation:1][citation:4].

  • 1953: Andhra Pradesh was created for Telugu speakers
  • 1956: States Reorganisation Act
  • Later, states like Nagaland, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand, and Telangana were created to recognise differences based on culture, ethnicity, or geography

5.2 Language Policy

India's language policy is another unique aspect of its federal system [citation:1][citation:7]:

  • The Constitution did not give the status of national language to any one language
  • Hindi was identified as the official language, but it is the mother tongue of only about 40% of Indians
  • Besides Hindi, there are 22 other languages recognised as Scheduled Languages by the Constitution
  • States have their own official languages
  • English is used along with Hindi for official purposes
💡 Why Did the Policy of Accommodation Work? If the central government had imposed Hindi as the national language, states like Tamil Nadu might have broken away. The flexible language policy accommodated diversity and strengthened national unity [citation:3][citation:5].

6. Centre-State Relations

6.1 Pre-1990 Era

  • The same party often ruled at the Centre and in most states
  • This made it difficult for state governments to function independently
  • The Central government often misused its power to dismiss state governments

6.2 Post-1990 Era

  • The rise of regional political parties in many states started an era of coalition governments at the Centre
  • Since no single party got a clear majority in the Lok Sabha, national parties had to enter into alliances with regional parties
  • This led to a new culture of power-sharing and respect for the autonomy of State Governments
  • It became difficult for the Central Government to dismiss state governments in an undemocratic manner [citation:1][citation:4]
🎯 Key Point: Coalition politics since the 1990s strengthened federalism in practice by forcing the Centre to accommodate regional interests.

7. Decentralisation in India

7.1 What is Decentralisation?

Decentralisation means taking power from the Central and State governments and giving it to local government bodies [citation:2][citation:7].

7.2 Why is Decentralisation Important?

  • Many problems are best settled at the local level
  • Local people have better knowledge of local issues
  • People can directly participate in decision-making
  • It helps inculcate a habit of democratic participation
  • It is the best way to realise the principle of local self-government [citation:4]

7.3 The 1992 Constitutional Amendment

A major step towards decentralisation was taken in 1992 when the Constitution was amended to make the third tier of democracy more powerful and effective [citation:1][citation:4]:

ProvisionDetails
Regular ElectionsIt is now constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies
Reservation for SC/ST/OBCSeats are reserved for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes
Reservation for WomenAt least one-third of all positions and seats are reserved for women
State Election CommissionAn independent institution called State Election Commission was created to conduct panchayat and municipal elections
Revenue SharingState governments are required to share some powers and revenue with local government bodies

7.4 Rural Local Government (Panchayati Raj)

Rural local government is popularly known as Panchayati Raj. It has a three-tier structure [citation:2][citation:7]:

  • Gram Panchayat — Decision-making body for the entire village
  • Panchayat Samiti (Block/Mandal) — A few gram panchayats are grouped together
  • Zila Parishad — All panchayat samitis in a district together form the Zila Parishad

7.5 Urban Local Government

In urban areas [citation:2]:

  • In smaller towns — Municipal Councils
  • In big cities — Municipal Corporations (headed by a Mayor)

7.6 Differences: Pre-1992 vs Post-1992

AspectPre-1992Post-1992
ElectionsNot held regularlyConstitutionally mandated regular elections
PowersNo powers or resources of their ownState governments share powers and revenue
ReservationNo reservationSeats reserved for SC/ST/OBC and women

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Terms:
  • Federalism: Power divided between central authority and constituent units
  • Unitary System: Only one level of government or sub-units under central control
  • Union List: Subjects of national importance (Defence, Currency)
  • State List: Subjects of local importance (Police, Agriculture)
  • Concurrent List: Subjects of common interest (Education, Forests)
  • Residuary Powers: Subjects not in any list — belong to Union Government
  • Decentralisation: Power given to local government bodies
  • Linguistic States: States created on the basis of language
🎯 Key Comparisons:
  • Coming Together: USA, Switzerland, Australia — states have equal power
  • Holding Together: India, Spain, Belgium — Centre is more powerful
🎯 Key Points to Remember:
  • India is a holding together federation
  • Hindi is the official language, not the national language
  • 1992 Constitutional Amendment strengthened local self-government
  • Coalition politics since the 1990s strengthened federalism in practice
  • Jammu and Kashmir had its own Constitution (special provision)
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing Union List (Defence, Banking) with Concurrent List (Education, Forests)
  • Forgetting that in case of conflict on Concurrent List, the Union law prevails, not the State law
  • Mixing up coming together (USA) with holding together (India) federations
  • Not mentioning that Hindi is the official language, not the national language
  • Forgetting the three-tier structure of Panchayati Raj (Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti, Zila Parishad)
  • Not giving the year 1992 for the Constitutional Amendment on decentralisation
