Agriculture High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains the types of farming, cropping seasons, major crops grown in India, and the technological and institutional reforms that transformed Indian agriculture. Board exams frequently test farming types, crop conditions, cropping seasons, and the impact of globalisation on agriculture.

1.1 What is Agriculture?

Agriculture is a primary activity that produces food crops and industrial raw materials. It is the art and science of cultivating soil, raising crops, and rearing livestock [citation:2][citation:15].

1.2 Why is Agriculture Important in India?

  • Employment: Two-thirds of India's population is still dependent on agriculture [citation:2]
  • Food Security: It provides food for the growing population
  • Raw Materials: It supplies raw materials to industries like cotton textiles, sugar, and food processing [citation:5]
  • Contribution to GDP: Agriculture is the backbone of the Indian economy
  • Export Earnings: Tea, coffee, spices, and other agricultural products are major exports
🎯 Key Point: Agriculture is called the backbone of the Indian economy because it employs more than half of the population and provides raw materials to major industries [citation:5].

2. Types of Farming

2.1 Primitive Subsistence Farming

  • Also called 'slash and burn' agriculture
  • Farmers clear a patch of land and produce cereals and other food crops to sustain their family
  • When soil fertility decreases, farmers shift to a fresh patch of land
  • Uses simple tools like hoe, dao, and digging sticks
  • Low productivity because no fertilisers or modern inputs are used
  • Known as Jhumming in Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, and Nagaland [citation:3][citation:12]

2.2 Intensive Subsistence Farming

  • Practised in areas of high population pressure on land
  • Farmer cultivates a small plot using simple tools and more labour
  • Uses bio-chemical inputs and manure to get maximum output
  • Land productivity is high due to intensive use

2.3 Commercial Farming

  • The main purpose is sale in the market to earn profit
  • Uses high doses of modern inputs — HYV seeds, chemical fertilisers, insecticides, pesticides
  • Practised on large landholdings
  • Examples: Wheat in Punjab, Haryana; Cotton in Maharashtra

2.4 Plantation Agriculture

  • A type of commercial farming where a single crop is grown on a large area [citation:2]
  • Interface of agriculture and industry
  • Uses capital-intensive inputs and skilled labour
  • Examples: Tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane, banana
🎯 Quick Comparison:
  • Primitive: Slash and burn, family labour, low output
  • Intensive: Small plots, high population, labour-intensive
  • Commercial: Large farms, modern inputs, for market
  • Plantation: Single crop, large area, capital-intensive

3. Cropping Seasons in India

India has three main cropping seasons [citation:5]:

RabiOctober to DecemberApril to JuneWheat, gram, mustard, linseed
KharifJune to July (with monsoon)September to OctoberRice, maize, jowar, bajra, cotton, jute
ZaidMarch to JuneSummer monthsWatermelon, muskmelon, cucumber
🎯 Memory Trick:
  • Rabi: "Winter crop" — Wheat, Gram (Remember: Rabi = Round wheat)
  • Kharif: "Monsoon crop" — Rice, Maize (Remember: Kharif = Ki monsoon)
  • Zaid: "Summer crop" — Watermelon

4. Major Crops of India

India grows both food crops and non-food crops. Food crops include cereals, pulses, and horticulture crops. Non-food crops provide raw materials to industries [citation:11].

5. Food Crops

5.1 Rice

  • Staple food crop of India [citation:2]
  • Kharif crop requiring high temperature (above 25°C) and high humidity
  • Requires 100 cm rainfall or irrigation
  • Grown in plains of North India, North-East India, coastal areas, and deltaic regions [citation:8]
  • Major states: West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Bihar, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu

5.2 Wheat

  • Main food crop in north and north-western India [citation:12]
  • Rabi crop requiring cool growing season (10-15°C) and bright sunshine at harvest (21-26°C) [citation:5]
  • Requires 50-75 cm rainfall
  • Two main growing zones: Ganga-Satluj plains and Deccan black soil region [citation:12]
  • Major states: Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh

5.3 Millets (Jowar, Bajra, Ragi)

  • Called coarse grains
  • Grown in dry and rain-fed areas
  • Nutritionally rich — high in iron, calcium, and fibre
  • Jowar is the third most important food crop in India

5.4 Pulses

  • Major source of protein in vegetarian diet
  • Leguminous crops that restore soil fertility by fixing nitrogen [citation:2]
  • India is the largest producer and consumer of pulses [citation:12]
  • Grown in all parts of India

6. Non-Food Crops

6.1 Sugarcane

  • Tropical and subtropical crop
  • Requires hot and humid climate (21-27°C) and 75-100 cm rainfall
  • Main source of sugar, gur, and molasses
  • Major states: Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu

6.2 Tea

  • Important beverage crop and plantation crop [citation:2]
  • Requires tropical and subtropical climate, deep fertile well-drained soil rich in humus
  • Needs warm, moist, frost-free weather throughout the year [citation:11]
  • Labour-intensive industry requiring abundant, cheap, and skilled labour
  • Major states: Assam, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala

6.3 Coffee

  • Known for its quality in India
  • Arabica variety originally brought from Yemen [citation:11]
  • First introduced on the Baba Budan Hills
  • Major states: Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu

6.4 Cotton

  • Main raw material for the cotton textile industry
  • India is the second largest producer of cotton after China [citation:11]
  • Grows on black cotton soil of the Deccan Plateau
  • Requires high temperature, light rainfall, 210 frost-free days, and bright sunshine
  • Kharif crop taking 6-8 months to mature
  • Major states: Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka

6.5 Jute

  • Known as the Golden Fibre [citation:11][citation:15]
  • Grows on well-drained fertile soils in flood plains renewed every year
  • Requires high temperature during growth
  • Major states: West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Odisha
  • Used for gunny bags, mats, ropes, carpets

6.6 Rubber

  • Equatorial crop; also grows in tropical and subtropical areas under special conditions [citation:11]
  • Requires moist and humid climate, rainfall above 200 cm, temperature above 25°C
  • Important industrial raw material
  • Major states: Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andaman and Nicobar Islands
🎯 Crop Conditions Quick Reference:
  • Rice: High temp, high humidity, 100+ cm rainfall
  • Wheat: Cool growing, bright sunshine at harvest, 50-75 cm rainfall
  • Cotton: Black soil, high temp, 210 frost-free days
  • Jute: Flood plains, high temp, Golden Fibre
  • Rubber: Equatorial, 200+ cm rainfall, 25+°C
  • Tea: Tropical/subtropical, well-drained soil, frost-free

7. Technological and Institutional Reforms

Agriculture in India has been practised for thousands of years, but continued use of land without suitable technological and institutional changes slowed agricultural development. Reforms became necessary to ensure food security for the growing population [citation:11].

7.1 Institutional Reforms

After Independence, the government gave priority to land reforms [citation:11]:

  • Collectivisation — bringing small landholdings together
  • Consolidation of holdings — combining fragmented land
  • Cooperation — forming cooperative societies
  • Abolition of zamindari — ending the zamindari system

Land reform was the main focus of the First Five Year Plan [citation:11].

7.2 Technological Reforms

The government introduced agricultural reforms in the 1960s and 1970s [citation:8]:

  • Green Revolution — using HYV seeds, fertilisers, and technology to increase food production
  • White Revolution (Operation Flood) — increasing milk production [citation:8][citation:15]

7.3 Government Schemes for Farmers

💡 Impact of Reforms: The Green Revolution made India self-sufficient in food grains. However, its benefits were concentrated in a few areas (Punjab, Haryana, Western UP). The government then introduced broader land development programmes in the 1980s and 1990s [citation:8].

8. Impact of Globalisation on Indian Agriculture

8.1 Historical Context

Globalisation is not new. During colonialism, European traders exported Indian spices to other nations, and farmers in South India were encouraged to grow these crops [citation:16].

8.2 Challenges Since 1990

Since the 1990s, globalisation has brought new challenges for Indian farmers [citation:8]:

  • Indian agricultural products cannot compete with those from developed countries due to heavy subsidies in those nations
  • India is a major producer of rice, cotton, rubber, tea, coffee, jute, and spices but faces tough competition
  • Improving the condition of marginal and small farmers is a priority
⚠️ Key Challenge: Indian agriculture is at a crossroads. To make farming profitable, the focus must be on improving conditions for small and marginal farmers [citation:8].

  • Zaid: March-June — Watermelon, Muskmelon
🎯 Major Crops Quick Reference:
  • Rice: Kharif, high temp, 100+ cm rainfall, staple crop
  • Wheat: Rabi, cool growing, bright sunshine at harvest
  • Cotton: Black soil, 210 frost-free days, 2nd largest producer
  • Jute: Golden Fibre, flood plains
  • Rubber: Equatorial, 200+ cm rainfall
  • Tea: Plantation crop, frost-free, labour-intensive
  • Coffee: Arabica variety, Baba Budan Hills
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing Rabi (wheat, gram) with Kharif (rice, cotton) crops
  • Forgetting that pulses are leguminous and restore soil fertility [citation:2]
  • Mixing up Tea (beverage + plantation) with Coffee (beverage + plantation but different regions)
  • Not mentioning Jhumming when asked about primitive subsistence farming [citation:3]
  • Forgetting that India is the largest producer and consumer of pulses [citation:12]
