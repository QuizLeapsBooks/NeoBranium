Chapter 3: Metals and Non-metals High Scoring Chapter

Complete detailed notes covering physical & chemical properties, reactivity series, extraction of metals, ionic compounds, corrosion prevention, alloys, and most repeated board exam questions.

Why this chapter is important? Every year 4-6 questions appear — reactivity series order, displacement reactions, extraction methods (roasting/calcination), and properties of ionic compounds.

1. Physical Properties of Metals and Non-metals

🔩 Metals

  • Lustrous (shiny)
  • Malleable (beaten into sheets)
  • Ductile (drawn into wires)
  • Good conductors of heat & electricity
  • High melting & boiling points
  • Sonorous (ring when struck)
  • Examples: Iron, Copper, Gold, Aluminum

⚫ Non-metals

  • Dull appearance (except iodine, diamond)
  • Brittle (break easily)
  • Non-ductile (cannot be drawn into wires)
  • Poor conductors (except graphite)
  • Low melting & boiling points
  • Non-sonorous
  • Examples: Carbon, Sulfur, Phosphorus, Oxygen
📌 Exception to Remember: Sodium and Potassium are metals but soft (can be cut with knife). Mercury is the only liquid metal. Iodine is a non-metal but lustrous. Graphite (carbon) is a non-metal that conducts electricity.

2. Chemical Properties of Metals and Non-metals

🔥 Reaction with Oxygen

Metals form basic oxides. Non-metals form acidic oxides.

  • Metal + O2 → Metal Oxide (basic)
    Example: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO (Magnesium burns with dazzling white light)
  • Non-metal + O2 → Non-metal Oxide (acidic)
    Example: S + O2 → SO2 (Sulfur dioxide)

Amphoteric Oxides: React with both acids and bases. Examples: Al2O3, ZnO

💧 Reaction with Water

  • Sodium/Potassium react violently with cold water → 2Na + 2H2O → 2NaOH + H2
  • Magnesium reacts with hot water → Mg + H2O → MgO + H2
  • Iron reacts with steam (not cold water) → 3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2
  • Copper, Silver, Gold do not react with water

⚡ Reaction with Acids

Metals react with dilute acids to produce salt + hydrogen gas (except noble metals like Cu, Ag, Au).

Example: Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2↑

3. Reactivity Series of Metals Must Memorize

The reactivity series arranges metals in decreasing order of their reactivity. The most reactive metal (Potassium) is at the top, and the least reactive (Gold) is at the bottom.

K (Potassium) Na (Sodium) Ca (Calcium) Mg (Magnesium) Al (Aluminum) Zn (Zinc) Fe (Iron) Pb (Lead) H (Hydrogen) Cu (Copper) Ag (Silver) Au (Gold)
🎯 Mnemonic to Remember: "Kya Na Ca Mali Al Zara Feeke Pani Mein Hain? Cu Aur Ag Anmol Gold!"
K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au

Important Rule: A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from its salt solution.
Example: Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu (Iron displaces Copper)

4. Ionic Compounds – Properties & Formation

Ionic compounds are formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal.

  • Formation of NaCl: Na → Na+ + e- ; Cl + e- → Cl- → Na+Cl-
  • Properties: High melting points, soluble in water, conduct electricity in molten/aqueous state (due to free ions), solid state does NOT conduct electricity.
⭐ Ionic compounds are crystalline solids with high melting points because of strong electrostatic forces between ions.

5. Extraction of Metals – Metallurgy Board Favourite (5 marks)

📌 Steps of Extraction:

  1. Enrichment of Ore: Removing gangue (unwanted impurities) from ore.
  2. Extraction of Metal: Converting metal oxide to metal using reduction.
  3. Refining: Purifying the metal (electrolytic refining).

🔥 Roasting vs Calcination

Roasting

Heating ore in excess of air. Used for sulfide ores.
Example: 2ZnS + 3O2 → 2ZnO + 2SO2

Calcination

Heating ore in limited air. Used for carbonate ores.
Example: ZnCO3 → ZnO + CO2

🔧 Reduction Methods:

  • Highly reactive metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al): Electrolytic reduction
  • Moderately reactive metals (Zn, Fe, Pb): Reduction with Carbon (Coke)
  • Less reactive metals (Cu, Ag, Au): Found in native state

⚡ Electrolytic Refining of Copper:

Impure copper anode → Pure copper cathode. Copper ions from anode deposit on cathode. Impurities settle as anode mud (gold, silver).

6. Corrosion and Alloys

🛡️ Corrosion

Gradual destruction of metals by reaction with air, moisture, or chemicals.

  • Rusting of Iron: 4Fe + 3O2 + xH2O → 2Fe2O3.xH2O
  • Prevention Methods: Galvanization (Zn coating), painting, oiling, alloying, greasing.

🏗️ Alloys

Homogeneous mixture of two or more metals (or metal + non-metal). Alloys are stronger and more resistant to corrosion.

Alloy Composition Uses
Brass Cu + Zn Musical instruments, decorative items
Bronze Cu + Sn Statues, medals
Stainless Steel Fe + Cr + Ni Utensils, surgical instruments
Solder Pb + Sn Soldering electrical components
Amalgam Hg + other metals Dental fillings
📌 Important: Aqua Regia (3:1 mixture of conc. HCl and conc. HNO3) can dissolve Gold and Platinum.

7. Most Repeated Previous Year Questions (2020-2025)

Top Board Questions

  1. What are amphoteric oxides? Give two examples. (Answer: Oxides that react with both acids and bases. Examples: Al2O3, ZnO)
  2. Explain the process of electrolytic refining of Copper with a neat labeled diagram. (Answer: Impure Cu anode, pure Cu cathode, CuSO4 solution as electrolyte)
  3. Why are ionic compounds solid and have high melting points? (Answer: Strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions require high energy to break)
  4. A metal X forms a water-soluble salt XNO3. When XNO3 is added to common salt solution, a white precipitate Y is formed. Identify X and Y. (Answer: X = Ag (Silver), Y = AgCl)
  5. Differentiate between roasting and calcination with examples. (Answer: Roasting – sulfide ores in excess air; Calcination – carbonate ores in limited air)
