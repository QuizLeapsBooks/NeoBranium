The Age of Industrialisation Periodic Assessment Only

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains how production shifted from homes to factories, how workers struggled during this change, and how countries like India were affected under British rule. It covers proto-industrialisation, the factory system, and the decline of Indian textiles.

1.1 What is Industrialisation?

Industrialisation refers to the process of change from an agrarian and handicraft economy to one dominated by industry and machine manufacturing. It began in Britain in the 18th century and gradually spread to other parts of the world.

🎯 Key Point: Industrialisation is not just about machines and factories — it is also about how people worked, earned, and lived their daily lives.

2. Proto-Industrialisation

2.1 What is Proto-Industrialisation?

Proto-industrialisation is the term used to describe the phase of large-scale industrial production that existed before the factory system. Production was carried out in homes and villages, not in factories.

2.2 How Did It Work?

  • Merchants based in towns supplied raw materials to peasants and artisans in villages
  • Rural families produced goods in their homes
  • The finished products were collected by merchants and sold in international markets
  • This system helped merchants avoid the strict rules of urban guilds

2.3 Why Did Merchants Move to Villages?

  • Urban guilds were powerful and restricted new producers from entering trade
  • Rural families needed extra income as farming alone was not enough
  • Demand for goods was growing with expanding world trade
💡 Key Difference: Proto-industrialisation was a decentralised system (production in homes), while factory production was centralised (all processes under one roof).

3. The Coming Up of the Factory

3.1 When Did Factories Appear?

The earliest factories in England appeared by the 1730s. Their number multiplied in the late 18th century. Cotton became the first major industry of the new industrial era.

3.2 Richard Arkwright and the Cotton Mill

Richard Arkwright created the cotton mill, which brought all the processes of cloth production under one roof and management. This allowed:

  • Careful supervision of production
  • Watch over quality
  • Regulation of labour

3.3 The Pace of Industrial Change

Industrialisation was not rapid or dramatic as often imagined:

  • Cotton led until the 1840s; then iron and steel took over
  • Less than 20% of Britain's workforce was employed in technologically advanced sectors by the late 19th century
  • Traditional industries and domestic production remained important
  • New technology spread slowly because machines were costly and often broke down
🎯 Key Fact: Steam power did not spread instantly. At the beginning of the 19th century, England had only 321 steam engines — 80 in cotton and 9 in wool industries.

4. Hand Labour and Steam Power

4.1 Why Did Industrialists Prefer Hand Labour?

In Victorian Britain, there was abundant human labour. Industrialists preferred hiring people over costly machines because:

  • Low Wages: Employers had little incentive to replace workers
  • Seasonal Demand: Many industries needed extra workers only during peak seasons
  • Product Variety: Machines produced standardised goods; markets demanded intricate designs
  • Prestige of Handmade: Upper classes associated handmade goods with refinement and status
  • High Investment Risk: Machines required capital, maintenance, and repairs

4.2 Seasonal Industries

IndustryBusy Season

⚠️ Important: In countries with labour shortages (like 19th-century America), industrialists preferred machines. But in Britain, cheap labour was available, so hand labour remained important.

5. Life of the Workers in Victorian Britain

5.1 Hardships Faced by Workers

  • Long Working Hours: Workers worked long hours with no job security
  • Low Wages: Abundance of labour kept wages low
  • Seasonal Unemployment: Workers were employed during busy months and jobless afterward
  • No Job Security: Workers could be fired anytime

5.2 The Spinning Jenny and Worker Resistance

The Spinning Jenny, devised by James Hargreaves in 1764, enabled one worker to operate several spindles at once. This threatened the livelihoods of women who depended on hand spinning.

  • Fear of unemployment made workers hostile to new technology
  • Women attacked the machines because they threatened to reduce wages and jobs
  • This led to protests like the Luddite Movement (1811-1817)

5.3 Improvement After 1840s

After the 1840s, building activity intensified in cities, opening up new employment opportunities:

  • Roads were widened
  • New railway stations came up
  • Railway lines were extended
  • Tunnels dug, drainage and sewers laid
  • Rivers were embanked

6. The Age of Indian Textiles

6.1 India's Dominance in Global Textile Market

Before the age of machine industries, Indian silk and cotton goods dominated the international market. The finest varieties of cotton came from India.

6.2 Trade Routes and Ports

6.3 The Indian Commercial Network

Indian merchants and bankers financed production, carried goods, and supplied exporters. Supply merchants gave advances to weavers and bought woven cloth.

💡 Key Point: This entire export network was controlled by Indian merchants until the 1750s. As European companies gained power, the old ports of Surat and Hooghly declined, while new colonial ports of Bombay and Calcutta grew.

7. What Happened to Indian Weavers?

7.1 East India Company's Control

After gaining political power in Bengal (Battle of Plassey, 1757), the East India Company wanted to eliminate competition and control costs.

  • Removed existing traders and brokers
  • Appointed paid officials called gomasthas to supervise weavers
  • Introduced a system of advances — loans to buy raw materials
  • Once weavers accepted advances, they were bound to sell only to the Company

7.2 Who Were Gomasthas?

Gomasthas were paid servants appointed by the East India Company to:

  • Supervise weavers
  • Collect supplies
  • Examine the quality of cloth

Unlike earlier supply merchants who lived in villages and had close relationships with weavers, gomasthas were outsiders. They acted arrogantly and often punished weavers for delays.

7.3 Weavers' Problems

  • Lost the freedom to bargain for prices
  • Could not sell to other buyers
  • Received miserably low prices from the Company
  • Were trapped in debt due to advances
⚠️ Clashes Between Weavers and Gomasthas: In many weaving villages, weavers clashed with gomasthas. Some weavers deserted villages, others refused loans, shut workshops, or became agricultural labourers.

8. Manchester Comes to India

8.1 Decline of Indian Textile Exports

Indian textile exports declined dramatically in the 19th century:

PeriodPiece-goods as % of India's exports
1811-1233%
1850-513%

8.2 Flooding of Indian Markets

As British cotton industries grew, industrial groups pushed for import duties on cotton textiles. British manufactures were sold in Indian markets. By the 1870s, cotton piece-goods formed over 50% of Indian imports.

8.3 Dual Problems for Indian Weavers

  • Export Market Collapsed: Indian textiles could not compete with cheaper British goods
  • Local Market Shrank: Indian markets were flooded with cheap Manchester imports

8.4 American Civil War (1860s)

During the American Civil War, cotton supplies from the US were cut off. Britain turned to India for raw cotton, and raw cotton prices increased. Weavers had to buy raw cotton at high prices, making weaving less profitable.

9.2 Early Indian Entrepreneurs

  • Dwarkanath Tagore: Made his fortune in the China trade; later invested in industries
  • Dinshaw Petit and Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Tata: Built large industrial empires in Bombay
  • Seth Hukumchand: Marwari businessman; set up first Indian jute mill in Calcutta (1917)

9.3 Where Did Factory Workers Come From?

  • Most workers came from nearby districts
  • Over 50% of Bombay cotton mill workers in 1911 came from Ratnagiri
  • Kanpur mills drew workers from villages within Kanpur district
  • Workers moved between village and city, returning home during harvests

9.4 Role of the Jobber

Industrialists employed jobbers to recruit workers. A jobber was often an old and trusted worker who:

  • Brought workers from his village
  • Helped them settle in the city
  • Gave money during difficult times
  • Gradually began demanding money and gifts for this favour

9.5 First World War and Indian Industries

The First World War (1914-1918) proved to be a boon to Indian industries:

  • British factories focused on war production; exports to India fell
  • Indian manufacturers faced less foreign competition
  • Military demand increased; Indian factories received new orders
  • Production expanded; more workers and longer working hours were required
  • Indian mills captured home markets and substituted foreign manufactures

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Terms:
  • Proto-industrialisation: Large-scale production before factories, in homes and villages
  • Spinning Jenny: Machine devised by James Hargreaves (1764) to speed up spinning
  • Gomastha: Paid agent of East India Company to supervise weavers
  • Jobber: Person employed to recruit workers for factories
  • Fly Shuttle: Device that increased weaver's speed and productivity
🎯 Key People:
  • Richard Arkwright: Created the cotton mill
  • James Hargreaves: Devised the Spinning Jenny (1764)
  • James Watt: Improved steam engine technology
  • Dwarkanath Tagore: Early Indian entrepreneur
  • Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Tata: Major Indian industrialist

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing proto-industrialisation (home-based) with factory production (centralised)
  • Forgetting that less than 20% of Britain's workforce was in advanced sectors even by late 19th century
  • Mixing up gomasthas (Company agents) with jobbers (factory recruiters)
  • Not mentioning Spinning Jenny when discussing worker resistance to machines
