Print Culture and the Modern World High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter traces the history of print from its beginnings in East Asia to its expansion in Europe and India. It explains how print technology changed society, spread ideas of enlightenment and revolution, and transformed political and social life. Board exams frequently test the print revolution, Gutenberg's press, and the impact of print in colonial India.

1.1 Introduction

It is difficult for us to imagine a world without printed matter. We find evidence of print everywhere around us — in books, journals, newspapers, prints of famous paintings, and also in everyday things like theatre programmes, official circulars, calendars, diaries, advertisements, and cinema posters.

We take for granted this world of print and often forget that there was a time before print. Print itself has a history which has shaped our contemporary world.

🎯 Key Point: Print culture refers to the production, distribution, and consumption of printed material. It played a crucial role in shaping modern society, politics, and culture.

2. The First Printed Books

2.1 Print in China, Japan and Korea

The earliest kind of print technology was developed in China, Japan and Korea. This was a system of hand printing.

  • From AD 594 onwards, books in China were printed by rubbing paper against the inked surface of woodblocks.
  • Both sides of the thin, porous sheet could not be printed, so the traditional Chinese 'accordion book' was folded and stitched at the side.
  • Superbly skilled craftsmen could duplicate, with remarkable accuracy, the beauty of calligraphy.

2.2 Role of the Imperial State in China

For a very long time, the imperial state in China was the major producer of printed material.

  • China possessed a huge bureaucratic system which recruited its personnel through civil service examinations.
  • Textbooks for this examination were printed in vast numbers under the sponsorship of the imperial state.
  • From the sixteenth century, the number of examination candidates went up and that increased the volume of print.

2.3 Diversification of Print in China (17th Century)

By the seventeenth century, as urban culture bloomed in China, the uses of print diversified:

  • Print was no longer used just by scholar-officials
  • Merchants used print in their everyday life, as they collected trade information
  • Reading increasingly became a leisure activity
  • The new readership preferred fictional narratives, poetry, autobiographies, anthologies of literary masterpieces, and romantic plays
  • Rich women began to read, and many women began publishing their poetry and plays

2.4 Print in Japan

  • Buddhist missionaries from China introduced hand-printing technology into Japan around AD 768-770.
  • The oldest Japanese book, printed in AD 868, is the Buddhist Diamond Sutra, containing six sheets of text and woodcut illustrations.
  • Pictures were printed on textiles, playing cards and paper money.
  • In medieval Japan, poets and prose writers were regularly published, and books were cheap and abundant.
  • In the late eighteenth century, in the flourishing urban circles at Edo (later known as Tokyo), illustrated collections of paintings depicted an elegant urban culture.

3. Print Comes to Europe

3.1 Marco Polo and Woodblock Printing

In 1295, the Italian explorer Marco Polo returned to Italy from China. He brought back the knowledge of woodblock printing to Europe.

By the early fifteenth century, woodblock printing gradually replaced the books made by calligraphy.

3.2 Gutenberg and the Printing Press

Johannes Gutenberg developed the printing press in the 1430s in Germany.

  • He used the olive press as the model for the printing press.
  • He used moulds for casting metal types for the letters.
  • Gutenberg perfected the system by 1448.
  • The first book printed by him was the Bible.
🎯 Key Point: Gutenberg's printing press was revolutionary. It allowed the mass production of books, drastically reducing costs and time, and brought printed works to the general public.

3.3 Why Woodblock Printing Only Came to Europe After 1295

The knowledge of woodblock printing reached Europe only after Marco Polo returned from China in 1295. Before this, books in Europe were handwritten and extremely expensive.

4. The Print Revolution and Its Impact

4.1 A New Reading Public

With the print technology, a new reading public emerged:

  • Books became cheaper because of printing
  • Numerous copies could now be produced with much ease
  • Popular ballads and folk tales were published which could be listened to by even the illiterates
  • Literate people read out stories and ballads to those who could not read

4.2 Religious Discourse and Fear of Print

  • Print created an opportunity for new debate and discussion
  • People began questioning some established notions of religion
  • The Roman Church felt troubled by new ideas which raised questions about the existing norms of faith
  • It started to maintain an Index of Prohibited Books from 1558

4.3 Martin Luther and the Protestant Reformation

In 1517, Martin Luther wrote the Ninety-Five Theses, criticizing Church practices like the sale of indulgences.

  • Printers quickly reproduced and distributed his writings across Europe
  • His ideas reached thousands, sparking the Protestant Reformation
  • Print helped him spread his message faster than the Church could suppress it
💡 Impact on Renaissance and Reformation: Print enabled faster dissemination of classical Greek and Roman literature, fuelling Renaissance ideals in art, science, and philosophy. It also enabled more people to read religious texts directly, weakening the Church's control over religious interpretation.

5. The Reading Mania

5.1 Growth of Literacy in Europe

The literacy levels improved through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries in Europe:

  • By the end of the eighteenth century, literacy rates were as high as 60 to 80 percent in some parts of Europe
  • Ideas of scientists and philosophers became more accessible to the common people
  • New ideas could be debated and shared with a wider target audience

5.2 New Forms of Publication

  • By the close of the eighteenth century, books became the medium of progress and enlightenment
  • The writings of eminent thinkers and philosophers such as Jean Jacques Rousseau, Thomas Paine and Voltaire were widely printed and circulated
  • Their notions about science, rationality and reasoning entered the corpus of literature
  • Cartographers began to curate authentic maps and scientific diagrams, which were widely printed

5.3 Children, Women and Workers (19th Century)

The nineteenth century brought new readers into the world of print:

New ReadersPrinted Material
ChildrenSchool books and fairy tales; children's press set up in France in 1857; Grimm Brothers collected folk tales in Germany
WomenPenny magazines, manuals, novels; Jane Austen, Bronte sisters, George Eliot became well-known novelists
WorkersLending libraries became common; workers read political tracts and wrote autobiographies

5.4 Further Innovations in Print Technology

  • By the late eighteenth century, the press was made of metal
  • Richard M. Hoe of New York perfected the power-driven cylindrical press, which could print about 8,000 sheets per hour
  • In the late nineteenth century, the offset press was developed, which could print up to six colours at a time
  • From the early twentieth century, electrically operated presses improved print speed

6. Print Culture and the French Revolution

Many historians argue that print culture helped create conditions for the French Revolution. Print did not directly cause the Revolution, but it opened new ways of thinking.

ArgumentExplanation
Enlightenment ideas spreadPrint spread the ideas of Enlightenment thinkers like Voltaire and Rousseau, who questioned tradition, superstition and despotism
Dialogue increasedPrint created a culture of dialogue and debate; people discussed values, institutions and authority
Royalty was criticisedPrinted literature mocked the royalty and criticised their morality; cartoons and pamphlets showed the suffering of common people
Reason gained importancePeople judged ideas through rational thinking
🎯 Key Point: Print culture changed public opinion, giving people the intellectual power to question authority and demand change.

7. India and the World of Print

7.1 Manuscripts Before Print in India

India had a long manuscript tradition before printing presses arrived:

  • Handwritten manuscripts in Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian and several vernacular languages
  • They were copied on palm leaves or handmade paper
  • Manuscripts were sometimes pressed between wooden covers or stitched together
  • They were often beautifully illustrated
  • Manuscripts were expensive and fragile; they had to be handled carefully and were not easy to read
  • In Bengal, many students learnt to write but did not read printed texts; teachers often dictated portions from memory

7.3 Religious Reform and Public Debate

Print encouraged religious debate in India:

  • Raja Rammohun Roy published the Sambad Kaumudi from 1821
  • Hindu orthodoxy replied through the Samachar Chandrika
  • Muslim reformers used cheap lithographic presses to publish religious texts and issued newspapers to debate religious practices
  • Print helped communities read, interpret and debate religious ideas, creating a wider public space
💡 Political Awakening: Newspapers became a platform for anti-colonial sentiment. Indian leaders and intellectuals used the press to voice opposition to British policies, shaping public opinion and awareness.

8. Religious Reform and Public Debate

8.1 Print and Social Reform

  • Raja Ram Mohan Roy published against sati, child marriage, and idol worship
  • Tracts and newspapers spread ideas of reform
  • Both reformers and orthodox Hindus used print to spread their views
  • Debates on widow remarriage were conducted through print
  • Religious texts printed in vernacular languages for common people

8.2 Print and Multiple Voices

A notable feature of the print revolution in India was the variety of voices it enabled:

  • Not just the educated elite, but also people from lower castes like Jyotiba Phule and later B.R. Ambedkar used print to expose caste injustice
  • Local protest movements and sects also used print to spread their ideas

9. Print and Women

9.1 Women as Readers and Writers

  • Lives of women were written by both men and women
  • Liberal husbands and fathers taught women to read
  • Women's journals encouraged female education and awareness

9.2 Notable Women Writers

🎯 Key Point: Print opened new intellectual spaces for women despite resistance. By the early 20th century, women themselves began writing, expressing emotions, struggles, and aspirations.

10. Print and Censorship

10.1 Early Regulations

  • Before 1798, the colonial state had no control over the press
  • 1798: Governor-General Lord Wellesley imposed censorship rules; newspapers had to be pre-approved before publication
  • 1835: Governor-General William Bentinck revoked these restrictions

10.2 Vernacular Press Act, 1878

The British government passed the Vernacular Press Act in 1878 to curb the influence of Indian-language newspapers.

  • Passed by: Viceroy Lord Lytton
  • Government could censor Indian language newspapers
  • If a report was found "seditious", the press could be confiscated
  • Printing machinery could be seized
  • Modelled on Irish Press Laws
  • Criticised as "Gagging Act" — attempted to silence Indian press
  • English newspapers were exempted — discrimination!

10.3 Nationalist Response to Censorship

  • Nationalists protested against censorship laws
  • Many newspapers continued publishing despite restrictions
  • Some editors were arrested and jailed
  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak was imprisoned for his writings in Kesari
  • Press became a powerful symbol of nationalism
💡 Gandhi's Words: "The fight for Swaraj is a fight for liberty of speech, liberty of the press, and freedom of association."

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing Gutenberg (1448) with Marco Polo (1295) — Polo brought woodblock printing, Gutenberg developed the printing press
  • Forgetting that Index of Prohibited Books was maintained by the Roman Catholic Church
  • Mixing up Vernacular Press Act (1878) with other colonial laws
  • Not mentioning Rashsundari Debi when discussing women and print in India
  • Forgetting that English newspapers were exempted from the Vernacular Press Act
