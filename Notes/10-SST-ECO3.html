Money and Credit High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains the evolution of money, the working of the banking system, and the concept of credit. It teaches you how money solves the problems of the barter system, how banks function, and the difference between formal and informal sources of credit. Board exams frequently test modern forms of money, credit situations, and SHGs.

1.1 Barter System

Before money was invented, people used the barter system — direct exchange of goods for goods without using money.

Example: A farmer with wheat exchanges it for shoes from a cobbler.

1.2 Double Coincidence of Wants

The barter system required a double coincidence of wants — both parties must agree to sell and buy each other's commodities at the same time.

Example: A shoe seller who wants bread must find a baker who wants shoes. This is difficult and time-consuming.

1.3 How Money Solves This Problem

Money acts as a medium of exchange. A person holding money can easily exchange it for any commodity or service they want.

Money eliminates the need for double coincidence of wants because the seller only needs to find a buyer — not someone who has exactly what the seller wants.

🎯 Key Point: Money is anything that has common acceptability as a means of exchange, a measure of value, and a store of value.

2. Modern Forms of Money

2.1 Evolution of Money

In early ages, Indians used grains and cattle as money. Later, metallic coins of gold, silver, and copper were used. Today, the modern forms of money are currency and bank deposits.

2.2 Currency

Currency includes paper notes and coins. In India, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) issues currency notes on behalf of the central government.

  • No other individual or organisation is allowed to issue currency.
  • The rupee is widely accepted as a medium of exchange in India.
  • On a 10 rupee note, "Reserve Bank of India" and "Guaranteed by Central Government" are written.

2.3 Deposits in Banks

People also hold money as deposits with banks by opening a bank account.

  • Banks accept deposits and pay interest on them.
  • Deposits that can be withdrawn on demand are called demand deposits.
  • Payments are made using cheques instead of cash.

2.4 Cheque

A cheque is a paper instructing the bank to pay a specific amount from the person's account to the person in whose name the cheque has been issued.

🎯 Key Point: Modern forms of money — currency and deposits — are closely linked to the workings of the modern banking system.

3. Loan Activities of Banks

3.1 How Banks Use Deposits

Banks keep only a small proportion of their deposits as cash. In India, banks hold about 15% of their deposits as cash. This is kept as a provision to pay depositors who may come to withdraw money.

The major portion of deposits is used to extend loans for various economic activities.

3.2 Banks as Intermediaries

Banks mediate between those who have surplus funds (depositors) and those who need funds (borrowers).

  • Banks pay interest to depositors.
  • Banks charge a higher interest rate on loans than what they offer on deposits.
  • The difference between what is charged from borrowers and what is paid to depositors is the bank's main source of income.
💡 Example: A bank might offer 4% interest on savings deposits but charge 10% on a car loan.

4. Two Different Credit Situations

4.1 What is Credit?

Credit (loan) refers to an agreement in which the lender supplies the borrower with money, goods, or services in return for the promise of future payment.

4.2 Salim's Story — Credit Helps

Salim is a shoe manufacturer. During the festival season, he receives a large order. He needs leather, workers, and other inputs.

  • He gets credit from the leather supplier and a trader.
  • He completes production, earns profit, and repays the borrowed amount.
  • Credit increases his earnings — he is better off than before.

4.3 Swapna's Story — Credit Pushes into Debt Trap

Swapna is a small farmer. She takes a loan for groundnut cultivation. Her crop fails due to pests.

  • She cannot repay the loan.
  • The debt grows, and she takes a fresh loan the next year.
  • She has to sell part of her land to repay the loan.
  • Credit pushes her into a debt trap — she is worse off than before.
Key Lesson: Whether credit is useful or harmful depends on the risks in the situation and whether there is some support in case of loss.

4.4 Debt Trap

A debt trap is a situation where a borrower is unable to repay the loan and must take more loans or sell assets to repay old debt.

Causes:

  • Crop failure or business loss
  • Very high interest rate
  • No savings or support
  • Harsh repayment conditions

5. Terms of Credit

Every loan agreement specifies terms of credit that the borrower must agree to.

TermMeaning
Interest RateThe cost of borrowing, shown as a percentage of the loan
CollateralAn asset the borrower owns (land, building, vehicle, livestock, bank deposits) and uses as a guarantee until the loan is repaid
DocumentationPapers and information the borrower must provide (ID proof, address proof, income statements)
Mode of RepaymentInstallments or lump sum; cash, gold, or property

5.1 Collateral in Detail

If the borrower fails to repay the loan, the lender has the right to sell the collateral to recover the money.

Banks often ask for collateral before giving loans. This creates a problem for poor borrowers because many of them do not own assets that can be used as collateral.

🎯 Key Point: Terms of credit may vary depending on the nature of the lender and the borrower.

6. Formal Sector Credit in India

6.1 Definition

Formal sector loans include loans from banks and cooperatives. These sources are supervised and follow rules.

6.2 Characteristics of Formal Credit

  • Lower interest rates compared to informal sources
  • Regulated by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI)
  • Requires documentation, credit checks, and sometimes collateral
  • Helps people borrow for farming, business, housing, and other productive needs

6.3 Why Formal Credit is Important

Cheap and affordable credit is crucial for the country's development.

  • It creates opportunities for self-employment
  • It helps poor people invest in new technology and resources
  • It expands trade and commerce, leading to GDP growth
  • It ends the cycle of debt trap
💡 Example: Arun has seven acres of land and receives a bank loan at 8.5% per annum. He can repay it over three years.

7. Informal Sector Credit in India

7.1 Definition

Informal sector loans include loans from moneylenders, traders, employers, relatives, and friends. There is no organisation to supervise the functioning of informal sources of credit.

7.2 Characteristics of Informal Credit

  • Higher interest rates — sometimes 5% per month (60% per annum)
  • No government regulation or supervision
  • Flexible terms and documentation
  • Often involves exploitation and unfair means to recover money

7.3 Why Poor Households Depend on Informal Credit

  • Banking facilities are not available everywhere in rural India
  • Banks require collateral and proper documentation which poor households lack
  • Moneylenders know borrowers personally and are willing to give loans without collateral
  • Most poor households are uneducated and find bank processes difficult
⚠️ Problem with Informal Credit: High interest rates mean a large part of the borrower's earnings goes into repaying loans. This can lead to a debt trap.

8. Self-Help Groups (SHGs)

8.1 What are SHGs?

Self-Help Groups (SHGs) are small voluntary groups, usually of 15–20 members, often women from rural areas. They pool their savings and provide loans to members at reasonable interest rates.

8.2 How SHGs Work

  • Members pool their savings depending on their ability
  • Members can avail timely loans from the group at reasonable or lower interest rates than moneylenders
  • If the group is regular for more than a year, it can get a loan from a bank for self-employment activities
  • All decisions are taken by members collectively

8.3 Benefits of SHGs

  • Empowering Women: Promotes financial independence
  • Reduces Dependence on Moneylenders: Provides fair credit access
  • Acts as a Bridge to Banks: Facilitates access to formal banking
  • Solves Collateral Problem: Members get loans without collateral
  • Platform for Social Issues: Helps discuss health, nutrition, etc.
🎯 Key Point: SHGs are the building blocks of the rural economy — they help the poor access credit without collateral and at reasonable terms.

9. Role of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI)

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) supervises the functioning of formal sources of loans.

9.1 Functions of RBI

  • Issues Currency: Issues currency notes on behalf of the central government
  • Monitors Cash Reserves: Ensures banks maintain minimum cash balances to meet depositors' withdrawal needs
  • Regulates Lending: Ensures banks lend not just for profit to traders but also to small borrowers, small cultivators, and small industries
  • Collects Information: Banks submit information on how much they are lending, to whom, and at what interest rate
  • Ensures Fair Practices: Protects customers from unfair terms
  • Banker to Government: Acts as the custodian of foreign exchange reserves
Why RBI Supervision Matters: If there is no supervision of informal lending, moneylenders can charge whatever interest rate they wish and use unfair means to get their money back.

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Terms:
  • Barter System: Direct exchange of goods without money
  • Double Coincidence of Wants: Both parties must want each other's goods
  • Money: Medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value
  • Demand Deposits: Bank deposits withdrawable on demand
  • Credit: Loan agreement for future payment
  • Collateral: Asset pledged as guarantee for loan
  • Debt Trap: Unable to repay loan, must take more loans
  • SHG: Self-Help Group of 15–20 members pooling savings
🎯 Key Comparisons:
  • Formal Credit: Banks, cooperatives — low interest, RBI supervised
  • Informal Credit: Moneylenders, traders — high interest, no supervision
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing barter system with a money-based economy
  • Thinking money only acts as a medium of exchange — it also measures and stores value
  • Forgetting that RBI supervises only formal sources, not informal ones
  • Not giving examples when asked about credit situations (Salim vs Swapna)
