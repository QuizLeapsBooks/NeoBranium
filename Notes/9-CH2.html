Is Matter Around Us Pure High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter helps you understand the difference between pure substances and mixtures, and how to separate them. Questions on separation techniques, colloids, and the Tyndall effect are frequently asked in board exams.

1. Pure Substances

A pure substance consists of a single type of particles and has a uniform and definite composition. Pure substances are classified into two categories:

  • Elements: A basic form of matter that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions. Examples: Iron, Gold, Oxygen, Hydrogen.
  • Compounds: Substances composed of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion. Examples: Water (H₂O), Sodium Chloride (NaCl), Carbon Dioxide (CO₂).
🔑 Key Difference: Elements vs Compounds
  • Elements are made of one type of atom. Compounds are made of two or more different types of atoms chemically bonded.
  • The properties of a compound are different from its constituent elements. For example, hydrogen gas burns and oxygen supports combustion, but water (H₂O) extinguishes fire.

2. Mixtures and Solutions

A mixture is made up of two or more substances physically combined, where each substance retains its own properties. Mixtures can be separated using physical methods.

Types of Mixtures

  • Homogeneous Mixture: Has a uniform composition throughout. The components are not visibly distinguishable. Example: Sugar dissolved in water, Air.
  • Heterogeneous Mixture: Has a non-uniform composition. The components are visibly different. Example: Sand and iron filings, Oil and water.

Solutions

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. It consists of a solute (the substance that is dissolved) and a solvent (the substance in which the solute is dissolved).

📌 Properties of a Solution:
  • It is clear and transparent.
  • Particles are too small to be seen by the naked eye (less than 1 nm).
  • It does not scatter light (no Tyndall effect).
  • Cannot be separated by filtration.

Concentration of a Solution

The concentration of a solution is the amount of solute present in a given amount of solution. It can be expressed as:

Mass by Mass % = (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) × 100

Mass by Volume % = (Mass of solute / Volume of solution) × 100
  • Saturated Solution: A solution in which no more solute can be dissolved at a given temperature.
  • Unsaturated Solution: A solution in which more solute can be dissolved at a given temperature.

3. Colloids and Suspensions

Suspension

A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture in which the solute particles do not dissolve but remain suspended throughout the bulk of the medium. Example: Chalk powder in water, Muddy water.

Properties of Suspension:
  • Particles are large and visible to the naked eye (greater than 1000 nm).
  • It scatters light (shows Tyndall effect).
  • Can be separated by filtration.
  • Not stable - particles settle down over time.

Colloidal Solution

A colloid is a heterogeneous mixture where the particle size lies between that of a true solution and a suspension (1 nm to 1000 nm). Example: Milk, Fog, Smoke, Ink.

Properties of Colloids:
  • Particles are not visible to the naked eye but are large enough to scatter light.
  • Shows the Tyndall Effect - the scattering of a beam of light by colloidal particles.
  • Cannot be separated by ordinary filter paper.
  • Relatively stable - particles do not settle down easily.
Tyndall Effect Example: When sunlight passes through the canopy of a dense forest, the path of light becomes visible because tiny water droplets and dust particles in the air scatter the light.

Types of Colloids

Dispersed Phase | Dispersion Medium | Type | Example

Liquid | Gas | Aerosol | Fog, Clouds
Solid | Gas | Aerosol | Smoke
Gas | Liquid | Foam | Shaving Cream
Liquid | Liquid | Emulsion | Milk
Solid | Liquid | Sol | Milk of Magnesia

4. Separation Techniques

Various methods are used to separate the components of mixtures. The choice of technique depends on the nature of the components.

Evaporation

Used to separate a volatile solvent from a non-volatile solute. Example: Separating salt from saltwater.

Centrifugation

Used to separate heavier particles from lighter particles in a liquid. The denser particles settle at the bottom. Example: Separating cream from milk.

Separating Funnel

Used to separate two immiscible liquids based on the difference in their densities. The heavier liquid forms the lower layer. Example: Separating oil and water.

Chromatography

Used to separate colored components (dyes/pigments) that are dissolved in a solvent. Example: Separating dyes from black ink.

Distillation

Used to separate two miscible liquids with different boiling points. Example: Separating acetone and water.

Sublimation

Used to separate a sublimating substance from a non-sublimating substance. Example: Separating ammonium chloride from sodium chloride.

🎯 Quick Reference Table (Exam Favourite):
  • Sodium chloride from water: Evaporation
  • Ammonium chloride from salt: Sublimation
  • Cream from milk: Centrifugation
  • Oil from water: Separating funnel
  • Dyes from ink: Chromatography
  • Iron filings from sand: Magnetic separation
  • Tea leaves from tea: Filtration

Most Repeated Previous Year Questions

  1. Differentiate between a mixture and a compound. (3 Marks) — Answer: A mixture is formed by physical combination where components retain their properties and can be separated by physical methods. A compound is formed by chemical combination in a fixed ratio, has new properties, and can only be separated by chemical methods.
  2. What is the Tyndall effect? Give an example. (2 Marks) — Answer: The scattering of a beam of light by colloidal particles is called the Tyndall effect. Example: Sunlight passing through a dense forest canopy becomes visible due to scattering by dust and water droplets.
  3. Which separation technique will you apply for the separation of butter from curd? (1 Mark) — Answer: Centrifugation.
  4. What is meant by an aqueous and non-aqueous solution? Give one example each. (2 Marks) — Answer: An aqueous solution is prepared in water (e.g., sodium chloride in water). A non-aqueous solution is prepared in a solvent other than water (e.g., iodine in carbon tetrachloride).
  5. Why does copper sulphate solution in water not show the Tyndall effect but a mixture of water and milk shows? (2 Marks) — Answer: Copper sulphate forms a true solution with particles smaller than 1 nm, which do not scatter light. Milk forms a colloidal solution with larger particles (1-1000 nm) that scatter light, showing the Tyndall effect.
  6. Name the separation technique by which we can obtain coloured components from ink. (1 Mark) — Answer: Chromatography.
  7. Calculate the concentration of a solution in terms of mass by mass percentage if 36 g of sodium chloride is dissolved in 100 g of water. (2 Marks) — Answer: Mass of solution = 36 + 100 = 136 g. Concentration = (36/136) × 100 = 26.47%.
  8. Why is a mixture called a substance that can be separated by physical methods? (2 Marks) — Answer: Because the components of a mixture are not chemically combined; they retain their individual properties and can be separated using physical techniques like filtration, evaporation, etc..
  9. How are sol, solution, and suspension different from each other? (3 Marks) — Answer: A solution is a homogeneous mixture with particles less than 1 nm. A colloid (sol) is a heterogeneous mixture with particles between 1-1000 nm, showing the Tyndall effect. A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture with particles larger than 1000 nm that settle down and can be separated by filtration.
  10. What is crystallization? Give its applications. (2 Marks) — Answer: Crystallization is a technique to purify solids by dissolving them in a solvent and allowing pure crystals to form. Applications: Purification of salt, pharmaceutical drugs, and sugar refining.

Quick Revision — At a Glance

🎯 Key Formulas & Concepts:
  • Concentration (Mass by Mass %): (Mass of solute / Mass of solution) × 100
  • Concentration (Mass by Volume %): (Mass of solute / Volume of solution) × 100
  • Tyndall Effect: Scattering of light by colloidal particles
  • Particle Size: Solution < 1 nm | Colloid: 1-1000 nm | Suspension > 1000 nm
  • Saturated Solution: No more solute can dissolve at a given temperature
🎯 Key Tips for Exam:
  • Elements are pure substances made of one type of atom; compounds are made of two or more elements chemically combined.
  • Solutions are homogeneous and transparent; colloids scatter light (Tyndall effect); suspensions are heterogeneous and settle down.
  • Alloys are considered mixtures because they show properties of their constituents and can be separated by physical means.
  • Chromatography is used to separate colored components (dyes, pigments).
  • Centrifugation is used for separating cream from milk and in diagnostic laboratories for blood tests.
  • Sublimation is used for substances like camphor, naphthalene, and ammonium chloride.
Did You Know? The term "colloid" comes from the Greek word "kolla" meaning glue. Colloids are found everywhere — from the milk you drink to the fog outside. The Tyndall effect, named after physicist John Tyndall, is used to distinguish colloids from true solutions!
