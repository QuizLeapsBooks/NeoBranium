The Making of a Global World Partially in Board Syllabus

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains how the world became interconnected through trade, migration, and the exchange of goods, ideas, and diseases. It covers the Silk Routes, the Columbian Exchange, and the impact of European conquests. For board exams, focus on the Pre-Modern World section.

1.1 What is Globalisation?

Globalisation refers to the rapid integration or interconnection between countries. It involves the movement of goods, services, investments, technology, and people across national boundaries.

While globalisation is often associated with the modern era, the process of global interconnectedness has a long history spanning thousands of years.

🎯 Key Point: The world we live in today is interconnected through trade, travel, and communication. This chapter traces the historical roots of this global integration.

2. The Pre-Modern World

2.1 Human Societies and Interconnection

Human societies have been interconnected for thousands of years. People travelled long distances in search of food, work, and opportunities. They also exchanged goods, ideas, and cultures along the way.

2.2 The Silk Routes

The Silk Routes were a network of trade routes connecting Asia, Europe, and Africa. They were not just one road but multiple routes — both overland and maritime.

Route TypeDescriptionGoods Traded
Overland Silk RoutesAcross Central Asia, connecting China to the MediterraneanChinese silk, Indian textiles, Persian carpets, Arabian horses
Maritime Silk RoutesAcross the Indian Ocean, connecting East Africa, Arabia, India, and Southeast AsiaSpices, textiles, precious stones, gold

The Silk Routes were important not just for trade but also for the exchange of:

  • Ideas and religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam)
  • Technologies and inventions
  • Cultures and languages
  • Diseases and germs
💡 Example: Chinese silk was traded along the Silk Routes and became popular in Rome. In return, Chinese traders received gold, silver, and wool from the West.

3.2 Columbus and the New World

Christopher Columbus's voyage to the Americas in 1492 marked a turning point in global history. This led to the exchange of plants, animals, and diseases between the Old World (Europe, Asia, Africa) and the New World (Americas).

This exchange is called the Columbian Exchange.

3.3 Impact of Food Exchange

  • Potato and maize became staple foods in Europe, helping to feed a growing population
  • Chilli and tomato became integral to Indian cuisine
  • Maize and potato spread to Africa and Asia
  • Food exchange led to changes in dietary habits and agricultural practices worldwide
🎯 Key Point: Food travels are a perfect example of how globalisation worked in pre-modern times. What we eat today is the result of centuries of exchange across continents.

4. Conquest, Disease, and Trade

4.1 The Spanish Conquest of the Americas

The Spanish conquest of the Americas in the 16th century was one of the most significant events in global history.

  • 1520: Spanish conquerors (conquistadors) reached Mexico
  • 1530s: They conquered the Inca Empire in Peru
  • 1550s: They established control over much of South and Central America

4.2 Smallpox and Devastation

The most devastating weapon the Spanish had was not guns or horses but disease. Smallpox, to which the native Americans had no immunity, killed millions of people.

  • Smallpox spread rapidly among the native population
  • The native population of the Americas declined dramatically — by some estimates, up to 90%
  • This made it easier for the Spanish to conquer and control these regions
⚠️ Historical Impact: The smallpox epidemic was one of the deadliest events in human history. It wiped out entire communities and civilizations, changing the demographic and cultural landscape of the Americas forever.

4.3 Portuguese Trade in the Indian Ocean

While the Spanish conquered the Americas, the Portuguese dominated trade in the Indian Ocean.

  • 1498: Vasco da Gama reached India (Calicut)
  • The Portuguese established trading posts along the Indian coast
  • They controlled the spice trade from India to Europe
  • Portuguese traders also brought new food items to India

4.4 Precious Metals and the Slave Trade

The conquest of the Americas also led to the extraction of precious metals and the expansion of the slave trade.

  • Gold and silver from the Americas (especially from Mexico and Peru) flowed to Europe
  • This increased Europe's wealth and purchasing power
  • The Portuguese and Spanish also began the transatlantic slave trade, forcibly transporting Africans to work on plantations in the Americas
💡 Example: Silver from Potosí (Bolivia) was used by the Spanish to trade with China and India. It became a global currency, showing how interconnected the world had become.

5. The Nineteenth Century (1815-1914) Internal Assessment Only

⚠️ Note: This section is part of the Interdisciplinary Project (Internal Assessment) for the 2025-26 academic year, not the Board Examination.

5.1 Globalisation in the Nineteenth Century

The 19th century saw a dramatic increase in global trade and interconnection. Three main factors drove this:

  • Expansion of Railways: Railways were built across continents, making transportation faster and cheaper
  • Steam Ships: Steamships reduced travel time and costs for goods and people
  • Telegraph: The telegraph allowed instant communication across long distances

5.2 The Role of Britain

Britain played a central role in 19th-century globalisation:

  • Britain was the world's leading industrial power
  • It exported manufactured goods to colonies and other countries
  • It imported raw materials from colonies like India, Egypt, and South Africa
  • London became the financial capital of the world

5.3 The Role of Indian Trade

India played a crucial role in the global economy of the 19th century:

  • India exported cotton, indigo, opium, and tea
  • India imported British manufactured goods, especially textiles
  • Indian merchants and traders had extensive networks across the Indian Ocean and beyond
  • Indian indentured labourers were sent to work on plantations in Fiji, Mauritius, Trinidad, and other colonies

6. Indian Trade and the Global World Internal Assessment Only

⚠️ Note: This section is part of the Interdisciplinary Project (Internal Assessment) for the 2025-26 academic year, not the Board Examination.

6.1 India's Role in the Global Economy

By the end of the 19th century, India had become an important part of the global economy:

  • India was a major exporter of agricultural products — cotton, jute, indigo, tea, and opium
  • India was a major market for British manufactured goods
  • The British built railways and ports in India to facilitate trade
  • Indian merchants (like the Tatas and Birlas) emerged as important players in global trade

6.2 Impact of British Rule on Indian Trade

ImpactDescription
De-industrialisationIndian handicrafts declined due to competition from cheap British machine-made goods
Commercialisation of AgricultureFarmers were forced to grow cash crops (indigo, opium, cotton) instead of food crops
Drain of WealthIndia's wealth was transferred to Britain through trade surpluses
FaminesCommercialisation and food shortages led to devastating famines

6.3 Indian Labour Migration

During the 19th century, thousands of Indian labourers were taken to work on plantations in other colonies:

  • Mauritius: Indian labourers worked on sugar plantations
  • Fiji and Trinidad: Indian workers were brought to work on sugarcane and other plantations
  • South Africa: Mahatma Gandhi began his political activism while working as a lawyer for Indian labourers
  • Assam: Workers from other parts of India were brought to work on tea plantations

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Terms:
  • Silk Routes: Network of trade routes connecting Asia, Europe, and Africa
  • Columbian Exchange: Exchange of plants, animals, and diseases between Old and New Worlds
  • Smallpox: Disease that devastated native American populations
  • Conquistadors: Spanish conquerors of the Americas
  • Transatlantic Slave Trade: Forced transportation of Africans to the Americas
🎯 Key Dates:
1492Columbus reaches the Americas
1498Vasco da Gama reaches India
1520Spanish conquerors reach Mexico
1530sSpanish conquer the Inca Empire
🎯 Food Origins:
  • Potato: South America → Europe, Asia
  • Chilli: South America → India, China
  • Maize: Mexico → Europe, Africa
  • Spaghetti: China → Italy
  • Tomato: South America → Europe, India
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing the Silk Routes (trade network) with the Silk Road (singular) — there were multiple routes
  • Forgetting that smallpox (not guns) was the main reason for the Spanish conquest of the Americas
  • Mixing up Columbus (1492) with Vasco da Gama (1498)
  • Not mentioning the Columbian Exchange when discussing food travels
  • Forgetting that only the Pre-Modern World section is in the Board syllabus for 2025-26
