Chapter 1: Units and Measurements

A foundational chapter dealing with physical quantities, systems of units, and the analysis of errors in measurement.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the International System of Units (SI).
  • Apply dimensional analysis to check the correctness of equations.
  • Calculate absolute, relative, and percentage errors.
  • Determine significant figures in a measurement.

Key Concepts

  • Physical Quantity: A quantity that can be measured. It has a numerical magnitude and a unit (Q = nu).
  • Fundamental Units: Base units that are independent of each other (e.g., length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity).
  • Derived Units: Units obtained by multiplying or dividing fundamental units (e.g., velocity = m/s).
  • Significant Figures: The reliable digits in a measurement plus the first uncertain digit. They indicate the precision of measurement.

Important Definitions

  • Least Count: The smallest value that can be measured by a measuring instrument.
  • Accuracy: How close a measured value is to the true value.
  • Precision: How close the measured values are to each other (resolution of the instrument).

Dimensional Analysis

Dimensions represent the nature of a physical quantity. We use square brackets [ ] to denote dimensions.

  • Mass [M], Length [L], Time [T]
  • Velocity (v) = Displacement / Time = [L] / [T] = [L T⁻¹]
  • Acceleration (a) = Velocity / Time = [L T⁻¹] / [T] = [L T⁻²]
  • Force (F) = Mass × Acceleration = [M] [L T⁻²] = [M L T⁻²]

Principle of Homogeneity: In a correct physical equation, the dimensions of all the terms must be the same.

Important Formulae

  • Absolute Error (Δa): Δa = |True Value - Measured Value|
  • Mean Absolute Error: Δaₘₑₐₙ = (Σ|Δaᵢ|) / n
  • Relative Error: Δaₘₑₐₙ / aₘₑₐₙ
  • Percentage Error: (Relative Error) × 100%
  • Error in Product/Quotient: If Z = A × B or Z = A / B, then ΔZ/Z = (ΔA/A) + (ΔB/B)
  • Error in Power: If Z = Aⁿ, then ΔZ/Z = n × (ΔA/A)

Solved Example

Q: The resistance R = V/I where V = (100 ± 5) V and I = (10 ± 0.2) A. Find the percentage error in R.

Solution:
% error in V = (5/100) × 100 = 5%
% error in I = (0.2/10) × 100 = 2%
Since R = V/I, the relative errors add up.
% error in R = % error in V + % error in I = 5% + 2% = 7%

Answer: 7%

⚠️ Common Mistakes

Students often subtract errors when physical quantities are subtracted (e.g., Z = A - B). Remember that errors always add up to give the maximum possible error, so ΔZ = ΔA + ΔB, not ΔA - ΔB.

📌 Exam-Focused Points

  • Dimensional analysis cannot determine dimensionless constants.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are always significant.
  • Trailing zeros in a number with a decimal point are significant.

Chapter 2: Motion in a Straight Line

Kinematics of a particle moving in one dimension under uniform and non-uniform acceleration.

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate between distance and displacement.
  • Understand average/instantaneous velocity and acceleration.
  • Derive and apply the equations of kinematics for uniformly accelerated motion.
  • Analyze distance-time and velocity-time graphs.

Key Concepts

  • Displacement: Shortest distance from initial to final position (vector).
  • Distance: Total path length covered (scalar).
  • Instantaneous Velocity: Velocity at a specific instant of time. v = dx/dt.
  • Instantaneous Acceleration: Rate of change of velocity at a specific instant. a = dv/dt = d²x/dt².

Derivation of Kinematic Equations (Calculus Method)

Let acceleration 'a' be constant.

1. Velocity-Time Relation:
a = dv/dt ⇒ dv = a dt
Integrating both sides from t=0 (v=u) to t=t (v=v):
∫dv = ∫a dt ⇒ [v] = a[t] ⇒ v = u + at

2. Position-Time Relation:
v = dx/dt ⇒ dx = v dt = (u + at) dt
Integrating from t=0 (x=0) to t=t (x=s):
∫dx = ∫(u + at) dt ⇒ s = ut + ½at²

3. Position-Velocity Relation:
a = dv/dt = (dv/dx)(dx/dt) = v(dv/dx) ⇒ a dx = v dv
Integrating: ∫a dx = ∫v dv ⇒ as = ½(v² - u²) ⇒ v² = u² + 2as

Important Formulae

  • v = u + at (Velocity-time)
  • s = ut + ½at² (Displacement-time)
  • v² = u² + 2as (Velocity-displacement)
  • sₙ = u + a/2 (2n - 1) (Distance traveled in the nth second)
  • Variables: u = initial velocity (m/s), v = final velocity (m/s), a = constant acceleration (m/s²), t = time (s), s = displacement (m).

Solved Example

Q: A car moving with a speed of 50 km/h can be stopped by brakes after at least 6 m. If the same car is moving at a speed of 100 km/h, what is the minimum stopping distance?

Solution:
Using v² = u² + 2as, where final velocity v = 0.
0 = u² + 2as ⇒ s = -u² / 2a ⇒ s ∝ u² (since a is same).
s₂ / s₁ = (u₂ / u₁)² = (100 / 50)² = (2)² = 4
s₂ = 4 × 6 = 24 m

Answer: 24 m

⚠️ Common Mistakes

Using the kinematic equations when acceleration is NOT constant. These equations (v=u+at, etc.) only apply to uniform acceleration. If acceleration depends on time (e.g. a = 2t), you must use calculus (integration/differentiation) directly.

Chapter 3: Motion in a Plane

Vector algebra, resolution of vectors, projectile motion, and uniform circular motion.

Learning Objectives

  • Add and subtract vectors using geometric and analytical methods.
  • Resolve a vector into its rectangular components.
  • Analyze projectile motion (find time of flight, maximum height, and horizontal range).
  • Understand uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration.

Important Definitions

  • Scalar: A quantity with magnitude only (e.g., mass, time, distance).
  • Vector: A quantity with magnitude and direction, obeying the triangle law of addition (e.g., velocity, force).
  • Projectile: An object thrown into space upon which the only acting force is gravity.

Projectile Motion Derivations

Consider a projectile launched with initial velocity u at an angle θ to the horizontal.

  • Horizontal component: u_x = u cosθ, a_x = 0
  • Vertical component: u_y = u sinθ, a_y = -g

1. Time of Flight (T):
Time to go up and come back down. Vertical displacement y = 0.
y = u_y t + ½ a_y t² ⇒ 0 = (u sinθ)T - ½ gT²
T = 2u sinθ / g

2. Maximum Height (H):
At max height, vertical velocity v_y = 0.
v_y² = u_y² + 2a_y H ⇒ 0 = (u sinθ)² - 2gH
H = u² sin²θ / 2g

3. Horizontal Range (R):
Horizontal distance covered in time T.
R = u_x × T = (u cosθ) × (2u sinθ / g) = u²(2 sinθ cosθ) / g
R = u² sin2θ / g

Important Formulae

  • Vector Addition (Magnitude): R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cosθ)
  • Vector Addition (Direction): tan α = (B sinθ) / (A + B cosθ)
  • Dot Product: A · B = AB cosθ = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z
  • Cross Product Magnitude: |A × B| = AB sinθ
  • Centripetal Acceleration: a_c = v² / r = ω²r

📌 Exam-Focused Points

  • For maximum horizontal range, the angle of projection must be θ = 45°. The max range is R_max = u²/g.
  • The range is the same for complementary angles of projection (θ and 90°-θ).
  • In uniform circular motion, the speed is constant but velocity changes continuously due to changing direction. Hence, there is always a centripetal acceleration directed towards the center.

Chapter 4: Laws of Motion

Understanding the fundamental laws governing the motion of objects.

Learning Objectives

  • State and apply Newton's Three Laws of Motion.
  • Understand the concepts of momentum, impulse, and conservation of momentum.
  • Analyze forces using Free Body Diagrams (FBDs).
  • Explain the types and laws of friction.

Key Concepts

  • Inertia: The inherent property of a body to resist a change in its state of rest or of uniform motion.
  • Momentum (p): Product of mass and velocity. p = mv. It is a vector quantity.
  • Impulse (J): A large force acting for a short time. J = F × Δt = Change in momentum (Δp).

Newton's Laws & Derivations

First Law (Law of Inertia): An object remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a net external force.

Second Law: The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied force. F ∝ dp/dt.

Derivation: F = k(dp/dt) = k(d(mv)/dt) = km(dv/dt) = kma. (k=1 in SI units, so F = ma).

Third Law: To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. F_AB = -F_BA.

📌 Friction

  • Static Friction (f_s): Self-adjusting force when a body is at rest. Maximum value is limiting friction: (f_s)_max = μ_s N.
  • Kinetic Friction (f_k): Opposing force when a body is in motion. f_k = μ_k N. Always μ_k < μ_s.
  • Rolling Friction: Friction when a body rolls. It is much smaller than sliding kinetic friction.

Chapter 5: Work, Energy and Power

The relationship between work done, energy transferred, and the rate of doing work.

Learning Objectives

  • Calculate work done by constant and variable forces.
  • Apply the Work-Energy Theorem.
  • Understand conservative vs non-conservative forces.
  • Analyze elastic and inelastic collisions.

Key Concepts

  • Work (W): W = F · d = Fd cosθ. (Zero work if θ=90°).
  • Kinetic Energy (K): K = ½mv². Energy possessed by virtue of motion.
  • Potential Energy (U): Energy by virtue of position or configuration (e.g., U = mgh for gravity, U = ½kx² for a spring).
  • Power (P): Rate of doing work. P = dW/dt = F · v. Unit: Watt (W).

Work-Energy Theorem

Statement: The work done by the net force on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy.

Derivation:
W = ∫ F dx = ∫ (ma) dx = ∫ m (v dv/dx) dx = m ∫ v dv
W = ½mv² - ½mu² = K_f - K_i = ΔK.

Important Formulae & Collisions

  • Conservative Force: Work done is independent of the path taken (e.g., Gravity, Electrostatic).
  • Elastic Collision: Both Momentum and Kinetic Energy are conserved. (Coefficient of restitution e = 1).
  • Inelastic Collision: Only Momentum is conserved. Kinetic energy is lost (e < 1). Perfectly inelastic: bodies stick together (e = 0).

Chapter 6: System of Particles and Rotational Motion

Dynamics of rigid bodies, center of mass, and rotational equivalents of linear motion.

Learning Objectives

  • Locate the center of mass for various systems.
  • Calculate torque and angular momentum.
  • Apply theorems of parallel and perpendicular axes for moment of inertia.
  • Analyze rolling motion.

Key Concepts

  • Center of Mass (CM): A point where the entire mass of the system is supposed to be concentrated. R_cm = (Σ m_i r_i) / M.
  • Torque (τ): The turning effect of force. τ = r × F.
  • Angular Momentum (L): L = r × p. Also L = Iω.

Moment of Inertia (I)

I = Σ m_i r_i². It is the rotational analog of mass.

Theorem of Parallel Axes: I = I_cm + Md²

Theorem of Perpendicular Axes (for planar bodies): I_z = I_x + I_y

Conservation of Angular Momentum: If external torque τ_ext = 0, then dL/dt = 0, so L = Iω = constant.

Chapter 7: Gravitation

The universal force of attraction between masses and planetary motion.

Learning Objectives

  • Apply Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation.
  • Calculate variation of g with height and depth.
  • Understand gravitational potential energy and escape velocity.
  • State and apply Kepler's Laws.

Key Concepts

  • Newton's Law: F = G(m₁m₂)/r². (G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²).
  • Acceleration due to gravity (g): g = GM/R².
  • Variation of g:
    • With height h: g_h = g(1 - 2h/R) for h << R.
    • With depth d: g_d = g(1 - d/R).

Escape & Orbital Velocity

Escape Velocity (v_e): Minimum velocity required to escape Earth's gravitational pull.
½mv_e² - GMm/R = 0 ⇒ v_e = √(2GM/R) = √(2gR) ≈ 11.2 km/s.

Orbital Velocity (v_o): Velocity required to keep a satellite in orbit near Earth's surface.
mv_o²/R = GMm/R² ⇒ v_o = √(GM/R) = √(gR) ≈ 7.9 km/s.

Chapter 8: Mechanical Properties of Solids

The elastic behavior of solid materials under applied forces.

Key Concepts

  • Stress: Restoring force per unit area (σ = F/A). Types: Longitudinal, Shearing, Hydraulic.
  • Strain: Fractional change in dimension (e.g., Longitudinal strain = ΔL/L).
  • Hooke's Law: Within the elastic limit, Stress ∝ Strain.

Elastic Moduli

  • Young's Modulus (Y): Longitudinal Stress / Longitudinal Strain = (F/A) / (ΔL/L).
  • Shear Modulus (G): Shearing Stress / Shearing Strain.
  • Bulk Modulus (B): Hydraulic Stress / Volumetric Strain = -P / (ΔV/V).
  • Elastic Potential Energy: U = ½ × Stress × Strain × Volume.

Chapter 9: Mechanical Properties of Fluids

Statics and dynamics of liquids and gases.

Key Concepts

  • Pascal's Law: Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every point.
  • Archimedes' Principle: Buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid displaced.
  • Viscosity: Internal friction in a fluid. Stokes' Law: F = 6πηrv.
  • Surface Tension: Force per unit length acting on the surface of a liquid.

Fluid Dynamics

Equation of Continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ (Conservation of mass).

Bernoulli's Theorem: For a streamlined flow of an ideal fluid, the sum of pressure energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy per unit volume is constant.
P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant

Chapter 10: Thermal Properties of Matter

Heat, temperature, and modes of heat transfer.

Key Concepts

  • Thermal Expansion: Linear (ΔL = αLΔT), Area (ΔA = βAΔT), Volume (ΔV = γVΔT). Relation: α = β/2 = γ/3.
  • Specific Heat (s): Heat required to raise temp of 1 kg by 1°C. Q = msΔT.
  • Latent Heat (L): Heat required for phase change at constant temperature. Q = mL.
  • Heat Transfer: Conduction (through solids), Convection (through fluids), Radiation (no medium required).

Chapter 11: Thermodynamics

The laws governing heat, work, and internal energy.

Key Concepts

  • Zeroth Law: If A and B are in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium. (Defines Temperature).
  • First Law: ΔQ = ΔU + ΔW (Conservation of energy). Heat supplied equals change in internal energy plus work done.
  • Thermodynamic Processes: Isothermal (T=const, ΔU=0), Adiabatic (ΔQ=0), Isochoric (V=const, ΔW=0), Isobaric (P=const).
  • Second Law: Heat cannot flow from a colder body to a hotter body without external work. (Defines entropy).
  • Heat Engine Efficiency: η = W/Q₁ = 1 - (Q₂/Q₁) = 1 - (T₂/T₁) for Carnot engine.

Chapter 12: Kinetic Theory

Microscopic behavior of ideal gases.

Key Concepts

  • Ideal Gas Equation: PV = nRT = NkT.
  • Pressure of Gas: P = ⅓ ρ (v_rms)².
  • Kinetic Energy: Average KE per molecule = (3/2)kT. It depends ONLY on absolute temperature.
  • Equipartition of Energy: Total energy is distributed equally among all active degrees of freedom, each having ½kT per molecule.
  • Mean Free Path: Average distance covered by a molecule between two successive collisions. λ = 1 / (√2 π d² n).

Chapter 13: Oscillations

Periodic motion and Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM).

Key Concepts

  • SHM: Motion where restoring force is proportional to displacement (F = -kx).
  • Equations:
    Displacement: x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ)
    Velocity: v(t) = ω√(A² - x²)
    Acceleration: a(t) = -ω²x
  • Time Period (T): T = 2π/ω.
    For Spring: T = 2π√(m/k).
    For Pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g).
  • Energy in SHM: Total Energy = ½kA² = constant. KE and PE oscillate, but sum is conserved.

Chapter 14: Waves

Propagation of disturbances through mediums.

Key Concepts

  • Transverse Waves: Particles vibrate perpendicular to propagation (e.g., light, string waves).
  • Longitudinal Waves: Particles vibrate parallel to propagation (e.g., sound).
  • Wave Equation: y(x,t) = A sin(kx - ωt + φ), where k = 2π/λ and ω = 2π/T.
  • Speed of Wave: v = νλ = ω/k. Speed of sound in gas: v = √(γRT/M).
  • Principle of Superposition: Resultant displacement is the vector sum of individual displacements. Leads to Interference, Standing Waves, and Beats.
  • Beats: Frequency of beats f_b = |f₁ - f₂|.
