Nationalism in India High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? This chapter explains how Indian nationalism grew during the freedom struggle through movements like Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience. It covers Mahatma Gandhi's role, the Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh, Khilafat Movement, and Salt March. Board exams frequently test key events, dates, personalities, and the participation of different social groups.

1.1 Impact of the First World War (1914-1918)

The First World War created a new political and economic situation in India:

  • Increased defence expenditure — financed by war loans and increasing taxes
  • Customs duties were raised and income tax introduced
  • Prices increased sharply, leading to extreme hardship for the common people
  • Villagers were forcibly recruited into the army, causing widespread anger
  • Crop failure led to acute food shortages, widespread influenza epidemic, and loss of lives

1.2 Rise of Nationalism

The war years saw an increase in nationalistic feelings. People hoped that their hardships would end after the war, but that did not happen. This led to a new wave of nationalist movements.

🎯 Key Point: The First World War and its economic hardships made Indians realize the need for self-rule and strengthened the nationalist movement.

2. Satyagraha

2.1 What is Satyagraha?

Satyagraha (Sanskrit: Satya = truth, Agraha = insistence) is a philosophy and practice of non-violent resistance developed by Mahatma Gandhi.

2.2 Gandhi's Early Satyagrahas

YearPlaceIssue
1916Champaran (Bihar)Indigo planters exploiting farmers
1917Kheda (Gujarat)Farmers demanding revenue relief due to crop failure
1918AhmedabadCotton mill workers demanding higher wages

Through these movements, Gandhi successfully demonstrated that non-violent resistance could be used to fight injustice. He gained widespread popularity among peasants, workers, and the middle class.

🎯 Key Point: Gandhi returned to India from South Africa in January 1915 and introduced the technique of Satyagraha — a novel way of fighting colonial rule through non-violent means.

3. Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh Massacre

3.1 Rowlatt Act (1919)

  • Passed by the Imperial Legislative Council in 1919
  • Allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years
  • Gave enormous power to the government to repress political activities

3.2 Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919)

  • Gandhi called for a nationwide hartal (strike) on 6 April 1919
  • On 13 April 1919, a crowd gathered at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar to protest
  • General Dyer blocked the exits and opened fire on the peaceful crowd
  • Hundreds of innocent people were killed and wounded

The massacre caused widespread outrage across India. Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest. Gandhi called for a nationwide movement against British rule.

⚠️ Key Point: The Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh Massacre were turning points in India's freedom struggle. They shattered the myth that the British were fair rulers.

4. Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-1922)

4.1 Background

Gandhi believed that British rule in India was established and maintained with the cooperation of Indians. If Indians refused to cooperate, British rule would collapse.

4.2 Methods of Non-Cooperation

StageActions
Stage 1Surrender of titles; boycott of government schools, colleges, law courts, and councils
Stage 2Boycott of foreign goods; picketing of liquor shops; promotion of khadi and Indian goods
Stage 3If needed, refusal to pay taxes (as in the Salt March)

4.3 Spread of the Movement

  • Students left schools and colleges
  • Lawyers gave up practice
  • People boycotted foreign cloth and goods
  • Tribal peasants in Andhra Pradesh rose in rebellion
  • Workers went on strike in many places

4.4 Withdrawal (1922)

Gandhi withdrew the movement in February 1922 after the Chauri Chaura incident, where a violent mob attacked a police station and killed 22 policemen. Gandhi believed that the movement was turning violent and called it off.

🎯 Key Point: Non-Cooperation Movement was the first nationwide mass movement led by Mahatma Gandhi. It united different sections of society against British rule.

5. Khilafat Movement (1919-1922)

5.1 Background

  • After the First World War, the Ottoman Empire (Turkey) was defeated and dismembered
  • The Ottoman Sultan was the Khalifa (spiritual head) of the Islamic world
  • Indian Muslims feared that the Khalifa would lose his authority and holy places would be taken over

5.2 Gandhi's Role

Gandhi joined hands with the Khilafat Movement because he saw it as an opportunity to bring Hindus and Muslims together in a united struggle against British rule.

🎯 Key Point: The Khilafat issue was one of the reasons why Gandhi decided to launch the Non-Cooperation Movement. It was the first time that Hindus and Muslims came together on a large scale against British rule.

6. Civil Disobedience Movement (1930-1934)

6.1 Background

The Simon Commission was appointed in 1927 to review India's constitutional system. All its members were British, which led to protests across India with the slogan "Go back Simon".

6.2 Lahore Congress (1929)

At the Lahore session of the Indian National Congress in 1929, Purna Swaraj (complete independence) was declared as the goal of the Congress. 26 January 1930 was observed as Independence Day.

6.3 Launch of Civil Disobedience

Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience Movement by starting the Salt March on 12 March 1930. The movement involved:

  • Breaking the salt law
  • Boycott of foreign cloth and goods
  • Picketing of liquor shops
  • Refusal to pay taxes
  • Resignation from government jobs
Difference from Non-Cooperation: The Civil Disobedience Movement was more aggressive. People not only refused to cooperate but also broke laws (like the salt law) and deliberately violated colonial rules.

7. Salt March (Dandi March) — 12 March 1930

7.1 Why Salt?

Salt was consumed by both rich and poor. The British government had a monopoly on salt production and collection of taxes on it. Gandhi saw salt as a symbol of British oppression that affected every Indian household.

7.2 The March

  • Gandhi started the march from Sabarmati Ashram on 12 March 1930
  • He walked 240 miles (about 385 km) to Dandi on the Gujarat coast
  • On 6 April 1930, he picked up a handful of salt and broke the salt law
  • Thousands joined him on the way

7.3 Impact

  • Triggered the Civil Disobedience Movement across India
  • Thousands were arrested, including Gandhi
  • Became a symbol of Indian resistance to British rule
🎯 Key Point: The Salt March was a brilliant example of Gandhi's ability to connect a simple everyday issue (salt) to the larger struggle for freedom.

8. Participation of Different Social Groups

GroupRole & Response
PeasantsParticipated in large numbers; especially in UP, Bihar, and Gujarat; demanded reduction of revenue
WorkersParticipated in strikes and protests; demanded better wages and working conditions
Tribal PeopleRose in rebellion in Andhra Pradesh and other regions
WomenParticipated in large numbers in picketing, protests, and meetings
MuslimsInitially joined Khilafat; later many became disillusioned and did not participate in Civil Disobedience
IndustrialistsSupported the movement financially; wanted protection against foreign imports

8.1 Women's Participation

Women participated in large numbers in the Civil Disobedience Movement. They:

  • Participated in protest marches
  • Manufactured salt
  • Picketed foreign cloth and liquor shops
  • Went to jail

However, the Congress was reluctant to give them positions of authority in the organization.

8.2 Limitations and Responses

  • Dalits (untouchables) did not initially participate in large numbers because they felt the movement was dominated by upper castes
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar demanded separate electorates for Dalits
  • Gandhi opposed separate electorates and went on a fast; eventually, the Poona Pact (1932) was signed
  • Muslims were concerned about their minority status in a Hindu-majority India

9. The Sense of Collective Belonging

Nationalism spread when people began to believe that they were part of the same community. Several factors contributed to this:

  • Unified Symbols: The tricolour flag, the image of Bharat Mata, and the slogan "Vande Mataram" created a sense of unity
  • Folklore and History: Nationalists used folk tales, songs, and stories to revive a sense of pride in India's past
  • Icons and Heroes: Leaders like Mahatma Gandhi, Subhas Chandra Bose, and Bhagat Singh became symbols of resistance
  • Common Struggle: Participation in movements like Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience created a shared experience
  • National Education: Nationalist leaders promoted Indian education and culture
🎯 Key Point: By the 1930s, the Indian National Congress had become a truly mass organization. People from all sections of society identified with the national movement.

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Terms:
  • Satyagraha: Non-violent resistance (truth force)
  • Rowlatt Act: Allowed detention without trial (1919)
  • Jallianwala Bagh: Massacre on 13 April 1919
  • Non-Cooperation: Refusing to cooperate with British (1920-22)
  • Khilafat: Movement to protect Ottoman Khalifa
  • Civil Disobedience: Breaking unjust laws (1930-34)
  • Salt March: Dandi March (12 March 1930)
  • Purna Swaraj: Complete independence (1929)
🎯 Key Dates:

1917Kheda Satyagraha

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Confusing Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22) with Civil Disobedience Movement (1930-34)
  • Forgetting that Chauri Chaura incident (1922) led to the withdrawal of Non-Cooperation
  • Mixing up Rowlatt Act (1919) with Simon Commission (1927)
  • Not mentioning Purna Swaraj (1929) when discussing Civil Disobedience
  • Confusing Khilafat Movement (Ottoman Khalifa) with other movements
  • Forgetting that women participated in large numbers but were not given positions of authority
