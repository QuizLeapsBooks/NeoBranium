Probability High Weightage Chapter

Why this chapter is important? Probability is a numerical measure of the degree of certainty of events. It teaches you to quantify uncertainty and make predictions based on data. Board exams consistently test theoretical probability, complementary events, and problems involving coins, dice, and cards.

1.1 What is Probability?

Probability is the numerical measurement of the degree of certainty of the occurrence of events [citation:5]. It tells us how likely an event is to happen.

1.2 Historical Background

Probability theory had its origin in the 16th century when Italian physician and mathematician J. Cardan wrote the first book on the subject, The Book on Games of Chance. The theoretical definition of probability was given by Pierre Simon Laplace in 1795 [citation:9].

1.3 Basic Idea

Probability is expressed as a number between 0 and 1 [citation:5]:

  • 0 means the event is impossible (will never happen) [citation:5]
  • 1 means the event is certain (will definitely happen) [citation:5]
  • Values between 0 and 1 indicate varying degrees of likelihood
🎯 Key Point: In this chapter, we deal with experiments whose outcomes are equally likely — such as tossing a fair coin or rolling a fair die [citation:9].

2. Key Terms in Probability

2.1 Experiment

An experiment refers to an action or trial that leads to outcomes [citation:2]. For example, rolling a die or tossing a coin.

2.2 Outcome

An outcome is a possible result of an experiment. For a die, the outcomes are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 [citation:2].

2.3 Random Experiment

A random experiment is an experiment where all possible outcomes are known but the exact outcome cannot be predicted in advance [citation:6].

2.4 Sample Space

The sample space (denoted by S) is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment [citation:2].

Example: Tossing a coin twice gives S = {HH, HT, TH, TT} [citation:2].

2.5 Event

An event is a subset of the sample space [citation:2]. For example, getting an even number when rolling a die is the event {2, 4, 6}.

2.6 Types of Events

TypeDefinitionExample
Simple/Elementary EventAn event with a single outcome [citation:2]Rolling a 3 on a die
Compound EventAn event with more than one outcome [citation:2]Rolling an even number
Equally Likely OutcomesOutcomes that have the same chance of occurring [citation:6]Head or tail on a fair coin

3. Theoretical Probability Formula

The theoretical probability (also called classical probability) of an event E is defined as [citation:9]:

P(E) = Number of outcomes favourable to E
─────────────────────────────────
Total number of possible outcomes

This formula is valid when all outcomes are equally likely [citation:9].

Notation:
P(E) = Probability of event E
n(E) = Number of favourable outcomes
n(S) = Total number of possible outcomes

3.1 Example: Tossing a Coin

Example 1: Find the probability of getting a head when a coin is tossed once [citation:9].

Solution:

Sample space S = {H, T}, so n(S) = 2

Favourable outcome for head: {H}, so n(E) = 1

P(Head) = 1/2

3.2 Example: Marbles in a Box

Example 2: A box contains 3 blue, 2 white, and 4 red marbles. If a marble is drawn at random, find the probability that it is (i) white (ii) blue (iii) red [citation:1].

Solution:

Total marbles = 3 + 2 + 4 = 9

(i) P(White) = 2/9

(ii) P(Blue) = 3/9 = 1/3

(iii) P(Red) = 4/9

Note: P(W) + P(B) + P(R) = 2/9 + 3/9 + 4/9 = 1 ✓ [citation:1]

4. Range and Properties of Probability

4.1 Range of Probability

For any event E [citation:5]:

0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1

4.2 Impossible Event

An event that cannot happen has probability 0 [citation:8].

Example: Getting 8 on a normal die [citation:8].

4.3 Sure Event

An event that is certain to happen has probability 1 [citation:8].

Example: Getting a number less than 7 on a normal die [citation:8].

4.4 Sum of Probabilities of All Elementary Events

The sum of the probabilities of all elementary events of an experiment is 1 [citation:9].

P(E₁) + P(E₂) + ... + P(Eₙ) = 1

5. Complementary Events

5.1 Definition

For any event E, the event of "not E" (E not happening) is called the complementary event of E, denoted by Ē or E' [citation:5].

5.2 Formula

P(E) + P(Ē) = 1

P(Ē) = 1 − P(E)

5.3 Solved Example

Example 3: Harpreet tosses two different coins simultaneously. What is the probability that she gets at least one head? [citation:1]

Solution:

Sample space S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}, n(S) = 4

Event E = "at least one head" = {HH, HT, TH}, n(E) = 3

P(E) = 3/4

Alternative method (using complement):

Ē = "no head" = {TT}, n(Ē) = 1

P(Ē) = 1/4

P(E) = 1 − P(Ē) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4 ✓ [citation:1]

7. Important Solved Examples (NCERT)

7.1 Example: Die — Prime Number

Example 4: A die is thrown once. Find the probability of getting a prime number [citation:16].

Solution:

Total outcomes = 6

Prime numbers on a die: 2, 3, 5 → n(E) = 3

P(Prime) = 3/6 = 1/2

7.2 Example: Cards — Face Card

Example 5: One card is drawn from a well-shuffled deck of 52 cards. Find the probability of getting a face card [citation:16].

Solution:

Total cards = 52

Face cards (J, Q, K in each suit) = 4 × 3 = 12

P(Face card) = 12/52 = 3/13

7.3 Example: Two Dice — Sum 7

Example 6: Two dice are thrown together. Find the probability that the sum is 7 [citation:4].

Solution:

Total outcomes = 36

Favourable outcomes for sum 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) → n(E) = 6

P(Sum 7) = 6/36 = 1/6

7.4 Example: Good Pens

Example 7: A lot consists of 144 ball pens, of which 20 are defective and the rest are good. Nuri will buy a pen if it is good. The shopkeeper draws one pen at random. What is the probability that she will buy it? [citation:4]

Solution:

Total pens = 144

Good pens = 144 − 20 = 124

P(She will buy) = 124/144 = 31/36

P(She will not buy) = 20/144 = 5/36 [citation:4]

7.5 Example: Hanif's Game

Example 8: A game consists of tossing a one rupee coin 3 times. Hanif wins if all tosses give the same result (HHH or TTT), and loses otherwise. Calculate the probability that Hanif will lose the game [citation:12].

Solution:

Sample space = {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT}, n(S) = 8

Winning outcomes = {HHH, TTT}, n(Win) = 2

P(Win) = 2/8 = 1/4

P(Lose) = 1 − P(Win) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4

Quick Revision - At a Glance

🎯 Key Formulas:
  • Probability of Event: P(E) = Favourable Outcomes / Total Outcomes
  • Complementary Events: P(Ē) = 1 − P(E)
  • Range: 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
  • Sum of All Probabilities: P(E₁) + P(E₂) + ... + P(Eₙ) = 1
🎯 Sample Space Sizes:
  • 1 coin → 2 outcomes; n coins → 2ⁿ outcomes
  • 1 die → 6 outcomes; n dice → 6ⁿ outcomes
  • 2 dice → 36 outcomes (sum 7 has highest probability = 1/6)
  • 52 cards → 26 red, 26 black, 12 face cards, 4 aces
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Thinking two dice have only 11 outcomes (sums 2 to 12) — actually 36 outcomes [citation:4]
  • Forgetting to simplify fractions in the final answer
  • Confusing "at least one" with "exactly one" — use complementary events for "at least" problems
  • Not accounting for removal of items in problems where objects are not replaced [citation:16]
